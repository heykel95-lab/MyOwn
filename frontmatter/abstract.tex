\chapter*{Abstract}
\addcontentsline{toc}{chapter}{Abstract}
\ReviewMark{green}{The abstract states the problem, implemented mechanism,
principal measured ordering, null-space result, and scope limits without
overloading the reader with the full parameter matrix.}

Angular misalignment between a grinding tool and a surface can cause
edge-first contact. A rigidly controlled robot resists the resulting alignment
motion and can therefore generate high contact forces and moments. Neither the
size nor the direction of that misalignment is known before contact, so the
compliance intended to absorb it cannot be arranged from the known geometry
alone. This thesis investigates contact-induced alignment of a
torque-controlled collaborative robot against a planar surface, and evaluates
whether a fixed centre of compliance can be selected independently of the
direction and sign of the initial misalignment.

A real-time Cartesian impedance controller was implemented for a
seven-degree-of-freedom (\abbr{DOF}) Franka Emika Panda. The controller
regulates translational and rotational compliance relative to the surface and
allows the virtual centre of compliance to be displaced from the tool centre
point (TCP). This displacement introduces coupling between translation and
rotation: when the robot presses the tool toward the surface, the resulting
force can generate a moment that assists the alignment motion.

Experiments compared directional stiffness and different positions of the
virtual compliance centre. The compliance-centre position produced the largest
change in the measured alignment response. The assisting displacement lay in
the surface plane and perpendicular to the required rotation direction.
Reversing the initial angular misalignment also reversed the assisting
displacement direction, and a displacement selected for one surface tangent did
not give the same response about the orthogonal tangent. The response was
strongly direction-dependent: about one in-plane axis the robot already rotated
substantially toward the surface without a displaced compliance centre, whereas
about the orthogonal axis a selected \(40\,\mathrm{mm}\) displacement changed
the measured set-up rotation from \(-1.63^\circ\) to \(+4.43^\circ\). Tests at
intermediate in-plane directions supported the same geometric direction rule,
but the experiments did not establish one displacement magnitude that suits
every direction.

A non-zero tangential displacement is therefore direction-dependent by
construction, and each tested displacement suited only part of the tested
misalignment range. Within the investigated task, the compliance centre at the
TCP is the direction-independent fixed centre among the tested positions,
selectable before the sign and tangent-plane direction of the initial
misalignment are known. That independence is the whole of the term: in a
particular condition a displaced centre can produce the larger alignment
rotation, and it remains a condition-dependent alignment mechanism for use
where greater rotational authority is required. Returning to the TCP afterwards
removes its directional coupling for the sustained contact that follows.

A complementary null-space study showed that projected damping reduced
redundant joint motion without returning the redundant configuration, while
singular-value conditioning, active throughout the trial, kept the net
disturbance-induced redundant displacement close to zero under the tested
disturbance direction. The work demonstrates a
real-time method for using a virtual compliance-centre displacement to convert
normal contact loading into an alignment moment. Its main experimental
contribution is the separation of the fixed centre from the displaced one,
together with a simple geometric rule for choosing the direction and sign of
the displacement from the required tool-alignment rotation. Grinding itself was
not evaluated experimentally.
