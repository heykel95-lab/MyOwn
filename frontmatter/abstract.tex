\chapter*{Abstract}
\addcontentsline{toc}{chapter}{Abstract}

Small geometric errors between a rigidly controlled tool and a surface produce
contact loads and prevent the tool from aligning with it.
This thesis develops a real-time Cartesian impedance controller for a
seven-degree-of-freedom (\abbr{DOF}) Franka Emika Panda and investigates how
the commanded tilt, the stiffness of each surface tangent, and the position of
a virtual centre of compliance influence contact-induced alignment.

The controller maps the Cartesian wrench to joint torques through the
transposed Jacobian, with phase-dependent gains. A compliance-centre stiffness
and damping matrix is shifted to the TCP through an adjoint congruence, so
that a translational contact response also generates a rotational moment. The
experiments probe that coupling.

Placement of the virtual compliance centre dominated the response. Its effect
is sign-dependent, and the favourable sign is opposite for the two surface
tangents; the unfavourable sign removed the correction rather than reducing
it. Displacement along the surface normal stayed below the interpretation
threshold. Stiffness mattered less: a higher rotational stiffness on the
tilted axis reduced alignment monotonically, and no tested setting improved on
the most compliant baseline. Alignment was consistently larger about \(t_2\)
than about \(t_1\); the same tilt commanded about the axes of the grinding
face produced values near the linear combination of the two tangent responses,
which places the asymmetry in the surface frame rather than the face geometry.

In-plane compliance-centre placement, above all its sign relative to the
tilted axis, is therefore the dominant parameter here. Its generality is
limited by the single robot, tool, surface and fixture; by unmeasured tool
motion in the gripper, which can conceal but not create alignment and so makes
the \(t_2\) results lower bounds; and by the model-estimated wrench used in
place of an independent sensor.
