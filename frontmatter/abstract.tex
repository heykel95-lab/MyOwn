\chapter*{Abstract}
\addcontentsline{toc}{chapter}{Abstract}

Small geometric errors between a rigidly controlled tool and a workpiece can
produce undesirable contact loads and prevent the tool from aligning with the
surface. This thesis develops a real-time Cartesian impedance controller for a
seven-degree-of-freedom (\abbr{DOF}) Franka Emika Panda and investigates how
the commanded angular
tilt, the rotational and translational stiffness of each surface tangent,
and the spatial position of a virtual centre of compliance influence
contact-induced alignment.

The controller defines base and surface frames explicitly and maps the Cartesian
wrench to joint torques through \(J^\top\). Its Cartesian gains are
phase-dependent, and its damping is derived from a Cartesian-inertia estimate.
During set-up, a compliance-centre \(6\times6\) stiffness and damping matrix
is shifted to the TCP through an adjoint congruence. In the resulting
translation--rotation coupling, a translational contact response generates a
rotational moment about the selected virtual centre. An SVD-based
null-space controller is implemented for the redundant seventh joint, although
its effect is not evaluated in a separate experimental campaign.

The experimental study comprises \(75\) contact runs on a workpiece plane and
a grinding-face axis that are calibrated independently of one another, so the
commanded tilt is defined relative to a measured physical surface
frame. Three repetitions were performed for each of \(25\) settings, giving a
median within-setting standard deviation of \(0.02\)--\(0.03^\circ\) in the
alignment improvement.

Placement of the virtual compliance centre dominated the response. Moving the
lever \(60\,\mathrm{mm}\) along the tangent perpendicular to the tilted axis
raised the alignment improvement from \(0.56^\circ\) to \(6.05^\circ\) for a
tilt about \(t_1\) and from \(1.38^\circ\) to \(6.36^\circ\) for a tilt
about \(t_2\), reducing the residual misalignment to \(0.93^\circ\) and
\(2.14^\circ\). The effect is sign-dependent and the favourable sign is
opposite for the two tangents; the unfavourable sign removed the correction
rather than reducing it. Displacing the compliance centre \(20\,\mathrm{mm}\)
along the surface normal produced no change beyond the interpretation
threshold.

The stiffness entries had a substantially weaker influence. Raising the
rotational stiffness of the tilted axis from \(5\) to \(50\,\mathrm{Nm/rad}\)
reduced alignment monotonically, and no tested setting improved on the
\(5\,\mathrm{Nm/rad}\) baseline. Reducing the translational stiffness
perpendicular to the tilted axis improved alignment for a \(t_2\) tilt
only, and combining it with a high rotational stiffness removed that benefit.
Alignment was consistently larger for a tilt about \(t_2\) than about
\(t_1\); commanding the same tilt about the axes of the grinding face
instead produced values close to the linear combination of the two tangent
responses, which associates the asymmetry with the surface frame rather than
with the geometry of the face.

The results establish in-plane compliance-centre placement, and in particular
its sign relative to the tilted axis, as the dominant parameter for
contact-induced alignment in the investigated configuration. Their generality
is limited by the use of one robot, tool, workpiece, and fixture; by
approximately \(2^\circ\) of unmeasured tool motion within the gripper, which
can conceal but not create alignment and therefore makes the \(t_2\) results
lower bounds; by three tangential levels on one axis at a time rather than a
two-dimensional sample; by the use of positive commanded tilt only; and by
the use of a model-estimated external wrench instead of an independent
force/torque sensor.
