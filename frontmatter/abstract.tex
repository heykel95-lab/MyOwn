\chapter*{Abstract}
\addcontentsline{toc}{chapter}{Abstract}
\ReviewMark{green}{The abstract states the problem, implemented mechanism,
principal measured ordering, null-space result, and scope limits without
overloading the reader with the full parameter matrix.}

Angular misalignment between a grinding tool and a surface can cause
edge-first contact. A rigidly controlled robot resists the resulting alignment
motion and can therefore generate high contact forces and moments. Neither the
size nor the direction of that misalignment is known before contact, so the
compliance intended to absorb it cannot be arranged from the known geometry
alone. This thesis investigates contact-induced alignment of a
torque-controlled collaborative robot against a planar surface, and evaluates
whether a fixed centre of compliance can be selected independently of the
direction and sign of the initial misalignment.

A real-time Cartesian impedance controller was implemented for a
seven-degree-of-freedom (\abbr{DOF}) Franka Emika Panda. The controller
regulates translational and rotational compliance relative to the surface and
allows the virtual centre of compliance to be displaced from the tool centre
point (TCP). This displacement introduces coupling between translation and
rotation: when the robot presses the tool toward the surface, the resulting
force can generate a moment that assists the alignment motion.

Experiments compared directional Cartesian stiffness and different positions of
the virtual centre of compliance. Among the tested parameters, the
compliance-centre position produced the largest change in the measured
alignment response. An assisting displacement lay in the surface plane and
perpendicular to the required rotation direction. Reversing the initial angular
misalignment also reversed the required displacement direction. A displacement
that assisted alignment about one surface tangent did not produce the same
effect about the orthogonal tangent.

The response was strongly direction dependent. About one in-plane axis,
substantial alignment-directed rotation occurred with the centre of compliance
at the TCP. About the orthogonal axis, a selected \(40\,\mathrm{mm}\)
displacement changed the measured set-up rotation from \(-1.63^\circ\) to
\(+4.43^\circ\). Experiments at intermediate in-plane directions were
consistent with the same geometric direction rule. However, the tested
conditions did not establish one displacement magnitude that was suitable for
all directions.

The tested non-zero tangential displacements were therefore condition
dependent. Each displacement was effective only for part of the investigated
misalignment range. Within the investigated task, the TCP-centred condition was
the direction-independent fixed centre among the tested positions because it
could be selected without prior knowledge of the sign or tangent-plane
direction of the initial misalignment. This does not mean that the TCP produced
the largest alignment-directed rotation in every condition. Where additional
rotational alignment was required, a suitably selected displaced centre
provided greater alignment authority. Returning the centre of compliance to the
TCP afterwards removed the additional directional coupling for the
sustained-contact phase.

A complementary null-space study evaluated projected damping and singular-value
conditioning. Projected damping reduced redundant joint motion without driving
the redundant configuration back toward its initial state. Singular-value
conditioning, which was active throughout the trial, kept the net
disturbance-induced redundant displacement close to zero for the tested
disturbance direction.

The developed controller provides a real-time method for converting part of the
normal pressing action into an alignment moment through a virtual
compliance-centre displacement. The main experimental finding is the
distinction between a direction-independent TCP-centred condition and a
condition-dependent displaced centre, together with a geometric rule for
selecting the direction and sign of the displacement from the required
alignment rotation. Grinding itself was not evaluated experimentally.
