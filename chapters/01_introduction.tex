\section{Motivation}

\Added{In surface-contact tasks, the commanded tool pose is defined relative
to a configured surface reference with normal \mbox{\(n_s\)}. Placement and
calibration tolerances can make the physical surface normal
\mbox{\(n_{\mathrm{phys}}\)} differ from that configured reference. The tool
orientation achieved before contact can also differ from the desired
orientation relative to the same reference.}

\Added{Together, the difference between the configured and physical surfaces
and the difference between the desired and achieved tool orientations
determine the angular mismatch between the tool face and the physical surface
at contact entry. That mismatch can bring the tool onto the surface along an
edge or at a corner. A rigid position controller opposes the corresponding
corrective rotation and can therefore generate high contact loads.}

\Added{Torque-controlled collaborative robots provide the interface required
for active Cartesian compliance
\mbox{\citep{Hogan1985,Albu2003}}.} Joint torque sensing and a torque-level
command interface allow the controller to regulate both Cartesian motion and
compliance during contact. In surface finishing, this compliance can limit the
loads caused by angular tool--surface mismatch. It can also permit
end-effector rotation towards parallel alignment with the configured surface
under the commanded normal press. The rotation generated during Contact
Establishment forms the measured contact response.

Impedance control defines this compliant behaviour as a dynamic relation between
motion and interaction wrench \mbox{\citep{Hogan1985}}. Cartesian impedance control
applies that relation to the end-effector pose. Translational and
rotational stiffness and damping can be assigned independently along selected
Cartesian directions. During edge-first contact, the wrench contains both force and
moment components. Finite rotational compliance allows the contact moment to
rotate the end effector, while the translational impedance generates the
commanded press. The selected stiffness and damping
matrices therefore determine both the contact response and the free-space
pose-tracking behaviour.

\section{Related Work}

\Added{Cartesian impedance control builds on standard serial-manipulator
kinematics
\mbox{\citep{Siciliano2009,Craig2005,Murray1994}}: homogeneous transformations for
representing the end-effector pose and the Jacobian for relating joint motion
to Cartesian motion.} Khatib established the operational-space formulation, in
which a Cartesian wrench \mbox{\(F\)} is mapped to joint torques \mbox{\(\tau\)} through
\mbox{\(J^\top\)}
\mbox{\citep{Khatib1987}}; this mapping is adopted in the controller. Hogan defined
the impedance relation between end-effector motion and interaction force
\mbox{\citep{Hogan1985}}. Albu-Sch\"affer \mbox{et al.\ }implemented Cartesian impedance
control using joint-torque sensing \mbox{\citep{Albu2003}}, which corresponds to the
torque-controlled interface used in this thesis. These formulations supply the
kinematic and control basis adopted here.

\Added{Mayr \mbox{et al.\ }implemented a modular
Cartesian impedance controller for the Franka Emika Panda that combines
real-time model access, null-space control, signal treatment, and command
limiting \mbox{\citep{Mayr2024CartesianImpedance}}. Their implementation
identifies the real-time components required on the robot platform used
here.}

Recent work has applied compliant and force-controlled strategies directly to
robotic surface finishing. Alt \mbox{et al.\ }combined perception, planning, and
force-controlled execution for robotic surface treatment
\mbox{\citep{Alt2024RoboGrind}}. Min \mbox{et al.\ }integrated demonstrated motion and
force skills with impedance control on a 7-\mbox{\abbr{DOF}} collaborative robot
\mbox{\citep{Min2024HumanSkillsForceControl}}. Wu \mbox{et al.\ }regulated the
contact force during robotic grinding with adaptive variable impedance control
\mbox{\citep{Wu2023DualPIDImpedance}}. In each of these studies the regulated
quantity is the commanded contact force rather than the rotational response at
contact entry.

\Added{Fasse and Broenink formulated spatial impedance about a selected point,
so that translation
and rotation become coupled through the position of this point
\mbox{\citep{Fasse1997SpatialImpedance}}.} This formulation provides the theoretical
basis for the compliance-centre shift used in this thesis. The same principle
appears mechanically in remote-centre-compliance devices. Their elastic
elements are arranged so that the tool behaves as if it rotates about a virtual
point located away from the mechanism itself
\mbox{\citep{Nevins1989ComputerControlledAssembly,Watson1978RCC}}. More recent
mechanically compliant and variable-compliance-centre strategies apply this
principle to peg-in-hole insertion
\mbox{\citep{Wang2019VariableComplianceCenter,Hartisch2022FlexureRCC}}. In those
mechanisms, elastic deformation and mechanism geometry permit the contact
forces to generate corrective motion that reduces misalignment. Most of these
studies address insertion tasks and fix the compliance centre through either
the mechanical design or a strategy adapted to the hole geometry.

Suomalainen \mbox{et al.\ }experimentally investigated how
compliance-centre placement and compliant-axis selection influence alignment
in an assembly task \mbox{\citep{Suomalainen2019DualArmCompliance}}. Friedrich
\mbox{et al.\ }subsequently proposed an active remote centre of compliance
(\abbr{CoC}) that combines passive compliance with active force control
\mbox{\citep{Friedrich2025ActiveRCC}}. Both show that the location of the
compliance centre can strongly influence the direction and the magnitude of
contact-induced motion.

\Added{Together, the cited work provides the basis for compliant surface
treatment, spatial impedance about a selected point, and compliance-centre
placement for assembly.
In this thesis, the effects of directional Cartesian stiffness and a software-defined virtual
compliance centre were isolated during planar tool--surface contact.}

\section{Problem Statement}

Before contact, the tool--surface angular relation depends on differences
between the configured and physical surfaces and between the desired and
achieved tool orientations. The intended application can encounter both
directions of rotation about a surface-tangent axis and different rotation
axes within the tangent plane. Under rigid position control, the contact
geometry can generate high moments while the controller resists the corrective
motion. Finite rotational compliance, however, allows contact-induced
rotation, while a displaced \abbr{CoC} additionally couples translation and
rotation.

The displacement \mbox{\(r_c\)} points from the tool centre point (TCP) to the
virtual \abbr{CoC}. It contributes the term \mbox{\(r_c\times f\)} to the commanded
TCP moment. For the commanded normal press, the tangential direction of
\mbox{\(r_c\)} sets the direction of that additional moment. A displaced
centre therefore favours one rotational direction and opposes the other.

Different \abbr{CoC} positions were evaluated for initial angular offsets in
both directions of rotation about one axis in the surface plane. The comparison determined how
each centre position changed the measured end-effector response in the two
rotational directions.

The selected controller parameters were varied while the calibrated geometry,
robot configuration, and tool mounting were held constant. A configured
orientation offset sets the desired tool direction relative to the configured
surface reference. The orientation achieved at contact entry characterises the
actual initial condition of each experiment and accounts for the finite
orientation tolerance. \mbox{\Cref{ch:theory,ch:implementation}} introduce the
frame conventions and controller formulation used in the experiments.

\section{Contributions}
\label{sec:objectives}

The main contributions are:

\begin{itemize}[leftmargin=1.1cm]
\item A real-time Cartesian impedance controller with surface-relative
directional stiffness and damping and a shiftable virtual \abbr{CoC} was implemented on a
Franka Emika Panda.
\item The effects of rotational stiffness, translational stiffness along the
surface, and tangential compliance-centre position on contact-induced
end-effector rotation were characterised experimentally.
\item Projected null-space damping and singular-value conditioning were
implemented and evaluated separately in Cartesian pose hold.
\end{itemize}

With the \abbr{CoC} at the TCP, the measured end-effector rotation was towards
the configured surface for both tested rotational directions. A displaced
\abbr{CoC} favoured one rotational direction and opposed the other through the
additional commanded moment \mbox{\(r_c\times f\)}. Selecting the displacement
direction according to the initial angular-offset direction slightly increased
the final response, whereas the opposite displacement direction strongly
reduced it.
