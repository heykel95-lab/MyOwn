\chapter{Introduction}
\label{ch:intro}

\section{Motivation}
\label{sec:motivation}
\subsection{Collaborative Robots in Modern Automation}
Robotic manipulators are widely used for accurate and repeatable
motion in structured industrial environments. In classical automation,
the robot is often separated from humans and the task is defined as a
geometric trajectory that should be followed as precisely as possible.
This approach is effective for welding, handling, machining, or
pick-and-place operations when the environment is known and contact with
unexpected objects is avoided. The requirements change when the robot
is used in collaborative or interaction-based tasks. In these cases,
the robot may come into contact with a human operator, a tool, a
workpiece, or a surface whose exact position is not perfectly known.
Then, rigid position tracking alone is not sufficient because small
geometric errors can lead to large contact forces.

Collaborative robots are designed to make such interaction tasks more
practical. They typically include joint torque sensing, collision
detection, and control interfaces that allow compliant behavior.
According to the International Federation of Robotics, collaborative
robots are becoming an increasingly relevant part of industrial robot
deployments because they can support flexible human--robot
collaboration and shorter setup times \citep{IFR2023WorldRobotics}.
For this reason, the control problem is no longer only to move the
robot from one pose to another, but also to define how the robot should
react when its end-effector interacts with the environment.

\subsection{Compliant Robot Motion}
Compliant robot motion addresses this problem by allowing a controlled
deviation from the desired pose when interaction forces occur. Instead
of behaving like an ideally stiff positioning device, the robot can be
made to behave like a mechanical spring-damper system. The stiffness
determines how strongly the robot reacts to a displacement, while the
damping determines how strongly velocity is opposed. This idea is the
basis of impedance control, which was introduced by Hogan as a way to
specify the dynamic relation between motion and interaction forces
\citep{Hogan1985}. In Cartesian impedance control, this relation is
defined directly at the end-effector in task space.

Cartesian impedance control is especially useful for contact tasks.
For example, a robot that moves a tool along a surface should be stiff
in the direction in which a precise distance or contact condition must
be maintained, but more compliant in directions where small
misalignments should not generate large forces. The same idea applies
to orientation: if a tool edge contacts a surface while the tool is
slightly tilted, the external contact wrench contains both a force and
a moment. A suitably selected Cartesian stiffness matrix can allow a
small translation and rotation instead of resisting the contact
rigidly. The resulting translation and rotation observed during contact provide a
direct geometric description of whether the physical tool axis moves toward
the surface target and whether the selected tool feature remains sufficiently
bounded. Therefore, the
central control design question is not only how to compute a torque
command, but also how to choose meaningful stiffness and damping
matrices for the contact geometry.

\subsection{Importance of Impedance Control for Contact Tasks}
The mathematical foundations of this thesis build on standard robotics
methods for serial manipulators. Homogeneous transformations describe
the position and orientation of coordinate frames, forward kinematics
relates joint positions to end-effector pose, and the Jacobian maps
joint velocities to Cartesian velocities \citep{Siciliano2009,Craig2005,Murray1994}.
For force and torque control, the Jacobian is also used in the reverse
direction: a Cartesian wrench, consisting of force and moment, can be
mapped to joint torques using the Jacobian transpose. This relation is
closely connected to operational-space control and the work of Khatib
\citep{Khatib1987}.

Several previous works have extended impedance control toward more
complex interaction behavior. Ott et al. discuss Cartesian impedance
control for flexible joint robots and the importance of stable
interaction behavior \citep{Ott2008}. Albu-Sch\"affer et al. describe
Cartesian impedance control for torque-controlled lightweight robots,
which is directly relevant for robots with joint torque sensing
\citep{Albu2003}. Practical Cartesian impedance implementations for
robots such as the Franka Emika Panda also show the importance of
filtering, torque limiting, and null-space behavior in real robot
experiments \citep{Mayr2024CartesianImpedance}. Recent work on
energy-aware Cartesian impedance control on a Franka Emika Panda further
shows that contact tasks must be evaluated not only by tracking accuracy, but
also by the energy, power, damping, and safety behavior during unexpected
interaction \citep{Hjorth2023EnergyAwareImpedance}. Villani et al. provide
a broader view of human--robot collaboration and physical interaction
in manufacturing \citep{Villani2018HRC}. For the specific question of how
the center of compliance or stiffness can be chosen in space, the
spatial impedance formulation of Fasse and Broenink is important
because it shows how translation and rotation can be coupled through a
selected point \citep{Fasse1997SpatialImpedance}. This is closely
related to the older remote-center-compliance idea in assembly and to
variable compliance-center strategies for peg-in-hole tasks, where the
compliance geometry is chosen so that contact forces tend to reduce rather
than amplify misalignment
\citep{Nevins1989ComputerControlledAssembly,Watson1978RCC,Wang2019VariableComplianceCenter,Hartisch2022FlexureRCC}.
In this thesis, the same principle is investigated as a compliance- and
pole-gain-selection problem within a Cartesian impedance controller for a
surface-contact task.

\section{Problem Statement}
\label{sec:problem_statement}
\subsection{Challenges in Cartesian Impedance Control}
Although the basic impedance law resembles a spring-damper equation,
its practical implementation on a torque-controlled robot is not
trivial. First, the controller must use consistent coordinate frames.
The desired pose, measured pose, Cartesian error, stiffness matrix, and
damping matrix must all be expressed in compatible frames before the
resulting wrench is mapped to joint torques. Second, the stiffness and
damping matrices must be selected so that the robot is stiff where the
task requires accuracy and compliant where interaction or alignment is
needed. Third, the commanded joint torque must be monitored against the
configured robot collision/reflex thresholds and the timing
budget of the real-time loop. The implemented controller does not add a
separate software torque or torque-rate saturation stage, so boundedness
must not be inferred from the impedance equation alone.

The contact-alignment task adds another difficulty. A tool that is tilted
with respect to a surface cannot be aligned by translational compliance
alone. The orientation behavior must be defined so that the tool can
rotate toward the surface while the contact force remains limited. A selected
local linearized center of compliance provides a useful way to design this
behavior by coupling translation and rotation about a chosen point near the
tool edge or surface contact. It is a task-to-impedance mapping concept, not a
claim that the measured tool motion has an exact physical pivot. The resulting
response is therefore evaluated from the measured motion during contact.

\section{Objectives of the Thesis}
\label{sec:objectives}

The objective of this thesis is to design, implement, and evaluate a real-time
Cartesian impedance controller for the Franka Emika Panda. The controller is
not evaluated only as a position-tracking controller. Its main purpose is to
support compliant contact with a known surface and to study whether a selected
Cartesian stiffness and damping behavior can bias useful alignment during
contact without prescribing a task-space tipping trajectory.

\subsection{Design of a Cartesian Impedance Controller}
The first objective is to formulate the Cartesian impedance law used in the
thesis. The controller computes a Cartesian wrench from position error,
orientation error, linear velocity, and angular velocity, and maps this wrench
to joint torques with the transpose of the geometric Jacobian. The stiffness
and damping matrices are treated as the main design parameters, because they
define how strongly the robot reacts to pose errors and motion during contact.

\subsection{Real-Time Implementation on a 7-DOF Collaborative Robot}
The second objective is to implement the controller on the Franka Emika Panda
using the Franka Control Interface and libfranka. The implementation must run in
the real-time torque-control callback, use the robot state and model quantities,
include Coriolis compensation, handle the redundant seventh joint through a
null-space term, and verify the commanded torques and callback timing under the
configured collision/reflex behavior.

\subsection{Surface-Constrained Motion}
The third objective is to define the controller behavior with respect to a known
surface frame. The surface normal and tangent directions are used to express
stiffness and damping in task-relevant directions. This makes it possible to
separate normal compliance, tangential motion, and rotational behavior relative
to the surface instead of tuning all gains only in the robot base frame.

\subsection{Unscripted Task-Space Alignment During Contact}
The fourth objective is to study the alignment response during contact after a controlled approach to
the surface. In free space the controller first aligns the configured physical
tool axis with the signed surface normal. During set-up, the selected tool edge
is then pressed toward the plane while the impedance---rather than a scripted
rotation trajectory---determines the residual tipping motion caused by contact.
A selected center of compliance couples translation and rotation through a
spatial adjoint. The active null-space mode also applies the sigma bias, which
can influence the observed motion. The response is therefore classified as
unscripted task-space alignment rather than isolated passive motion and is
evaluated through the physical tool-axis angle and the selected-feature/TCP
displacement.

\section{Scope and Contributions}
\subsection{Scope of the Implemented Controller}
This thesis focuses on a phase-scheduled Cartesian impedance controller
implemented in C++ with libfranka and Eigen. The controller follows a
Jacobian-transpose torque mapping and uses the model quantities
provided by libfranka. It does not attempt to implement a full
operational-space inverse-dynamics controller, adaptive impedance
control, or online surface estimation. The surface is treated as a
given task geometry, for example through a surface normal and a point
on the surface. This scope is appropriate for investigating the core
question of how the controller behavior depends on the selected
six-dimensional impedance parameters. Stiffness remains configured per phase;
damping can be computed once for each phase group from the model-based
Cartesian inertia and a configured damping factor.

The main contributions of the thesis follow directly from this scope. On the
practical side, the work configures and uses the Franka Emika Panda through the
\abbr{FCI} and libfranka in a C++ development environment, and on this basis
formulates a Cartesian impedance controller that computes a wrench from the pose
error, maps it to joint torques with the Jacobian transpose, and adds
model-based Coriolis compensation. Around this core law, task-dependent
stiffness and damping matrices are constructed in explicitly stated base and
surface frames. A geometric phase supervisor aligns the tool, approaches the
plane to a configured clearance, selects and locks a rectangular-face contact
feature, ramps the set-up press, and then executes the grinding motion. During
set-up, a chosen center of compliance is implemented directly by transforming
a pole-frame \(6\times6\) stiffness and damping through the spatial adjoint;
the resulting off-diagonal blocks couple translation and rotation.

The resulting contact motion is analysed using physical alignment and
displacement metrics. Finally, the controller integrates projected joint damping and a finite-difference
smallest-singular-value bias for the redundant 7-\abbr{DOF} arm. Its
truncated-SVD implementation explicitly separates the structural null vector
from the right-singular direction \(v_6\) associated with the reported
\(\sigma_{\min}=\sigma_6\), whether or not that value passes the inverse
cutoff.

The work therefore connects the theoretical concepts of impedance
control, center-of-compliance design, and redundancy resolution with a
real-time implementation on the Panda robot. The emphasis is on
understanding how the impedance parameters influence the observed
contact response and what stiffness selection supports useful unscripted
contact alignment under a chosen pole-coupled impedance.

\section{Thesis Structure}
Chapter~\ref{ch:theory} develops the theoretical background for coordinate
frames, kinematics, Cartesian impedance control, null-space behavior,
and stiffness and damping representation.
Chapter~\ref{ch:implementation} describes the real-time controller
implementation on the Panda.
Chapter~\ref{ch:methodology} presents the experimental methodology, including
the robot setup, contact geometry, gain-selection workflow, logged data, and
evaluation metrics.
Chapter~\ref{ch:results_discussion} separates qualitative design expectations
from the measured historical coupled-pole campaign and records the controller
revision to which those measurements belong.
Chapter~\ref{ch:conclusion} summarises the conclusions, limitations, and
future work.
