\chapter{Introduction}
\label{ch:intro}

\section{Motivation}
\label{sec:motivation}
\subsection{Collaborative Robots in Modern Automation}
Industrial manipulators are built to follow a commanded trajectory precisely,
and inside a structured cell that is sufficient: the workpiece sits where the
program says it does. A surface-finishing task breaks that assumption. The
workpiece plane is fixtured by hand, the tool is held in a gripper, and the
residual angle between the two is not known before contact. Under rigid
position control that angle has nowhere to go, so it appears as contact force.

Collaborative robots make another option available, and they account for a
growing share of industrial deployments
\citep{IFR2023WorldRobotics,Villani2018HRC}. Joint torque sensing and a
torque-level command interface let
the controller specify how the robot yields under contact, not only where it
goes.

\subsection{Compliant Robot Motion}
Impedance control specifies that yielding as a dynamic relation between motion
and interaction force \citep{Hogan1985}. Cartesian impedance control imposes
the relation at the end-effector, in task space, so compliance can be shaped
per direction rather than per joint.

Direction is what a contact task needs. A tool moved across a surface must
hold its distance to that surface, yet a small lateral misalignment should not
build a large force. Orientation behaves the same way: when a tilted tool edge
meets the surface, the contact wrench carries a moment as well as a force, and
a stiffness matrix chosen for the contact geometry admits a small rotation
instead of resisting it. The rotation that actually occurs during contact is
the quantity this thesis measures, which makes the stiffness and damping
matrices a choice about contact geometry and not only about free-space
tracking.

\subsection{Foundations and Related Work}
The controller rests on standard serial-manipulator kinematics
\citep{Siciliano2009,Craig2005,Murray1994}: homogeneous transformations for
pose, and the Jacobian for the velocity map. It uses the Jacobian in the
reverse direction as well, mapping a Cartesian wrench to joint torques through
\(J^\top\), which is the operational-space construction of Khatib
\citep{Khatib1987}.

Ott et al. extended Cartesian impedance control to flexible-joint robots,
where joint elasticity limits the stiffness the controller can actually
realise \citep{Ott2008}. Albu-Sch\"affer et al. built the same law on
joint-torque sensing \citep{Albu2003}, which is the interface this thesis
uses. Implementations on the Franka Emika Panda report filtering, torque limiting,
and null-space handling as practical requirements
\citep{Mayr2024CartesianImpedance}. Fasse and Broenink formulate spatial
impedance about a selected point, so that translation and rotation couple
through its placement \citep{Fasse1997SpatialImpedance}. The same geometry
appears in remote-centre-compliance devices for assembly and in
variable-compliance-centre strategies for peg-in-hole insertion, where the
compliance is arranged so that contact forces reduce rather than amplify
misalignment
\citep{Nevins1989ComputerControlledAssembly,Watson1978RCC,Wang2019VariableComplianceCenter,Hartisch2022FlexureRCC}.

That literature is concentrated on insertion, and fixes the compliance centre
through mechanical design or through a strategy tuned for a hole. This thesis
places it in software, inside the impedance law of a torque-controlled robot,
and measures what its placement does to tool alignment against a surface.

\section{Problem Statement}
\label{sec:problem_statement}
\subsection{Challenges in Cartesian Impedance Control}
Although the basic impedance law resembles a spring-damper equation,
its practical implementation on a torque-controlled robot is not
trivial. First, the controller must use consistent coordinate frames.
The desired pose, measured pose, Cartesian error, stiffness matrix, and
damping matrix must all be expressed in compatible frames before the
resulting wrench is mapped to joint torques. Second, the stiffness and
damping matrices must be selected so that the robot is stiff where the
task requires accuracy and compliant where interaction or alignment is
needed. Third, the commanded joint torque must be monitored against the
configured robot collision/reflex thresholds and the timing
budget of the real-time loop. The implemented controller does not add a
separate software torque or torque-rate saturation stage, so boundedness
must not be inferred from the impedance equation alone.

A tool tilted with respect to the surface cannot be brought flat by
translational compliance alone: the wrench that closes the gap must also carry
a moment. Placing the centre of compliance near the tool edge couples
translation and rotation, so that pressing the tool into the plane also
rotates it toward the plane. The placement is a task-to-impedance mapping, not
a claim that the tool pivots about that point physically, and the response it
produces is therefore read from the motion measured during contact.

\section{Scope and Contributions}
\label{sec:objectives}

This work investigates how the commanded tilt, the rotational and
translational stiffness of each surface tangent, and the spatial position of a
virtual centre of compliance influence contact-induced alignment. The
controller is a phase-scheduled Cartesian impedance law, written in C++ with
libfranka and Eigen and running in the \(1\,\mathrm{kHz}\) torque loop of the
Franka Emika Panda. Torques are mapped with \(J^\top\), and Coriolis terms are
compensated from the model. Each phase carries its own Cartesian gains;
damping follows from a Cartesian-inertia estimate rather than being set by
hand. A surface frame carries the normal and the two tangents \(t_1\) and
\(t_2\) explicitly.

One run proceeds through fixed phases: the tool axis is aligned, the plane is
approached to a geometric clearance, the leading feature of the rectangular
tool face is selected, that feature is ramped into the surface, and tangential
grinding motion follows. During set-up, a compliance-centre \(6\times6\)
stiffness and damping matrix is shifted to the TCP through an adjoint
congruence. Its off-diagonal blocks are the translation--rotation coupling
this thesis measures, over \(75\) contact runs spanning \(25\) settings.

The principal contributions are:

\begin{itemize}
  \item a frame-consistent real-time Cartesian impedance controller with a
        spatially shifted centre of compliance;
  \item a repeatable methodology for evaluating contact-induced tool alignment;
  \item axis-specific measurement of the rotational and translational
        stiffness contributions to contact-induced alignment; and
  \item experimental characterisation of the compliance-centre lever as the
        dominant parameter for alignment, including the reversal of its
        favourable sign between the two surface tangents.
\end{itemize}

The implementation also contains projected joint damping and a two-point
smallest-singular-value bias for the redundant seventh joint. These terms
complete the controller architecture, but their effect is not treated as an
experimentally established contribution. Full operational-space inverse
dynamics, adaptive impedance, online surface estimation, and claims concerning
human safety are outside the scope.
