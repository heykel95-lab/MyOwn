\section{Conclusion}
\label{sec:conclusion_summary}

\Revised{A real-time Cartesian impedance controller was implemented for the
contact-induced alignment of a grinding tool with a plane surface. During the
set-up phase, the Cartesian stiffness and damping were spatially shifted from
a selected centre of compliance to the TCP. This shift introduced
translation--rotation coupling, so that the normal interaction load could
generate a moment about the surface tangents.}\Added{ The controller was
evaluated in \mbox{\(174\)} surface-contact runs across \mbox{\(58\)} parameter
settings in eight experimental cases. The cases covered commanded tilt,
directional stiffness, and the position and magnitude of the
compliance-centre lever.}\Revised{ A separate Cartesian
pose-hold study was used to examine the projected null-space damping and
singular-value-conditioning terms.}

\Revised{The zero-tilt condition showed that the set-up procedure itself introduced an
orientation change. The alignment angle increased from \mbox{\(1.07^\circ\)} to
\mbox{\(2.04^\circ\)}, corresponding to an alignment change of
\mbox{\(-0.97^\circ\)}. The tilted conditions were therefore interpreted together
with this baseline response. The zero-tilt value was retained as a separate
measurement and was not subtracted from the remaining results.}

\Revised{Among the varied parameters, the position of the virtual centre of compliance
had the largest measured influence on alignment. With the lever directed so
that the induced moment assisted the required rotation, the alignment
improvement reached \mbox{\(6.05^\circ\)} for a tilt about \mbox{\(t_1\)} and
\mbox{\(6.36^\circ\)} for a tilt about \mbox{\(t_2\)}. The corresponding zero-lever
conditions produced \mbox{\(0.56^\circ\)} and \mbox{\(1.38^\circ\)}, respectively. The
assisting lever direction changed with the tilted surface tangent:
\mbox{\(r_{c,t_2}=-60\,\mathrm{mm}\)} was required for the \mbox{\(t_1\)} condition,
whereas \mbox{\(r_{c,t_1}=+60\,\mathrm{mm}\)} was required for the \mbox{\(t_2\)}
condition. Applying the lever in the opposite direction produced no
comparable improvement. The compliance-centre lever therefore acts as a
directional bias whose sign must agree with the required corrective rotation.}

\Added{Reversing the commanded tilt reversed the assisting lever direction. At
the reversed tilt the lever selected for the positive tilt produced no
improvement beyond the zero-lever condition on either surface tangent. The
reversed lever restored most of the response about \mbox{\(t_2\)} and about two
fifths of it about \mbox{\(t_1\)}. The sign of the assisting lever therefore
follows the sign of the commanded tilt. The sign rule was reproduced on both
surface tangents, but the magnitude response was not symmetric between them.}

\Added{The lever magnitude giving the largest improvement depended on the
commanded tilt. For three settings, the improvement decreased after the signed
residual crossed the surface orientation. In the remaining three settings, the
response was still increasing at the largest tested magnitude. The location of
the maximum therefore remained unresolved within the investigated range. No
single lever magnitude gave the largest improvement at all tested settings.}

\Added{At the reversed \mbox{\(t_1\)} tilt the improvement remained smaller than
at the corresponding positive tilt. A longer lever raised it but did not bring
the tool to the surface orientation. This condition was also the only one in
which the orthogonal component increased during set-up. The tool-mount
compliance bears on that comparison.}

\Revised{The tested stiffness variations produced smaller changes. Increasing the
rotational stiffness about the tilted tangent reduced the alignment
improvement about \mbox{\(t_1\)}, and the highest tested rotational stiffness also
produced less improvement than the softer settings about \mbox{\(t_2\)}. Reducing the
translational stiffness perpendicular to the tilted tangent increased the
response about \mbox{\(t_2\)}, reaching \mbox{\(1.79^\circ\)} at
\mbox{\(300\,\mathrm{N/m}\)}. The corresponding variation about \mbox{\(t_1\)} remained
of the same order as the experimental scatter. Combining the lowest
translational stiffness with the highest rotational stiffness produced less
improvement than the low-translational-stiffness condition alone. This
non-additive response indicates that the two stiffness entries cannot be
selected independently over the tested range.}

\Added{Commanding the tilt in four directions in the tangent plane showed that a
lever selected from the measured direction at a common
\mbox{\(60\,\mathrm{mm}\)} magnitude assisted at every direction tested. In
the three matched comparisons, holding one lever fixed reduced the improvement
by \mbox{\(1.02^\circ\)} to \mbox{\(3.40^\circ\)}, or
\mbox{\(32\)}--\mbox{\(48\,\%\)}, although the paired initial angles differed
by no more than \mbox{\(0.04^\circ\)}. A single fixed tangential lever is
therefore not sufficient to retain the same response across the tested tilt
directions. This case evaluates the direction rule and does not optimize the
magnitude for each direction.
Moving the compliance centre along the surface normal changed the improvement
by about one degree over a range of
\mbox{\(180\,\mathrm{mm}\)}, which is small against the effect of the tangential
lever but is not zero beyond the displacement tested in Case~D.}

\Revised{Placing the centre of compliance at the centre of the grinding face produced
nearly the same response as the zero-lever condition. At the commanded
\mbox{\(10^\circ\)} tilt, the \mbox{\(20\,\mathrm{mm}\)} axial displacement generated only
a small tangential lever component. The measurements are therefore consistent
with the point-shifted impedance formulation: the tangential component of the
lever determines the moment about the tilted surface tangent, while the
component parallel to an ideal normal interaction load contributes no direct
force moment. The small measured normal-coordinate effect can therefore involve
non-normal force components, finite coupled motion, contact geometry, or
tool-mount compliance; the experiment does not distinguish these mechanisms.}

\Revised{Tilts commanded about the grinding-face axes produced alignment improvements
between those obtained for the two surface-tangent directions. Their agreement
with a linear combination of the tangent-axis measurements provides a
consistency check for the surface-frame interpretation. The comparison does
not establish linear superposition as a general response model, but it
indicates that the measured directional asymmetry was associated mainly with
the surface-frame response rather than solely with the rectangular
grinding-face geometry.}

\Revised{The Cartesian pose-hold study showed that projected null-space damping reduced
the cumulative redundant joint excursion by approximately \mbox{\(25\,\%\)} relative
to the matched condition without a null-space torque. The two tested
singular-value-conditioning commands produced similar final conditioning
changes, while the larger command caused greater joint excursion and a larger
Cartesian position error. These measurements characterise the separated
null-space terms for the investigated free-space hold condition.}

\Revised{Overall, the experiments show that the spatial placement and sign of the
virtual centre of compliance governed the alignment response more strongly
than the stiffness variations considered in this work. The point-shifted
Cartesian impedance provided a direct means of converting the commanded
normal interaction into an alignment moment without prescribing an explicit
rotational trajectory during set-up.}\Added{ The three research questions can
therefore be answered as one sequence. First, stiffness changes the resistance
to contact-induced alignment, but its measured influence was smaller than that
of compliance-centre placement. Second, the lever direction and sign determine
whether the press assists or opposes alignment, while the preferred magnitude
is condition-dependent rather than universal. Third, the measured tilt defines
the geometric selection rule: the tangential lever is chosen perpendicular to
the signed tilt axis so that the induced moment opposes the angular error. The
four-direction Case-H result supports this rule beyond the principal surface
tangents and shows that one fixed tangential lever did not retain the same
alignment response across the tested tilt directions.}


\section{Limitations}
\label{sec:limitations}

\subsection{Experimental Range}
\label{subsec:conclusion_experimental_range}

\Added{The contact experiments were performed with one robot, one tool mounting,
one plane surface, one press trajectory, and a finite set of controller
parameters. Both signs of the commanded tilt were measured, and the assisting
lever direction reversed with the sign on both surface tangents. The measured
lever magnitudes follow from the investigated contact geometry. The rule
relating lever direction and sign to the measured tilt is the transferable
result; the values in millimetres are not.}

\Added{The systematic magnitude sweep used four lever magnitudes along one
surface tangent at a time. Case~H additionally tested four combined in-plane
tilt and lever directions at one common magnitude. These measurements identify
the assisting direction and show that the magnitude giving the largest
improvement depends on the commanded tilt. They do not provide a dense
two-dimensional sweep or identify the preferred magnitude at the diagonal
directions.}\Revised{ The stiffness
comparison likewise covered a limited number of values, and the combined
low-translational-stiffness and high-rotational-stiffness condition represents
one selected combination rather than a complete factorial study.}

\Added{The software versions, control period, calibrated geometry, and active
controller parameters are documented, but the real-time-computer hardware,
payload parameters, and complete robot transform configuration were not
retained with the experimental record. Exact dynamic and timing reproduction
is therefore limited by these missing system data. Archived controller or robot
records should be used to recover and document them if they remain available.}


\subsection{Measurement and Tool-Mount Uncertainty}
\label{subsec:conclusion_measurement_uncertainty}

\Added{The grinding-face direction used in the evaluation was calculated from the
measured end-effector orientation and the calibrated grinding-face direction
in the end-effector frame. The tool could rotate by approximately
\mbox{\(\pm2^\circ\)} relative to the gripper about \mbox{\(y_{\mathrm{EE}}\)}. The reported
alignment angle therefore describes the combined robot--gripper--tool response
and cannot separate controller-induced rotation from motion within the
gripper. This uncertainty is particularly relevant to the \mbox{\(t_2\)} conditions,
for which the largest within-setting variability was observed. Because the
relative tool rotation was not measured, the physical grinding-face rotation
may differ from the reported value in either direction.}

\Revised{The alignment metric also inherits uncertainty from the calibrated surface
normal and the tool calibration. The reported external force and moment were
the model-based estimates supplied by libfranka. They were not obtained from
an independent force/torque sensor and were therefore used as supporting
quantities rather than as direct measurements of the contact load.}


\subsection{Null-Space and Timing Scope}
\label{subsec:conclusion_nullspace_scope}

\Revised{Only projected damping was active during the surface-contact tests. The
singular-value-conditioning term was not applied, so the contact results
describe one null-space contribution rather than both. The pose-hold study
isolated the two terms only in free space under an internally commanded
point-force equivalent. It does not establish their response to physical link
contact, to surface contact, or to the combined null-space mode.}

\Revised{The controller operated through the nominal \mbox{\(1\,\mathrm{kHz}\)} libfranka
interface. The experiments evaluated motion and alignment behaviour rather
than worst-case callback execution time. A formal real-time timing analysis
was therefore outside the scope of the reported measurements.}


\section{Future Work}
\label{sec:future}

\Added{Three extensions to the compliance-centre study are considered most
important. Finer lever-magnitude sampling around and beyond the best tested
regions would determine whether a local maximum lies within or outside the
investigated range. A finer angular sweep, for example in
\mbox{\(15^\circ\)} or \mbox{\(30^\circ\)} increments, should then be combined
with magnitude variation at each direction. Case~H already samples four
directions, including two diagonals between the surface-axis cases; the next
step is therefore a denser joint characterization of direction and magnitude,
not merely the addition of intermediate directions.}

\Added{An angular measurement of the grinding face taken independently of the
end-effector pose would separate the rotation of the tool from that of the
mount. This bears on the reversed \mbox{\(t_1\)} result and on the magnitude of
every reported alignment change. Further contact geometries, tool faces and
press trajectories would establish how far the selection rule carries beyond the
investigated arrangement.}

\Revised{The grinding-face orientation should be measured independently of the
end-effector orientation. A more rigid tool mount would reduce the relative
motion observed at the gripper, while an external angular measurement would
allow controller rotation and tool-mount rotation to be separated. An
independent force/torque sensor would also permit the model-estimated external
wrench to be compared with a direct contact-load measurement.}

\Revised{The null-space study should be repeated with a physical disturbance applied to
an intermediate robot link and should include the combined damping and
singular-value-conditioning mode, which the contact tests did not use.
Repeating this
comparison during surface interaction would show whether the ordering observed
in free-space hold is retained in contact. Recording callback execution times
and deadline overruns during these tests would additionally provide a direct
assessment of the controller's real-time performance.}

\Revised{Finally, the compliance-centre selection can be incorporated into the
controller as a geometry-based scheduling step. After the initial tilt is
estimated at the clearance transition, the lever direction could be selected
so that the induced moment opposes the measured angular error. Such a strategy
would replace a lever fixed in advance with a run-specific placement while
retaining the point-shifted Cartesian impedance used in this work.}
