\chapter{Conclusion and Future Work}
\label{ch:conclusion}
\section{Conclusion}
\label{sec:conclusion_summary}

A real-time Cartesian impedance controller was implemented for the
contact-induced alignment of a grinding tool with a plane surface. During the
set-up phase, the Cartesian stiffness and damping were spatially shifted from
a selected centre of compliance \mbox{\(p_c\)} to the TCP. This shift introduced
translation--rotation coupling. The normal interaction load could therefore
generate a moment about the surface tangents.

The point-shift adjoint and congruence transformation are established
theory. The implementation contribution is their phase-dependent integration
into the real-time contact controller.

The controller was evaluated in eight surface-contact cases covering
commanded tool orientation offsets, directional stiffness, and the position and magnitude of the
compliance-centre lever \mbox{\(r_c\)}. Complementary Cartesian pose-hold trials quantified
the projected null-space damping and singular-value-conditioning terms. The
compliance-centre cases were measured at both signs of the commanded orientation offset
and on both surface tangents, so the direction that assists is established
against its own reversal rather than against a single reference.

The stiffness variations produced smaller changes than moving the
virtual centre of compliance \mbox{\(p_c\)}. Raising the rotational stiffness about the commanded
rotation axis reduced the signed set-up rotation. Varying the perpendicular
translational stiffness produced a much smaller effect. The condition in which
both stiffness entries were varied did not show an additive response for the
selected pair.

Within the investigated range, the centre of compliance \mbox{\(p_c\)} had the
largest measured influence. For each principal surface tangent, one lever
direction increased the signed set-up rotation and the opposite direction
reduced or reversed it. Reversing the commanded tool orientation offset
exchanged the two directions. The lever must therefore be selected from both
the commanded rotation axis and the required direction of the induced moment.

With \mbox{\(r_c=p_{\mathrm{TCP}}-p_c\)} and
\mbox{\(m_c=-r_c\times f_{\mathrm{press}}\)}, the experimentally supported selection rule is
a lever perpendicular to the commanded rotation axis in the tangent plane, with
\mbox{\(\rho_c>0\)}. The assisting tangential lever is therefore perpendicular
to the commanded rotation axis and reverses with the commanded rotation
direction.

The direction rule was supported for four commanded rotation directions in
the tangent plane. The direction-selected lever produced a numerically equal
or larger signed set-up rotation across the tested directions. The diagonal
differences of \mbox{\(0.07^\circ\)} and \mbox{\(0.05^\circ\)} were within the
descriptive experimental scatter because the fixed lever retained a
substantial component along the selected direction. About \mbox{\(t_2\)}, the
selected lever produced a clear change from \mbox{\(-1.61^\circ\)} to
\mbox{\(+4.43^\circ\)}.

Displacing the centre of compliance \mbox{\(p_c\)} along the tool axis
changed the set-up rotation by only \mbox{\(0.34^\circ\)} over the tested
\mbox{\(-40\)} to \mbox{\(+40\,\mathrm{mm}\)} range. The response was not
strictly monotonic and does not identify a preferred axial position. This weak
sensitivity is consistent with the absence of a first-order moment from a
lever parallel to a normal press.

The TCP-height metric classifies the tool as flat in 50 of the 57
settings. About \mbox{\(t_1\)}, the robot already turns
\mbox{\(+7.57^\circ\)} without a lever, and the assisting
\mbox{\(40\,\mathrm{mm}\)} lever increases this to only
\mbox{\(+7.87^\circ\)}. About \mbox{\(t_2\)}, the same
\mbox{\(40\,\mathrm{mm}\)} magnitude, with its tangential direction selected
for the \mbox{\(t_2\)} command, changes the response from
\mbox{\(-1.63^\circ\)} to \mbox{\(+4.43^\circ\)}. It is thus
necessary for helpful robot rotation about \mbox{\(t_2\)}, but insufficient
to remove the full initial EE-inferred deviation.

Naming the lever in surface coordinates rather than in the tool frame
changed the response once the tool turned. At a commanded orientation offset of
\mbox{\(10^\circ\)}, the set-up rotations differed by
\mbox{\(7.74^\circ\)}. Contact-induced rotation also separated the two
definitions in the zero-command comparison. The definition frame is therefore
part of the controller setting.

In the matched Cartesian pose-hold trials, projected null-space damping
reduced the cumulative redundant joint excursion by approximately
\mbox{\(25\,\%\)}. Both singular-value-conditioning settings returned the
minimum singular value \mbox{\(\sigma_{\min}\)} to approximately its initial level after the commanded
disturbance. These findings apply to the isolated free-space hold conditions
tested.

The experiments therefore support the use of a point-shifted Cartesian
impedance to convert the normal interaction into an alignment moment without
an explicit rotational trajectory during set-up. The measured results provide
a geometric rule for selecting the lever direction. Its magnitude remains a
condition-dependent design parameter rather than a universal setting.


\section{Limitations}
\label{sec:limitations}

\subsection{Experimental Range}
\label{subsec:conclusion_experimental_range}

The contact experiments used one Panda configuration, tool mount, plane
surface, press trajectory, and bounded parameter range. The direction-and-sign
rule was reproduced on both principal surface tangents and supported at four
in-plane directions. The measured lever magnitudes and stiffness effects remain
specific to the investigated geometry and tested settings.

\subsection{Measurement and Tool-Mount Uncertainty}
\label{subsec:conclusion_measurement_uncertainty}

The alignment evaluation reconstructs the grinding-face direction from the
end-effector pose and a calibrated tool direction. The resulting alignment angle
\mbox{\(\theta_{\mathrm{align}}\)} therefore describes the
EE-inferred grinding-face alignment under the assumption that the calibrated
tool-to-end-effector relation remains fixed; it is not a separately measured
grinding-face orientation. Relative tool--gripper motion under load was not measured. The
signed set-up rotation therefore supports claims about controller response,
while TCP height provides only a geometry-based flatness classification.

The supporting external wrench was obtained from the change in the
libfranka model estimate relative to its clearance-transition reference. It was not measured by an independent contact-load
sensor. The reported force and moment comparisons are consequently limited to
this common model-based measurement chain. Controller-response figures use the
commanded normal force and commanded TCP moment on one consistent basis.


\subsection{Null-Space Configuration and Real-Time Operation}
\label{subsec:conclusion_nullspace_scope}

Projected damping and singular-value conditioning were active together
in null-space mode~3 during the surface-contact experiments. Their individual
effects were isolated only in free-space pose hold under an internally
commanded point-force equivalent. Their separate effects during contact and
their response to a physical disturbance were not evaluated.


\section{Future Work}
\label{sec:future}

Three extensions to the compliance-centre study are considered most
important. Finer lever-magnitude sampling would extend the measured response
around and beyond the best tested regions. A finer angular sweep, for example in
\mbox{\(15^\circ\)} or \mbox{\(30^\circ\)} increments, should then be combined
with magnitude variation at each direction. Case~C already samples four
directions, including two diagonals between the surface-axis cases; the next
step is therefore a denser joint characterization of direction and magnitude,
not merely the addition of intermediate directions.

An angular measurement of the grinding face taken independently of the
end-effector pose would separate tool rotation from motion within the mount. A
more rigid tool mount would further reduce this relative motion. This bears
particularly on the reversed \mbox{\(t_1\)} result and on the interpretation
of the EE-inferred alignment change \mbox{\(\Delta\theta_{\mathrm{align}}\)}.
An independent force/torque sensor would
additionally allow the model-estimated external wrench to be compared with a
direct contact-load measurement. Further contact geometries, tool faces, and
press trajectories would establish how far the selection rule extends beyond
the investigated arrangement.

The null-space pose-hold study can be extended with a physical disturbance applied to
an intermediate robot link and with damping and singular-value conditioning
active together. A corresponding surface-interaction
comparison would show whether the pose-hold ordering is preserved during
contact.

Finally, the compliance-centre selection can be incorporated into the
controller as a geometry-based scheduling step. After the initial orientation deviation is
estimated at the clearance transition, the lever direction could be selected
so that the induced moment opposes the measured angular error. Such a strategy
would replace a lever fixed in advance with a run-specific placement while
continuing to use the point-shifted Cartesian impedance implemented in this
work.
