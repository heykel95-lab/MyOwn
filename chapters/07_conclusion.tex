\chapter{Conclusion and Future Work}
\label{ch:conclusion}

\section{Conclusion}
\label{sec:conclusion_summary}

A real-time Cartesian impedance controller was implemented on a
seven-degree-of-freedom Franka Emika Panda. Its purpose was to generate
compliant contact-induced end-effector rotation towards the configured
surface-parallel orientation. The controller assigned directional stiffness and
damping in the surface frame. During Contact Establishment, a shift of the
virtual centre of compliance from the TCP coupled the commanded translational
press to the commanded TCP moment through \mbox{\(r_c\times f\)}.

With the \abbr{CoC} at the TCP, the measured end-effector rotation was directed
towards the configured surface-parallel orientation for both directions of the
achieved initial angular offset. The response magnitudes reached
\mbox{\(81.3\,\%\)} and \mbox{\(115.1\,\%\)} of the respective achieved initial
angular-offset magnitudes in Case~A. For the negative-offset condition the
measured response component therefore exceeded the achieved initial offset
component.

Rotational stiffness had a considerably stronger effect than cross-axis
translational stiffness. Raising \mbox{\(K_{R,t_1}\)} from \mbox{\(5\)} to
\mbox{\(50\,\mathrm{N\,m/rad}\)} reduced the response by \mbox{\(63.9\,\%\)}
relative to the \mbox{\(5\,\mathrm{N\,m/rad}\)} condition in Case~B. The
Case-C response span across the tested \mbox{\(K_{p,t_2}\)} values equalled
only \mbox{\(0.4\,\%\)} of the TCP-centred reference.

In Case~D, tangential compliance-centre position produced the largest measured
variation. Relative to the TCP-centred response magnitudes, the displacement
direction selected for each offset direction increased the response by
\mbox{\(4.0\,\%\)} and \mbox{\(4.3\,\%\)}. The displacement in the other
direction reduced the response by \mbox{\(97.6\,\%\)} and
\mbox{\(99.2\,\%\)}. Every tested non-zero position therefore favoured one
direction of the initial angular offset and opposed the other. Within the
tested range, a fixed displaced centre acted principally as a direction
selector rather than as a means of increasing the response.

The TCP-centred condition was neutral with respect to the additional
\abbr{CoC}-induced moment, because \mbox{\(r_c=0\)} gives
\mbox{\(r_c\times f=0\)}. It was not neutral in the sense of equal response
magnitudes, which differed between the two offset directions. The measured
end-effector rotation was nevertheless directed towards the configured
surface-parallel orientation in both tested directions of rotation. A displaced
centre produced a slightly larger measured response when its displacement
direction was selected for the known direction of the initial angular offset.
Neutrality and a direction-specific alignment response are separate properties.

Projected joint damping and singular-value conditioning were separated in the
null-space pose-hold study. Projected damping reduced cumulative redundant
motion relative to the condition without null-space torque. The complete
singular-value-conditioning modes were active before and during the
disturbance, and they produced net redundant displacements close to zero under
the tested virtual disturbance. The largest measured Cartesian position error
was \mbox{\(1.304\,\mathrm{mm}\)}, below the specified
\mbox{\(2\,\mathrm{mm}\)} limit. The lower conditioning magnitude produced
comparable suppression with less cumulative redundant motion.

\section{Limitations}
\label{sec:limitations}

\subsection{Scope of the Surface-Contact Results}
\label{subsec:conclusion_experimental_range}

The contact study used one Panda configuration, one rectangular tool and
mounting arrangement, one configured surface reference, and one press
trajectory. The reported parameter effects therefore apply to this experimental
geometry, and to the tested ranges of rotational stiffness, translational
stiffness, achieved initial angular offset, and compliance-centre position.

The principal repeatable comparison covered rotation about \mbox{\(t_1\)}.
Measurements about \mbox{\(t_2\)} showed greater variability and did not
support the same repeatable comparison. The study covered Contact Establishment
with a fixed centre position. Sustained grinding, and a centre position changed
during contact, remained outside the experimental scope.

\subsection{Calibration and Measurement Scope}
\label{subsec:conclusion_measurement_uncertainty}

The achieved pose-based initial angular offset \mbox{\(\theta_{0,t_1}\)} was
calculated from the measured end-effector pose and the calibrated tool normal,
relative to the configured surface reference. The response
\mbox{\(\gamma_{t_1}\)} was calculated from the measured end-effector
orientations at the start and end of Contact Establishment. Both quantities
therefore carry the uncertainty of the configured surface reference, the
tool-normal calibration, and the robot pose measurements.

The physical plane normal and the physical tool-face orientation during contact
were not measured independently. The two quantities consequently describe the
pose-based end-effector condition and response relative to the configured
reference. They do not constitute an independent measurement of the physical
tool--surface angle. The selected tool point \mbox{\(p_{\mathrm{Tool}}\)} was
the geometric reference for trajectory generation. The physical pressure centre
and the instantaneous contact location on the tool face were not observed.

\subsection{Tool-Mount Compliance}
\label{subsec:conclusion_tool_mount}

The tool mount exhibited approximately \mbox{\(\pm2^\circ\)} of unloaded
angular play about \mbox{\(y_{\mathrm{EE}}\)}. This axis is approximately
aligned with \mbox{\(t_2\)} in the flat configuration. The relative motion
between tool and end effector was not tracked during contact, and it may have
contributed to the greater variability of the \mbox{\(t_2\)} measurements.

The reported contact response is therefore the measured end-effector rotation.
The physical tool-face rotation may differ from it through motion within the
tool mounting arrangement.

\subsection{Scope of Null-Space and Real-Time Validation}
\label{subsec:conclusion_nullspace_scope}

The null-space terms were separated only in Cartesian pose hold, under one
virtual disturbance direction. The conditioning law applies an active,
non-dissipative torque towards the sampled neighbouring configuration with the
larger \mbox{\(\sigma_{\min}\)}. Its measured response is therefore specific to
the tested disturbance direction and robot configuration.

The conditioning term was already active during the \mbox{\(5\,\mathrm{s}\)}
settling interval preceding the disturbance. It shifted the entry
configurations of the conditioning trials by approximately
\mbox{\(0.013\,\mathrm{rad}\)} relative to the trials without conditioning. The
study therefore compared complete closed-loop modes rather than isolated torque
contributions applied from identical configurations.

The surface-contact experiments held projected damping and singular-value
conditioning active together. Their individual contributions were consequently
not separated during contact. The separate pose-hold experiment also used a
virtual disturbance rather than a physical external disturbance.

The net redundant displacement \mbox{\(\Delta\eta_{\mathrm{dist}}\)} projects
the accumulated joint displacement onto one fixed reference direction. The
joint-velocity projector \mbox{\(N_q\)} changes with the robot configuration,
so this projection gives a common directional comparison rather than an exact
nonlinear redundant coordinate.

\Added{The geometric Jacobian combines translational and rotational rows
without a common length scale, so \mbox{\(\sigma_{\min}\)} is interpreted only
as a relative indicator under the fixed Jacobian convention.}

Operation was demonstrated through the nominal \mbox{\(1\,\mathrm{kHz}\)}
interface. Worst-case callback execution time and scheduling jitter were not
measured separately. The assembled torque command carried no application-side
saturation or torque-rate limiter; robot-side collision and reflex monitoring
supplied the independent protection layer.

\section{Future Work}
\label{sec:future}

Future work should address physical angle measurement, direction-dependent
compliance-centre adaptation, and extended validation of the null-space
controller.

The physical plane normal and the physical tool-face orientation should first
be measured independently during contact. Such measurements would quantify the
error between the configured and physical surface references. They would also
resolve end-effector rotation and tool-mount rotation as separate quantities.

The virtual \abbr{CoC} should then be adapted to the estimated direction of the
initial angular offset. Such a controller would begin at the TCP, estimate the
required rotation direction, apply a bounded tangential displacement during
Contact Establishment, and return to the TCP before sustained contact. The
evaluation should be repeated about \mbox{\(t_2\)} and additional tangent-plane
directions, with improved tool constraint and an independent measurement of the
physical tool orientation.

Projected damping and singular-value conditioning should finally be evaluated
together under a physical disturbance. Varying both terms would quantify their
combined effect on redundant motion and Cartesian position retention. A
continuous or hysteretic conditioning law should also be compared with the
implemented deadband-based direction selection, to establish whether the
observed cumulative back-and-forth motion can be reduced.

The real-time implementation itself remains to be characterised. Callback
execution time and scheduling jitter should be measured during operation at
\mbox{\(1\,\mathrm{kHz}\)}. A final torque-saturation and torque-rate-limiting
stage should be implemented after the torque contributions are assembled, and
evaluated together with the robot-side collision and reflex monitoring.
