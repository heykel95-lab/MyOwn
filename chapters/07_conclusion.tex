\chapter{Conclusion and Future Work}
\label{ch:conclusion}

\section{Conclusion}
\label{sec:conclusion_summary}

This thesis developed and experimentally evaluated a real-time Cartesian
impedance controller for contact-induced alignment of a grinding tool with a
plane surface. The controller runs in the Franka Control Interface torque loop
and combines surface-relative Cartesian impedance control, a virtual centre of
compliance, and null-space control. During set-up, the stiffness and damping
defined at the selected centre of compliance \(p_c\) are shifted to the TCP.
The resulting translation--rotation coupling allows the commanded normal press
to generate an alignment moment without prescribing an additional rotational
trajectory.

The experimental investigation focused on contact-induced rotational alignment
and on the selection of a fixed centre of compliance when the sign and
tangent-plane direction of the initial angular misalignment are not known
before contact. A displaced centre introduces a directional
translation--rotation coupling, so its effect depends on the direction of the
misalignment that it is intended to correct.

With the centre of compliance at the TCP, contact still produced
end-effector rotation. Setting \(r_c=0\) removes only the coupling introduced
by the virtual point shift. The commanded rotational moment, the finite
rotational compliance, and the physical tool-geometry lever \(r_T\) remain
active. For a commanded \(+10^\circ\) orientation offset, the end effector
rotated by \(+7.57^\circ\) about \(t_1\), close to the \(+7.87^\circ\)
obtained with the assisting \(40\,\mathrm{mm}\) lever. The zero-lever
response is consistent with contributions from the physical contact geometry,
the ordinary rotational impedance, and the compliance of the tool mounting.
These contributions were not isolated experimentally, and
\(m_{r_T}=r_T\times\Delta\hat f_{\mathrm{ext}}\) therefore remains a
geometry-based interpretation rather than a measured decomposition.

About \(t_2\), the same commanded offset produced a signed end-effector
rotation of \(-1.63^\circ\). The response was therefore not
alignment-directed according to the primary rotation metric, although the
final configuration satisfied the TCP-height flatness criterion. The tool
mounting exhibited approximately \(\pm2^\circ\) of rotational play about the
mounting direction associated with \(t_2\). This relative motion is not
contained in the robot state and was not measured separately under contact
load. The physical tool may therefore have undergone additional rotation
relative to the end effector, but its contribution cannot be quantified from
the recorded data. The result supports a statement about the measured
end-effector motion rather than about the instantaneous orientation of the
physical tool face. The TCP-height criterion and the end-effector rotation
therefore describe different aspects of the final contact configuration.

The Cartesian gains changed the magnitude of the response but did not remove
its axis dependence. Increasing the rotational stiffness about the commanded
rotation axis reduced the set-up rotation, most clearly about \(t_1\). The
tested variation of the cross-axis translational stiffness produced a smaller
change. The combined low-translational-stiffness and
high-rotational-stiffness condition was consistent with the individual effects
acting approximately additively within the descriptive comparison scale. No
tested stiffness combination produced alignment-directed end-effector
rotation about \(t_2\).

Changing the virtual centre of compliance produced the largest response
variation over the tested parameter ranges. With
\[
r_c = p_{\mathrm{TCP}}-p_c,
\qquad
m_{c,K} = -\,r_c\times f_K,
\]
a tangential displacement introduces an additional commanded moment from the
normal pressing action. The assisting lever lies in the surface tangent plane
and perpendicular to the commanded rotation direction. Its sign is selected
so that the resulting moment acts in the required alignment direction.
Reversing the sign of the angular misalignment therefore reverses the assisting
lever direction.

About \(t_2\), the selected \(40\,\mathrm{mm}\) lever changed the signed
set-up rotation from \(-1.63^\circ\) to \(+4.43^\circ\). Further experiments
showed that the response also depends on the frame in which the lever is
defined. A tool-fixed lever rotates with the end effector, whereas a
surface-fixed lever remains fixed relative to the calibrated surface.
Displacement along the tool axis produced only a small change in comparison
with the tangential displacement: over the tested range from
\(-40\,\mathrm{mm}\) to \(+40\,\mathrm{mm}\), the set-up rotation changed by
\(0.34^\circ\). The response also did not scale proportionally with the
commanded orientation-offset magnitude.

The experiments did not identify a direction-independent non-zero
compliance-centre displacement. Every tested non-zero tangential displacement
was direction dependent. The assisting lever changed sign when the initial
angular deviation changed sign, and its direction changed with the required
rotation direction in the tangent plane.

Case~H evaluated this direction-and-sign relation at four tangent-plane
directions using a common lever magnitude of \(40\,\mathrm{mm}\). At the two
diagonal directions, the responses obtained with the direction-selected and
fixed levers differed by only \(0.07^\circ\) and \(0.05^\circ\), respectively.
These differences are within the descriptive experimental scatter and are
consistent with the remaining projection of the fixed lever onto the selected
lever direction. About \(t_2\), where this projection vanishes, the two
responses separated from \(-1.61^\circ\) to \(+4.43^\circ\). Within the
definition introduced in \Cref{ch:intro}, the TCP-centred condition is
therefore the direction-independent fixed centre of compliance for the
investigated task because it can be selected without prior knowledge of the
sign or tangent-plane direction of the initial misalignment.

This result does not imply that the TCP produces the largest
alignment-directed rotation in every condition. About \(t_2\), the selected
\(40\,\mathrm{mm}\) tangential lever produced a substantially larger
alignment-directed response than the TCP-centred condition. The TCP therefore
serves as the direction-independent fixed reference, while a displaced centre
provides additional alignment authority when the required rotation direction
is known.

The same distinction applies to the phase after alignment. A non-zero
tangential displacement continues to generate a preferred moment direction as
long as the normal press is maintained. Returning the centre of compliance to
the TCP removes this additional directional coupling. The implemented phase
sequence follows this arrangement: the point-shifted impedance is used during
set-up, while the grinding branch returns to the decoupled impedance. This is
a design choice consistent with the observed coupling mechanism rather than an
experimental result for grinding, because the reported runs ended at the
pre-grinding gate.

The complementary null-space study evaluated the effect of projected damping
and singular-value conditioning under a repeatable internally commanded
disturbance. Projected null-space damping reduced the cumulative projected
null-space motion by approximately \(25\,\%\), and the net redundant
displacement decreased by a similar proportion. Because the damping torque is
proportional to the projected joint velocity, it vanishes when the redundant
motion stops and does not drive the robot back toward its initial redundant
configuration.

Singular-value conditioning behaved differently. The conditioning term was
active before and during the disturbance, and both tested torque magnitudes
ended the disturbance interval with net redundant displacements more than two
orders of magnitude smaller than in the case without null-space torque. Under
the tested disturbance direction, the conditioning term therefore limited the
development of redundant displacement rather than restoring the configuration
afterwards. The term drives the redundant configuration toward the sampled
neighbour with the larger \(\sigma_{\min}\), whether or not redundant motion
is already present. It is therefore not dissipative, and the observed
disturbance response cannot be transferred directly to a disturbance acting
toward increasing \(\sigma_{\min}\). Both null-space contributions act
through the null-space projector and therefore leave the Cartesian task
primary to first order. Of the two tested conditioning magnitudes, the lower
value achieved a comparable suppression of net displacement with substantially
less redundant motion. These results apply to the separate pose-hold
experiment; the individual contributions were not isolated during the
surface-contact measurements.

The experiments therefore support two different uses of the centre of
compliance. The TCP-centred condition provides the direction-independent fixed
reference for the investigated task and can be selected before the initial
misalignment is known. A non-zero tangential displacement instead provides
condition-dependent rotational alignment authority. Its direction and sign
follow from the required alignment rotation, while its magnitude remains
dependent on the tool geometry, contact conditions, and robot configuration.
The implemented controller reflects this distinction by applying the
compliance-centre displacement during set-up and removing it afterwards.
Projected damping and singular-value conditioning provide separate means of
influencing the redundant degree of freedom while keeping the Cartesian task
primary.


\section{Limitations}
\label{sec:limitations}

\subsection{Experimental Range}
\label{subsec:conclusion_experimental_range}

The contact study was performed with one Franka Panda configuration, one
rectangular tool and mounting arrangement, one plane surface, one press
trajectory, and a bounded range of stiffness and compliance-centre values. The
direction-and-sign relation was evaluated about both principal surface
tangents and at two diagonal directions. The measured response magnitudes
therefore remain specific to the investigated geometry and parameter range.

The experiments did not determine one compliance-centre displacement magnitude
that is suitable for every rotation direction or contact geometry. The usable
magnitude is also limited by the resulting joint torques. An exploratory
tool-axis displacement beyond the reported Case-F range reached a joint-torque
limit, showing that the centre position and the corresponding torque demand
must be considered together.

The identification of the TCP as the direction-independent fixed centre of
compliance applies only within the definition and experimental scope given in
\Cref{ch:intro}. The measurements show that every tested non-zero tangential
lever is direction dependent. For the investigated robot configuration, tool,
surface, press trajectory, and range of commanded orientation offsets, the
TCP-centred condition is the tested placement that can be selected without
prior knowledge of the misalignment. The experiments do not establish that the
same conclusion applies to other robots, tools, contact geometries, surfaces,
or grinding processes. They also do not evaluate the compliance-centre
placement during sustained grinding. No quantitative grinding experiment was
performed.

\subsection{Measurement and Tool-Mount Uncertainty}
\label{subsec:conclusion_measurement_uncertainty}

The primary controller-response quantity, the signed set-up rotation, was
obtained from the measured end-effector motion. The supporting alignment angle
\(\theta_{\mathrm{align}}\) was calculated from the end-effector pose and the
calibrated tool normal. This calculation assumes a fixed transformation between
the tool and the end effector.

The tool mounting exhibited approximately \(\pm2^\circ\) of relative
rotational play about \(y_{\mathrm{EE}}\). This value was measured in the
unloaded condition. The instantaneous tool--gripper rotation was not measured
during contact and is therefore not represented by a quantified angular
uncertainty under load. The TCP-height quantity provides a geometry-based
flatness classification and is likewise not a direct measurement of the
physical tool orientation.

The external wrench used as a supporting quantity was obtained from the
libfranka model estimate relative to the value stored at the clearance
transition. No independent force/torque sensor was used. The reported load
quantities should therefore be interpreted as model-estimated values on one
consistent evaluation basis.

\subsection{Null-Space Configuration and Real-Time Operation}
\label{subsec:conclusion_nullspace_scope}

Projected damping and singular-value conditioning were active together during
the surface-contact experiments. They were compared separately only in the
Cartesian pose-hold study under an internally commanded disturbance, and that
comparison carries two bounds of its own. The conditioning torque was active
from the start of each pose-hold trial, so the sigma-only trials entered the
disturbance from a redundant configuration displaced by approximately
\(0.013\,\mathrm{rad}\) from the one at which the trials without
conditioning began. The result therefore describes the closed-loop behaviour of
the complete modes rather than an instantaneous torque comparison at identical
joint configurations. The disturbance also acted in one direction, taken from
the null direction at the initial posture, and the conditioning law is not
dissipative, so the observed rejection cannot be assumed for a disturbance
acting towards larger \(\sigma_{\min}\). The experiments demonstrate
operation through the nominal \(1\,\mathrm{kHz}\) Franka control loop, but
they do not constitute a worst-case execution-time or jitter analysis.


\section{Future Work}
\label{sec:future}

The first direction is to test the fixed-centre conclusion beyond the
arrangement used here. Other tool geometries and surface orientations would show whether the TCP
remains the appropriate direction-independent fixed centre when the contact
geometry changes. Other robot configurations, initial angular directions,
larger commanded offsets and other press trajectories would show whether the
axis asymmetry observed between \(t_1\) and \(t_2\) transfers with it. The
lever study belongs to the same direction. The direction rule should be
evaluated over a denser set of tangent-plane angles while the lever magnitude
is varied independently at each direction, which would establish the dependence
of the required magnitude on the commanded orientation and the contact geometry.

The second direction is the direction-selected extension of the controller,
which the present implementation does not contain. The measurements support the
following sequence, and none of its scheduling was implemented or evaluated
here:

\begin{enumerate}[leftmargin=1.1cm]
\item The run begins with the centre of compliance at the TCP, which requires
no knowledge of the initial deviation.
\item The current tool--surface angular deviation is determined at the
clearance transition.
\item The contact response available at the TCP is assessed against the
alignment the task requires.
\item Where that response is insufficient, the direction-selected tangential
lever is calculated from the geometric rule.
\item Its magnitude \(\rho_c\) is scheduled within the geometric and
joint-torque limits.
\item The centre of compliance is returned smoothly to the TCP once the
alignment criterion is satisfied, before sustained contact or grinding.
\end{enumerate}

Such a strategy would keep the alignment authority the shifted centre
demonstrated while avoiding a persistent directional moment afterwards, and it
would replace a compliance-centre lever chosen before the run with a
geometry-based, run-specific selection. Both the deviation estimate it needs and
the criterion for returning to the TCP remain to be defined and measured.

Independent measurement of the physical tool response would strengthen every
one of these results. An external orientation measurement of the tool face
would separate end-effector rotation from relative motion within the tool
mount, and a direct force/torque sensor would allow the model-estimated
external wrench to be compared with an independent contact-load measurement.
The null-space study could similarly be extended to physical disturbances, to
disturbances acting in other redundant directions, and to experiments that
separate damping from singular-value conditioning during surface contact. The
switching activity observed at the larger conditioning magnitude could be
reduced by adjusting the comparison deadband or its hysteresis, or by replacing
the constant-magnitude direction command with a conditioning torque scaled
smoothly by the sampled singular-value difference. Either change would be a new
controller design and would require the pose-hold trials to be repeated.
