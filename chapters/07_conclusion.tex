\section{Conclusion}
\label{sec:conclusion_summary}

\Revised{A real-time Cartesian impedance controller was implemented for the
contact-induced alignment of a grinding tool with a plane surface. During the
set-up phase, the Cartesian stiffness and damping were spatially shifted from
a selected centre of compliance to the TCP. This shift introduced
translation--rotation coupling, so that the normal interaction load could
generate a moment about the surface tangents.}

\Revised{The controller was evaluated in eight surface-contact cases covering
commanded tilt, directional stiffness, and the position and magnitude of the
compliance-centre lever. Complementary Cartesian pose-hold trials quantified
the projected null-space damping and singular-value-conditioning terms.}

\Revised{Case~A established the baseline response through the zero-tilt condition. The
set-up procedure itself introduced an orientation change. The alignment angle increased from \mbox{\(1.07^\circ\)} to
\mbox{\(2.04^\circ\)}, corresponding to an alignment change of
\mbox{\(-0.97^\circ\)}. The tilted conditions were therefore interpreted together
with this baseline response. The zero-tilt value was kept as a separate
measurement and was not subtracted from the remaining results.}

\Revised{Case~D showed that the position of the virtual centre of compliance had the
largest measured influence on alignment. With the lever directed so
that the induced moment assisted the required rotation, the alignment
improvement reached \mbox{\(6.05^\circ\)} for a tilt about \mbox{\(t_1\)} and
\mbox{\(6.36^\circ\)} for a tilt about \mbox{\(t_2\)}. The corresponding zero-lever
conditions produced \mbox{\(0.56^\circ\)} and \mbox{\(1.38^\circ\)}, respectively. The
assisting lever direction changed with the tilted surface tangent:
\mbox{\(r_{c,t_2}=-60\,\mathrm{mm}\)} was required for the \mbox{\(t_1\)} condition,
whereas \mbox{\(r_{c,t_1}=+60\,\mathrm{mm}\)} was required for the \mbox{\(t_2\)}
condition. The opposite direction produced a response near the zero-lever
range. The contrast between the two directions establishes the lever-sign
requirement for the tested condition.}

\Revised{Case~F showed that reversing the commanded tilt reversed the assisting lever direction. At
the reversed tilt, the lever selected for the positive tilt matched the
zero-lever response on both surface tangents. The reversed lever restored most
of the response about \mbox{\(t_2\)} and about two
fifths of it about \mbox{\(t_1\)}. The sign of the assisting lever therefore
follows the sign of the commanded tilt. The sign rule was reproduced on both
surface tangents, while the magnitude response differed between them.}

\Revised{Case~G showed that the lever magnitude giving the largest improvement depended on the
commanded tilt. For three settings, the improvement decreased after the signed
residual crossed the surface orientation. In the remaining three settings, the
response was still increasing at the largest tested magnitude, so their largest
measured improvement occurred at the boundary of the tested range. The largest
improvements therefore occurred at different magnitudes across the tested
settings.}

\Revised{In the extended-lever comparison of Case~F, the improvement at the reversed
\mbox{\(t_1\)} tilt remained smaller than
at the corresponding positive tilt. The longer lever reduced the residual
alignment angle to \mbox{\(3.77^\circ\)}. This condition was also the only one in
which the orthogonal component increased during set-up. The tool-mount
compliance bears on that comparison.}

\Revised{Case~B provided the rotational-stiffness comparison. Increasing the
stiffness about the tilted tangent reduced the alignment
improvement about \mbox{\(t_1\)}, and the highest tested rotational stiffness also
produced less improvement than the softer settings about \mbox{\(t_2\)}. Case~C
provided the translational-stiffness comparison. Reducing the stiffness
perpendicular to the tilted tangent increased the
response about \mbox{\(t_2\)}, reaching \mbox{\(1.79^\circ\)} at
\mbox{\(300\,\mathrm{N/m}\)}. The corresponding variation about \mbox{\(t_1\)} remained
of the same order as the experimental scatter. Combining the lowest
translational stiffness with the highest rotational stiffness produced less
improvement than the low-translational-stiffness condition alone. This
non-additive response supports joint selection of the two stiffness entries
over the tested range.}

\Revised{Case~H showed that, for four tilt directions in the tangent plane, a
lever selected from the commanded direction at a common
\mbox{\(60\,\mathrm{mm}\)} magnitude assisted at every direction tested. In
the three matched comparisons, holding one lever fixed reduced the improvement
by \mbox{\(1.02^\circ\)} to \mbox{\(3.40^\circ\)}, or
\mbox{\(32\)}--\mbox{\(48\,\%\)}, although the paired initial angles differed
by no more than \mbox{\(0.04^\circ\)}. The matched reductions demonstrate the
benefit of selecting lever direction from the commanded tilt. The common
\mbox{\(60\,\mathrm{mm}\)} magnitude isolates the direction rule in this case.
The normal-coordinate sweep in Case~H changed the improvement
by about one degree over a range of \mbox{\(180\,\mathrm{mm}\)}. This response was
smaller than the tangential-lever effect and measurable beyond the displacement
tested in Case~D.}

\Revised{Within Case~D, placing the centre of compliance at the centre of the grinding face produced
nearly the same response as the zero-lever condition. At the commanded
\mbox{\(10^\circ\)} tilt, the \mbox{\(20\,\mathrm{mm}\)} axial displacement generated only
a small tangential lever component. The measurements are therefore consistent
with the point-shifted impedance formulation: the tangential component of the
lever determines the moment about the tilted surface tangent, while the
component parallel to an ideal normal interaction load contributes no direct
force moment. The small measured normal-coordinate effect can therefore involve
non-normal force components, finite coupled motion, contact geometry, or
tool-mount compliance. These effects provide the physical context for the
measured normal-coordinate response.}

\Revised{Case~E showed that tilts commanded about the grinding-face axes produced alignment improvements
between those obtained for the two surface-tangent directions. Their agreement
with a linear combination of the tangent-axis measurements provides a
consistency check for the surface-frame interpretation. Across these four
settings, the comparison associates the measured directional asymmetry mainly
with the surface-frame response rather than solely with the rectangular
grinding-face geometry.}

\Revised{The Cartesian pose-hold trials showed that projected null-space damping reduced
the cumulative redundant joint excursion by approximately \mbox{\(25\,\%\)} relative
to the matched condition without a null-space torque. Both tested conditioning
commands returned the minimum singular value to approximately its initial level
after the disturbance. The \mbox{\(1.5\,\mathrm{N\,m}\)} command achieved this recovery
with lower joint excursion and peak Cartesian position error. All pose-hold trials remained
below the predefined \mbox{\(2\,\mathrm{mm}\)} position-error limit. These measurements
quantify both null-space terms under the commanded disturbance.}

\Revised{Overall, the experiments show that the spatial placement and sign of the
virtual centre of compliance governed the alignment response more strongly
than the stiffness variations considered in this work. The point-shifted
Cartesian impedance provided a direct means of converting the commanded
normal interaction into an alignment moment without prescribing an explicit
rotational trajectory during set-up.}

\Revised{The three research questions can therefore be answered as one sequence.
First, stiffness changes the resistance
to contact-induced alignment, but its measured influence was smaller than that
of compliance-centre placement. Second, the lever direction and sign determine
whether the press assists or opposes alignment, while the preferred magnitude
is condition-dependent rather than universal. Third, the signed tilt direction
defines the geometric selection rule. The tangential lever is chosen
perpendicular to the signed tilt axis so that the induced moment opposes the
angular error. The
four-direction Case-H result supports this rule beyond the principal surface
tangents. The matched fixed-lever comparisons demonstrate the benefit of
tilt-dependent direction selection.}


\section{Limitations}
\label{sec:limitations}

\subsection{Experimental Range}
\label{subsec:conclusion_experimental_range}

\Revised{Common conditions for the Panda configuration, tool mount, plane surface,
and press trajectory were maintained across the contact campaign. This isolated
the controller-parameter effects. Both signs of the commanded tilt were measured, and the assisting
lever direction reversed with the sign on both surface tangents. This
direction-and-sign rule is the transferable controller result. The tested
lever magnitudes are geometry-specific design values.}

\Revised{The systematic magnitude sweep used four lever magnitudes along one
surface tangent at a time. Case~H additionally tested four combined in-plane
tilt and lever directions at one common magnitude. These measurements identify
the assisting direction and show that the magnitude giving the largest
improvement depends on the commanded tilt. The diagonal-direction results apply
to the common \mbox{\(60\,\mathrm{mm}\)} magnitude used in Case~H.}

\Revised{The stiffness comparison used three values for each varied entry. The combined
low-translational-stiffness and high-rotational-stiffness result applies to the
selected pair tested for each axis.}

\subsection{Measurement and Tool-Mount Uncertainty}
\label{subsec:conclusion_measurement_uncertainty}

\Revised{The grinding-face direction was reconstructed from the measured
end-effector orientation and its calibrated direction in the end-effector
frame. This measurement chain quantifies the combined robot--gripper--tool
response. The approximately \mbox{\(\pm2^\circ\)} relative tool rotation about
\mbox{\(y_{\mathrm{EE}}\)} defines its mount-related uncertainty. The largest
within-setting variability was measured in the \mbox{\(t_2\)} conditions.}

\Revised{The calibrated surface normal and tool direction complete the alignment
measurement chain. Bias-corrected model estimates supplied by libfranka
provided the supporting external-force and moment quantities across matched
runs.}


\subsection{Null-Space Configuration and Real-Time Operation}
\label{subsec:conclusion_nullspace_scope}

\Revised{The surface-contact results establish alignment performance with projected
damping as the active null-space configuration. Matched pose-hold trials
quantified projected damping and singular-value conditioning under an
internally commanded point-force equivalent.}

\Revised{The real-time computer passed libfranka's supplied communication check before
the experiments. The controller subsequently completed every reported contact
and pose-hold trial through the nominal \mbox{\(1\,\mathrm{kHz}\)} interface. The
reported campaign therefore completed at the nominal control rate.}


\section{Future Work}
\label{sec:future}

\Revised{Three extensions to the compliance-centre study are considered most
important. Finer lever-magnitude sampling would extend the measured response
around and beyond the best tested regions. A finer angular sweep, for example in
\mbox{\(15^\circ\)} or \mbox{\(30^\circ\)} increments, should then be combined
with magnitude variation at each direction. Case~H already samples four
directions, including two diagonals between the surface-axis cases; the next
step is therefore a denser joint characterization of direction and magnitude,
not merely the addition of intermediate directions.}

\Revised{An angular measurement of the grinding face taken independently of the
end-effector pose would separate the rotation of the tool from that of the
mount. This bears on the reversed \mbox{\(t_1\)} result and on the magnitude of
every reported alignment change. Further contact geometries, tool faces and
press trajectories would establish how far the selection rule carries beyond the
investigated arrangement.}

\Revised{The grinding-face orientation should be measured independently of the
end-effector orientation. A more rigid tool mount would reduce the relative
motion observed at the gripper, while an external angular measurement would
allow controller rotation and tool-mount rotation to be separated. An
independent force/torque sensor would also permit the model-estimated external
wrench to be evaluated alongside a direct contact-load measurement.}

\Revised{The null-space study can be extended with a physical disturbance applied to
an intermediate robot link and with the combined damping and
singular-value-conditioning mode. A corresponding surface-interaction
comparison would show whether the pose-hold ordering is preserved during
contact.}

\Revised{Finally, the compliance-centre selection can be incorporated into the
controller as a geometry-based scheduling step. After the initial tilt is
estimated at the clearance transition, the lever direction could be selected
so that the induced moment opposes the measured angular error. Such a strategy
would replace a lever fixed in advance with a run-specific placement while
continuing to use the point-shifted Cartesian impedance implemented in this
work.}
