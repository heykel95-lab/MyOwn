\chapter{Conclusion and Future Work}
\label{ch:conclusion}

\section{Summary}

This thesis developed and experimentally evaluated a real-time Cartesian
impedance controller for contact-induced alignment of a rectangular tool on a
planar workpiece. The controller runs on a 7-\abbr{DOF} Franka Emika Panda and
uses the joint-torque command
\[
  \tau_{\mathrm{cmd}}
  =
  J^\top w_{\mathrm{cmd}}
  +\tau_{\mathrm{null}}
  +c(q,\dot q).
\]
The Cartesian gains are expressed in explicitly defined base and surface
frames. During set-up, a virtual centre of compliance is introduced by shifting
a pole-frame \(6\times6\) stiffness and damping matrix to the TCP through an
adjoint congruence. The resulting off-diagonal blocks couple translational
error to rotational moment and rotational error to force.

The experimental study varied the common tangent-axis rotational stiffness
\(K_{R,t_1}=K_{R,t_2}\) and the three surface-frame coordinates of the virtual
compliance centre. The reported data comprise \(92\) run records, of which
\(89\) are admissible for their
recorded quantities. Settling, formulation equivalence, and nominal
repeatability were evaluated before the parameter studies. The principal
experimental conclusions are:

\begin{enumerate}
  \item Reducing both tangent-axis rotational stiffnesses from
        \(50\) to \(5\,\mathrm{Nm/rad}\) increased the mean alignment
        improvement from approximately \(0.79^\circ\) to \(2.38^\circ\).
        Across these settings, the mean model-estimated normal load remained
        within \(60.27\)--\(60.77\,\mathrm{N}\).

  \item The two in-plane coordinates of the virtual compliance centre strongly
        influenced the alignment response. An additive quadratic model in
        \(t_1\), \(t_1^2\), \(t_2\), and \(t_2^2\) accounts for
        \(R^2=0.962\) of the response over \(54\) fitted sweep runs; three
        additional edge-setting runs were retained as a held-out consistency
        check.

  \item The in-plane response was non-monotonic. The fitted model predicts a
        local maximum near \(t_1=+62\,\mathrm{mm}\) and
        \(t_2=+30\,\mathrm{mm}\), whereas sufficiently unsuitable placements
        produced negative alignment improvements as low as
        \(-10.31^\circ\). The predicted coordinate pair was not tested
        directly and is therefore not an experimentally confirmed optimum.

  \item Within the investigated configuration and parameter range, the
        physical normal coordinate provided little additional explanatory
        power after the two in-plane coordinates were included: adding it
        increased \(R^2\) from \(0.962\) to \(0.966\). This is a
        configuration-specific result rather than evidence that the normal
        coordinate is irrelevant in every contact geometry.
\end{enumerate}

The observed dependence on compliance-centre position is consistent with the
translation--rotation coupling of the shifted impedance. In particular, the
model-estimated external force lies predominantly along the surface normal, so
an in-plane lever produces a larger moment about the virtual centre than a
nearly parallel normal lever. The experiment supports this mechanism but does
not establish a universal placement rule because the physical contact
location and first-contact edge were not manipulated independently.

A subsequent calibrated-horizontal-plane pilot tested the direct surface-frame
lever required for an imposed \(t_1\) mismatch. The sign check showed that
\(r_{c,t_2}=+60\,\mathrm{mm}\) did not produce full-face contact, whereas
\(-60\,\mathrm{mm}\) did. Refinement to
\(r_{c,t_2}=-40\,\mathrm{mm}\) produced operator-observed full-face contact in
all three repetitions. Across those repetitions, the EE-inferred improvement
was \(1.70\pm0.05^\circ\), the \(90\%\) response time was
\(1.535\pm0.015\,\mathrm{s}\), and the steady model-estimated load was
\(44.83\pm0.15\,\mathrm{N}\). These records are separate from the
original \(92\)-record campaign. Because the tool mount retained approximately
\(2^\circ\) of play, they establish repeatable behaviour of the mounted system
and a repeatable qualitative full-face contact state, not an independently
measured physical residual angle. The equivalent axis-specific sign check for
\(t_2\) alignment remains to be completed.

The set-up response reaches a quasi-steady condition at approximately
\(4.6\,\mathrm{s}\). Reanalysis of the longer traces shows that the changes
after \(5\,\mathrm{s}\) are below the repeatability-based interpretation
threshold for the nominal push parameters. Alignment also continued during the
operator-terminated grinding phase by a further \(0.18^\circ\) to
\(0.64^\circ\); because the grinding duration was uncontrolled, this is
reported as a directional observation rather than a calibrated magnitude.

The SVD-based null-space controller is implemented and instrumented, including
the distinction between the smallest Jacobian singular value
\(\sigma_{\min}=\sigma_6\) and the structural null direction \(v_7\).
However, no controlled sigma-enabled campaign isolates its effect. Improved
singularity conditioning is therefore not an experimental conclusion of this
thesis.

\section{Main Contributions}
\label{sec:main_contributions}

The main contributions are:

\begin{itemize}
  \item implementation of a frame-consistent real-time Cartesian impedance
        controller with a spatially shifted \(6\times6\) stiffness and damping
        representation for the 7-\abbr{DOF} Panda;

  \item development of a repeatable surface-contact methodology that separates
        set-up settling, formulation equivalence, repeatability, stiffness
        variation, and compliance-centre variation;

  \item quantitative characterization of the influence of the paired
        tangent-axis rotational stiffness on contact-induced tool alignment;
        and

  \item experimental characterization of the strong, non-monotonic dependence
        of alignment on the two in-plane compliance-centre coordinates,
        including harmful placements and a model-predicted local maximum.
\end{itemize}

\section{Limitations}
\label{sec:limitations}

The measurements were obtained with one robot, one primary tool geometry, one
wooden workpiece, and one fixture arrangement. The extent to which the
identified response transfers to other tools, surfaces, contact locations,
friction conditions, and initial angular errors is unknown. The exact material
grades, surface finish, active payload parameters, robot transforms, and
real-time-computer hardware were not recorded completely.

The physical workpiece normal was estimated by hand-seating the tool face
against the surface three times. The resulting angular spread is approximately
\(\pm1.9^\circ\). Absolute residual angles inherit this uncertainty, although
the within-run alignment improvement changes by less than \(0.1^\circ\) across
the three physical-plane estimates. The controller also uses one configured
normal for plane height, clearance, surface-frame construction, and commanded
tool orientation. It does not independently represent a calibrated physical
plane and an intentional command-angle offset.

No dedicated force/torque sensor was used. The reported load and equivalent
contact location are inferred from libfranka's model-estimated external wrench
\texttt{O\_F\_ext\_hat\_K}. Distributed surface contact, model error, and
uncertainty in the selected tool feature prevent treating the inferred
location as an exact physical point contact.

The compliance-centre study uses cross-shaped sampling rather than a complete
two-dimensional grid. Consequently, the additive quadratic model does not
identify a \(t_1t_2\) interaction, and its predicted local maximum was not
measured directly. The unused contact-edge configuration provides a held-out
consistency check only; it does not establish generalisation beyond the
investigated region.

The rotational-stiffness campaign assigned the same value to
\(K_{R,t_1}\) and \(K_{R,t_2}\) in every setting. Moreover, the imposed
configured-to-physical plane mismatch was predominantly a rotation about
\(t_2\). The results therefore establish the effect of the paired setting but
do not identify the two axis-specific stiffness sensitivities.

The fixture moved under load, and the tool loosened in the gripper during two
runs. Those runs were repeated, but the mounted tool also retained
approximately \(\pm2^\circ\) of rotational play about \(y_{\mathrm{EE}}\).
The controller reconstructs the tool axis from the EE pose and a fixed
calibrated transform; it does not measure this relative motion. A tool face
that remains physically flat can therefore coexist with a changing
EE-inferred alignment angle. The angular results characterize the combined
robot--gripper--tool response and do not isolate physical tool-face rotation
from gripper compliance. The play direction is not labelled simply as
\(t_2\), because \(y_{\mathrm{EE}}\) is EE-fixed whereas \(t_2\) is defined by
the calibrated plane in the base frame.

The active experiment configuration combines projected joint damping with the
two-point singular-value bias. Without matched null-space-enabled and
null-space-disabled trials, its influence on the contact response cannot be
separated. The geometric Jacobian is unscaled, and the implemented projector
is kinematic rather than dynamically consistent. The recorded callback spacing
supports sustained operation near the nominal \(1\,\mathrm{kHz}\) interface
rate, but worst-case local callback execution time was not measured.

\section{Future Work}
\label{sec:future}

The physical plane should be estimated from at least three non-collinear
physical probe points after applying the calibrated probe offset. Repeated
plane estimates and held-out points can then quantify height and normal
uncertainty. The resulting physical plane should determine clearance,
projection, the surface frame, and the alignment metric, while the commanded
tool normal is defined independently through signed angular offsets about the
surface tangents.

The model-predicted compliance-centre maximum should be tested using a local
\(3\times3\) grid around
\(t_1=[45,60,75]\,\mathrm{mm}\) and
\(t_2=[15,30,45]\,\mathrm{mm}\), with repeated measurements at each point.
Matched decoupled, contact-edge-pole, and predicted-pole trials at controlled
initial errors of \(0^\circ\), \(5^\circ\), and \(10^\circ\) would separate
controller placement from starting geometry. Repeating these trials at
different surface locations and with a deliberately reversed first-contact
edge would test the spatial consistency and sign of the observed response.

The tangent-axis stiffnesses should also be separated experimentally. One
matched sweep should vary \(K_{R,t_1}\) while holding \(K_{R,t_2}\) fixed, and
a second should vary \(K_{R,t_2}\) while holding \(K_{R,t_1}\) fixed.
Repeating both sweeps with initial mismatches predominantly about \(t_1\) and
predominantly about \(t_2\) would distinguish gain direction from excitation
direction.

The nominal set-up duration should be confirmed at
\(4.5\), \(5\), \(6\), and \(8\,\mathrm{s}\), with repeated trials at
\(5\,\mathrm{s}\). A general phase transition should depend on completion of
the commanded press ramp followed by a short interval of low alignment and
load rates, with a calculated timeout as a fallback.

A dedicated force/torque sensor or independent contact instrumentation would
improve the normal-load estimate and permit a more defensible analysis of
distributed contact and equivalent contact location. Further experiments
should also include additional tools, surface materials, fixture conditions,
and initial orientation errors.

The null-space method requires matched damping-only and
damping-plus-conditioning trials. These experiments should report
\(\Delta\sigma_{\min}\), projected joint velocity, Cartesian task drift,
projector residual, joint motion, and sigma torque. Probe configurations should
be checked against joint limits, and callback wall-clock duration should be
instrumented to establish worst-case real-time execution behaviour.
