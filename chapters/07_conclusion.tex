\chapter{Conclusion and Future Work}
\label{ch:conclusion}

\section{Conclusion}
\label{sec:conclusion_summary}

This thesis developed and experimentally evaluated a real-time Cartesian
impedance controller for contact-induced alignment of a grinding tool with a
plane surface. The controller runs in the Franka Control Interface torque loop
and combines surface-relative Cartesian impedance control, a virtual centre of
compliance, and null-space control. During set-up, stiffness and damping defined
at the selected centre of compliance \(p_c\) are shifted to the TCP. The
resulting translation--rotation coupling allows the commanded normal pressing
action to generate an additional alignment moment without a time-varying
rotational trajectory.

The experiments focused on the measured end-effector response during contact
set-up and on the selection of a fixed centre of compliance when the sign and
tangent-plane direction of the initial angular misalignment are unknown before
contact. The TCP-centred condition, Cartesian stiffness, and displaced centres
were evaluated under matched conditions.

With the centre of compliance at the TCP, contact already produced substantial
end-effector rotation about \(t_1\). A commanded \(+10^\circ\) orientation
offset produced \(+7.57^\circ\) of signed set-up rotation, close to the
\(+7.87^\circ\) obtained with the assisting \(40\,\mathrm{mm}\) lever. This
zero-lever response is consistent with contributions from the physical contact
geometry, ordinary rotational impedance, and compliance of the tool mounting.
Their individual contributions were not isolated experimentally, and
\(m_{r_T}=r_T\times\Delta\hat f_{\mathrm{ext}}\) remains a geometry-based
interpretation of the interaction.

About \(t_2\), the same commanded offset produced \(-1.63^\circ\) of signed
end-effector rotation, while the final configuration satisfied the TCP-height
flatness criterion. The tool mounting exhibited approximately \(\pm2^\circ\)
of rotational play about the mounting direction associated with \(t_2\). This
relative tool--gripper motion is absent from the robot state and was not tracked
under contact load. The result therefore characterises the measured
end-effector motion; the instantaneous physical tool orientation remains
subject to the mounting compliance. The TCP-height criterion and the signed
end-effector rotation describe different aspects of the final contact
configuration.

Increasing the rotational stiffness about the commanded rotation axis reduced
the set-up rotation, most clearly about \(t_1\). Changes in cross-axis
translational stiffness produced a smaller response variation over the tested
range. The combined low-translational-stiffness and high-rotational-stiffness
condition was consistent with the individual effects acting approximately
additively within the descriptive comparison scale. The axis dependence of the
TCP-centred response remained present across the tested stiffness settings.

Changing the virtual centre of compliance produced the largest response
variation over the tested parameter ranges. With
\[
r_c = p_{\mathrm{TCP}}-p_c,
\qquad
m_{c,K} = -\,r_c\times f_K,
\]
a tangential displacement introduces an additional commanded moment from the
normal pressing action. The assisting lever lies in the surface tangent plane
and perpendicular to the required rotation direction. Its sign changes with
the sign of the angular misalignment.

About \(t_2\), the selected \(40\,\mathrm{mm}\) lever changed the signed set-up
rotation from \(-1.63^\circ\) to \(+4.43^\circ\). Additional experiments showed
that the lever-definition frame is part of the controller setting: a tool-fixed
lever rotates with the end effector, while a surface-fixed lever remains fixed
relative to the calibrated surface. A displacement along the tool axis changed
the set-up rotation by only \(0.34^\circ\) over the tested
\(-40\) to \(+40\,\mathrm{mm}\) range. The response also varied with the
commanded orientation-offset magnitude without following a proportional
scaling relation.

Within the tested conditions, every non-zero tangential compliance-centre
displacement that assisted alignment was specific to the initial misalignment.
Reversing the sign of the angular deviation required the lever to be placed on
the opposite side of the TCP, while changing the direction of the required
alignment rotation required a corresponding change in the tangential lever
direction. A non-zero displacement therefore cannot be selected independently
of the initial misalignment direction and sign.

Case~H evaluated this geometric direction-and-sign relation at four
tangent-plane directions using a common lever magnitude of
\(40\,\mathrm{mm}\). At the two diagonal directions, the direction-selected
and fixed-lever responses differed by only \(0.07^\circ\) and
\(0.05^\circ\), respectively, because the fixed lever retained a substantial
projection along the selected direction. About \(t_2\), where this projection
vanishes, the responses separated from \(-1.61^\circ\) to
\(+4.43^\circ\).

Within the definition introduced in \Cref{ch:intro}, the TCP-centred condition
is therefore the direction-independent fixed centre among the tested positions.
It can be selected before the sign and tangent-plane direction of the initial
misalignment are known. A displaced centre serves a different role: when the
required rotation direction is available, a suitable tangential lever provides
additional alignment authority. These two roles separate a fixed reference
from a condition-dependent alignment mechanism.

The same mechanism guides the phase after alignment. A non-zero tangential
lever retains its preferred rotational sense while the normal press continues.
The implemented controller therefore uses the shifted impedance during set-up
and returns to the decoupled TCP-centred impedance in the grinding branch. This
phase arrangement follows the measured coupling mechanism. The reported runs
ended at the pre-grinding gate, so the sustained grinding phase was outside the
experimental evaluation.

The complementary null-space study evaluated projected damping and
singular-value conditioning under a repeatable internally commanded
disturbance. Projected null-space damping reduced the cumulative projected
null-space motion by approximately \(25\,\%\), with a similar reduction in net
redundant displacement. Its velocity-dependent torque vanishes as the redundant
motion stops.

Singular-value conditioning was active before and throughout the commanded
disturbance. Both tested torque magnitudes ended the disturbance interval with
net redundant displacements more than two orders of magnitude smaller than the
condition without null-space torque. Under the tested disturbance direction,
the conditioning term acted concurrently with the disturbance and strongly
suppressed the resulting redundant displacement. The near-zero net displacement
therefore reflects disturbance rejection during its application rather than a
subsequent return of the redundant configuration after the disturbance. The
conditioning action drives the configuration toward the sampled neighbour with
the larger \(\sigma_{\min}\). The lower tested conditioning magnitude achieved
comparable suppression of net displacement with substantially less cumulative
redundant motion. The Cartesian position error remained below the predefined
\(2\,\mathrm{mm}\) limit in all pose-hold trials. These results apply to the
separate pose-hold experiment; the surface-contact measurements used the
combined secondary-torque mode.

Overall, the experiments support two uses of the centre of compliance. The
TCP-centred condition provides the direction-independent fixed reference for
the investigated task. A non-zero tangential displacement provides
condition-dependent rotational alignment authority, with direction and sign set
by the required alignment rotation and magnitude set by the contact geometry,
robot configuration, and operating limits. Projected damping and singular-value
conditioning provide separate means of influencing the redundant degree of
freedom while keeping the Cartesian task primary to first order.

\section{Limitations}
\label{sec:limitations}

\subsection{Experimental Range}
\label{subsec:conclusion_experimental_range}

The contact study used one Franka Panda configuration, one rectangular tool and
mounting arrangement, one plane surface, one press trajectory, and a bounded
range of stiffness and compliance-centre values. The direction-and-sign
relation was evaluated about both principal surface tangents and at two
diagonal directions. The measured response magnitudes therefore apply to this
experimental geometry and parameter range.

No single compliance-centre displacement magnitude was established for all
rotation directions or contact geometries. The usable magnitude is also limited
by the resulting joint torques. An exploratory tool-axis displacement beyond
the reported Case-F range reached a joint-torque limit, which links the centre
position directly to the available torque margin.

The TCP-centred condition is identified as the direction-independent fixed
centre within the definition and experimental scope of \Cref{ch:intro}. The
measurements establish the direction dependence of every tested non-zero
tangential lever for the investigated robot configuration, tool, surface, press
trajectory, and orientation-offset range. Other robots, tools, contact
geometries, surface orientations, and grinding processes require separate
evaluation. Sustained grinding was also outside the measured scope.

\subsection{Measurement and Tool-Mount Uncertainty}
\label{subsec:conclusion_measurement_uncertainty}

The primary controller-response quantity, the signed set-up rotation, was
obtained from the measured end-effector motion. The supporting alignment angle
\(\theta_{\mathrm{align}}\) was calculated from the end-effector pose and the
calibrated tool normal and therefore uses a fixed tool-to-end-effector
transformation.

The tool mounting exhibited approximately \(\pm2^\circ\) of relative rotational
play about \(y_{\mathrm{EE}}\) in the unloaded condition. The instantaneous
tool--gripper rotation under contact load was not measured, so the value is a
mounting characteristic rather than a quantified angular uncertainty during
the experiment. The TCP-height quantity provides a geometry-based flatness
classification and is not a direct measurement of the physical tool
orientation.

The supporting external wrench was obtained from the libfranka model estimate
relative to the value stored at the clearance transition. No independent
force/torque sensor was used. The reported load quantities therefore represent
model-estimated values on one consistent evaluation basis.

\subsection{Null-Space Configuration and Real-Time Operation}
\label{subsec:conclusion_nullspace_scope}

Projected damping and singular-value conditioning were active together during
the surface-contact experiments. Their separate assessment used the Cartesian
pose-hold study with an internally commanded disturbance. The conditioning
torque was already active before the disturbance, so the sigma-only trials
entered the disturbance from a redundant configuration displaced by
approximately \(0.013\,\mathrm{rad}\) from the reference configuration used by
the trials without conditioning. The comparison therefore describes the
closed-loop behaviour of the complete modes.

The disturbance acted along one redundant direction derived from the initial
posture. Singular-value conditioning is directional and non-dissipative, so the
measured response is specific to this disturbance direction. Real-time
operation was demonstrated through the nominal \(1\,\mathrm{kHz}\) Franka
control loop. A separate worst-case execution-time and scheduling-jitter study
was outside the experimental scope.

\section{Future Work}
\label{sec:future}

A first extension is to evaluate the fixed-centre result over a broader contact
geometry. Relevant variations include other tool shapes, surface orientations,
robot configurations, initial angular directions, commanded offsets, and press
trajectories. A denser set of tangent-plane rotation directions combined with
independent variation of the lever magnitude would further characterise the
relation between required displacement magnitude, initial orientation, and
contact geometry.

A second extension is an online direction-selected compliance-centre strategy.
The experimental results motivate the following sequence:

\begin{enumerate}[leftmargin=1.1cm]
\item Begin the run with the centre of compliance at the TCP.
\item Estimate the current tool--surface angular deviation at the clearance
transition.
\item Evaluate the alignment response available with the TCP-centred
impedance.
\item Introduce the direction-selected tangential lever when additional
alignment authority is required.
\item Schedule its magnitude \(\rho_c\) within the geometric and joint-torque
limits.
\item Return the centre smoothly to the TCP after the alignment criterion is
reached and before sustained grinding.
\end{enumerate}

This extension would replace a lever selected before the run with a
geometry-based, run-specific displacement. Its implementation requires a
validated deviation estimate, a criterion for activating the displaced centre,
and a criterion for returning to the TCP. These elements remain to be designed
and evaluated experimentally.

Independent measurement of the physical tool response would strengthen the
contact analysis. An external orientation measurement of the tool face would
separate end-effector rotation from relative motion within the tool mount. A
direct force/torque sensor would provide an independent reference for the
model-estimated external wrench.

The null-space study can also be extended to physical disturbances, additional
redundant directions, and contact experiments that vary projected damping and
singular-value conditioning separately. The increased switching activity
observed at the larger conditioning magnitude suggests two further controller
variants: a wider comparison deadband with hysteresis, or a conditioning torque
scaled continuously by the sampled singular-value difference. Both variants
would require a new pose-hold evaluation before use in the surface-contact task.
