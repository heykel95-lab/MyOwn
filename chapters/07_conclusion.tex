\chapter{Conclusion and Future Work}
\label{ch:conclusion}

\section{Summary}

A tool tilted against a plane will not come flat by translational compliance
alone. This thesis built a real-time Cartesian impedance controller for the
7-\abbr{DOF} Panda that closes the remaining angle through the impedance
itself, shifting the \(6\times6\) stiffness and damping to the TCP from a
chosen centre of compliance during set-up. Where that centre is placed turned
out to matter more than anything put inside it.

Four quantities were varied across the campaign: the commanded tilt, the
rotational stiffness of each surface tangent, the translational stiffness
perpendicular to the tilted tangent, and the compliance-centre lever.
Calibrating the surface plane and the grinding-face axis independently of one
another defined the commanded tilt against a measured physical surface frame.
All \(75\) runs completed set-up, and none was discarded.

\begin{enumerate}
  \item \textbf{Case A, commanded tilt.} The floor came first. With no
        commanded tilt at all, the set-up press \emph{increased} misalignment
        by \(0.97^\circ\), which bounds what the present fixture and
        calibration can resolve. Every nonzero tilt then improved alignment,
        and by roughly \(1.6\) times as much about \(t_2\) as about \(t_1\) at
        both magnitudes. Recovery does not scale with the error, though: the
        fraction removed fell from \(38\,\%\) at \(5^\circ\) about \(t_1\) to
        \(9\,\%\) at \(10^\circ\).

  \item \textbf{Case B, axis-specific rotational stiffness.} Every increase in
        the rotational stiffness of the tilted axis gave back less angle. Ten
        times the stiffness, \(5\) to \(50\,\mathrm{Nm/rad}\), removed roughly
        a quarter of the improvement about \(t_1\), \(0.64^\circ\) against
        \(0.49^\circ\), and nothing tested beat the \(5\,\mathrm{Nm/rad}\)
        baseline. The tangent entries moved singly, so the result holds per
        axis and says nothing about a common paired value.

  \item \textbf{Case C, cross-axis translational stiffness.} Softening the
        translation perpendicular to the tilted axis by a factor of about seven
        added roughly a tenth to the improvement about \(t_2\),
        \(1.59^\circ\) to \(1.79^\circ\), while \(t_1\) stayed inside the
        interpretation threshold. Combining that soft translation with
        \(50\,\mathrm{Nm/rad}\) of rotational stiffness cancelled the gain
        entirely, so the two entries are not independent.

  \item \textbf{Case D, virtual centre of compliance.} Lever placement
        dominated everything else examined. Sixty millimetres along the tangent
        perpendicular to the tilted axis multiplied the improvement by about
        eleven about \(t_1\), \(0.56^\circ\) to \(6.05^\circ\), and by about
        five about \(t_2\), \(1.38^\circ\) to \(6.36^\circ\) --- leaving
        residuals of \(0.93^\circ\) and \(2.14^\circ\) and a steady
        model-estimated load roughly a fifth lower. Sign decides the outcome,
        and the favourable sign is opposite on the two tangents: taken the
        wrong way, the lever removed the correction rather than reducing it,
        \(0.02\pm0.06^\circ\). Displacing the centre \(20\,\mathrm{mm}\) along
        the surface normal instead changed nothing beyond the interpretation
        threshold.

  \item \textbf{Case E, tilt about the tool axes.} Commanding the same tilt
        about the axes of the grinding face rather than the surface tangents
        landed between the two tangent values, with the two tool-axis results
        about half as far apart as the tangent pair. Both sit close to the
        linear combination of their tangent components, which places the
        asymmetry with the surface frame and not with the geometry of the face.
\end{enumerate}

Both the sign reversal and the lighter contact at the favourable placements
follow from the shifted impedance itself, where the sign of the lever sets the
direction of the moment the commanded penetration produces.
\Cref{sec:discussion_contact} works that argument through.

Settling was not an issue. Over the final second of the \(5\,\mathrm{s}\)
interval the model-estimated load moved by a median of \(0.10\,\mathrm{N}\) and
the tip angle by a median of \(0.00^\circ\), across all \(75\) runs.

One limitation shapes how the \(t_2\) numbers should be read. The mount leaves
the tool approximately \(2^\circ\) of play about \(y_{EE}\), a direction the
commanded spin places only \(25^\circ\) from \(t_2\), and motion there is
invisible to a metric taken from the robot pose. It can conceal correction that
happened but cannot manufacture it, which makes the reported \(t_2\)
improvements lower bounds and leaves the tangent-axis asymmetry standing.

Implementation of the SVD-based null-space controller is complete and
instrumented, down to the distinction between the smallest Jacobian singular
value \(\sigma_{\min}=\sigma_6\) and the structural null direction \(v_7\). No
controlled sigma-enabled campaign isolates its effect, so improved singularity
conditioning is not an experimental conclusion of this thesis.

\section{Main Contributions}
\label{sec:main_contributions}

Four contributions carry the work.

\begin{itemize}
  \item The controller realises a frame-consistent Cartesian impedance in real
        time on the 7-\abbr{DOF} Panda, with a \(6\times6\) stiffness and
        damping spatially shifted from a chosen centre of compliance.

  \item The contact methodology calibrates the surface plane and the
        grinding-face axis independently of each other. Commanded tilt,
        stiffness and compliance-centre placement can therefore each be varied
        alone against a measured physical surface frame.

  \item The rotational and translational stiffness contributions to
        contact-induced alignment are measured per axis, which separates the
        \(t_1\) and \(t_2\) responses that a common paired setting leaves
        confounded.

  \item The compliance-centre lever is identified as the dominant parameter for
        contact-induced alignment, together with its sign dependence, the
        reversal of the favourable sign between the two surface tangents, and
        the absence of a comparable effect along the surface normal.
\end{itemize}

\section{Limitations}
\label{sec:limitations}

Everything reported here comes from one robot, one tool geometry, one wooden
surface and one fixture. Whether the identified response survives a change in
any of them is unknown, as is its behaviour at other contact locations,
friction conditions and initial angular errors. Exact replication is limited
further by what went unrecorded: material grades, surface finish, payload
parameters, robot transforms, and the real-time computer.

Calibration carries its own uncertainty into every absolute angle. Three probed
points fitted the surface plane, checked against a fourth held out of the fit
at \(0.82\,\mathrm{mm}\), and the grinding-face normal was calibrated
separately to a held-out angular residual of \(0.50^\circ\). Worse, the probing
used the same robot and tool whose alignment is then measured, so a systematic
error in the tool transform would move the plane estimate and the alignment
metric together and largely cancel in the comparison that detects it.

No dedicated force/torque sensor was fitted. Load and equivalent contact
location are both inferred from libfranka's model-estimated external wrench,
and three things stand between that inference and a physical point contact:
distributed surface contact, model error, and uncertainty in which tool feature
was selected.

Three tangential levels on one axis at a time is what the compliance-centre
study sampled. That locates the favourable sign and establishes how large the
effect is. It identifies no optimum, resolves no interaction between the two
in-plane coordinates, and reaches the surface normal at a single displacement.

Sign was never varied on the command side either: every commanded tilt was
positive. Any asymmetry between positive and negative tilt is unmeasured, and
with it the generality of the favourable lever sign.

Approximately \(\pm2^\circ\) of rotational play about \(y_{\mathrm{EE}}\)
remains in the mount, and the controller never measures it: the tool axis is
reconstructed from the EE pose through a transform assumed fixed. A tool face
that stays physically flat can therefore coexist with a changing EE-inferred
alignment angle, and the angular results characterise the combined
robot--gripper--tool response without isolating face rotation from gripper
compliance. Calling the play direction \(t_2\) would compound the problem,
since \(y_{\mathrm{EE}}\) is EE-fixed while \(t_2\) is fixed in the base frame
by the calibrated plane. The commanded spin brings them within \(25^\circ\) of
one another, which is what makes a \(t_2\) tilt the condition most exposed.

Projected joint damping and the two-point singular-value bias ran together in
every reported run. Separating the null space's influence on the contact
response would need matched enabled and disabled trials, and there were none.
Two further caveats sit on that implementation: the geometric Jacobian is
unscaled, and the projector is kinematic rather than dynamically consistent.
Recorded callback spacing supports sustained operation near the nominal
\(1\,\mathrm{kHz}\) interface rate, though the worst-case local callback
execution time was never measured.

\section{Future Work}
\label{sec:future}

Reversing the commanded tilt comes first. Every case used a positive tilt, so
whether the favourable lever sign follows the tilted axis or the direction of
the initial error is still open. Repeating the Case-D levels at \(-5^\circ\)
and \(-10^\circ\) about each tangent would separate the two, and until that is
done the sign rule is a result about this campaign rather than about the
mechanism.

The compliance-centre response should then be sampled in two dimensions instead
of along one axis at a time. A local grid in \(t_1\) and \(t_2\) around the
favourable placements would identify an optimum and any interaction between the
coordinates, neither of which a three-level sweep can resolve. Further normal
displacements would show whether the absence of a normal effect survives beyond
the single \(20\,\mathrm{mm}\) offset tested.

Measuring the physical tool-face angle independently would remove the principal
interpretation limit. Alignment is inferred from the end-effector pose through
a transform assumed rigid, which leaves the approximately \(2^\circ\) of play
about \(y_{\mathrm{EE}}\) unobservable. Either a rigid mount or an external
angular measurement of the face would let the \(t_2\) results be reported as
values rather than as lower bounds.

A matched null-space comparison remains outstanding, and it is the one piece of
this work that is implemented but untested. Trials enabling and disabling the
singular-value bias under otherwise identical contact conditions should report
\(\Delta\sigma_{\min}\), projected joint velocity, Cartesian task drift,
projector residual, joint motion, and sigma torque. The probe configurations
want checking against the joint limits at the same time, and callback
wall-clock duration wants instrumenting, so that worst-case real-time behaviour
is established rather than inferred from the recorded spacing.

Set-up duration deserves confirmation at \(4.5\), \(5\), \(6\) and
\(8\,\mathrm{s}\), with repeated trials at \(5\,\mathrm{s}\). A phase
transition worth generalising would wait on completion of the commanded press
ramp followed by a short interval of low alignment and load rates, keeping a
calculated timeout only as the fallback.

Beyond that, a dedicated force/torque sensor or independent contact
instrumentation would sharpen the normal-load estimate and support a more
defensible analysis of distributed contact and equivalent contact location.
Additional tools, surface materials, fixture conditions and initial orientation
errors would begin to establish how far any of this generalises.
