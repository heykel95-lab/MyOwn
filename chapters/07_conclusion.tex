\chapter{Conclusion and Future Work}
\label{ch:conclusion}

\section{Conclusion}
\label{sec:conclusion_summary}

A real-time Cartesian impedance controller was implemented on a
seven-degree-of-freedom Franka Emika Panda to generate compliant
contact-induced rotation towards the configured surface. The controller assigns
directional translational and rotational stiffness and damping in the surface
frame and allows the virtual centre of compliance to be displaced from the TCP.

Shifting the \abbr{CoC} transforms the Cartesian stiffness and damping matrices
and introduces off-diagonal coupling terms between translation and rotation. A
translational position error can therefore contribute to the commanded moment,
while a rotational error can contribute to the commanded force. The shifted
reference also gives the commanded press the virtual lever arm
\mbox{\(r_c\)}, which generates the additional moment
\mbox{\(r_c\times f\)} at the TCP.

In the contact measurements, changing the \abbr{CoC} position had little effect
on the commanded normal force but changed the commanded TCP moment strongly,
including its direction. The contact response
\mbox{\(\gamma_{t_1}\)} changed with this moment, indicating that the
\abbr{CoC} mainly influenced the response through the additional moment at the
TCP.

With the \abbr{CoC} at the TCP, contact-induced rotation occurred towards the
configured surface for both initial rotational directions. The response reached
\mbox{\(81.3\,\%\)} and \mbox{\(115.1\,\%\)} of the respective initial
angular-offset magnitudes. The TCP-centred condition therefore showed an almost
symmetrical rotational behaviour with respect to the two initial directions,
although the measured response magnitudes were different. Since
\mbox{\(r_c=0\)}, no additional CoC-induced moment favours either direction.
The TCP-centred \abbr{CoC} is therefore suitable as the final configuration
after alignment because it removes the directional preference introduced by a
displaced \abbr{CoC} and allows compliant rotation in both directions.

The rotational stiffness strongly influenced the alignment response. Increasing
\mbox{\(K_{R,t_1}\)} from \mbox{\(5\)} to \mbox{\(50\,\mathrm{N\,m/rad}\)}
reduced the measured response by \mbox{\(63.9\,\%\)}, showing that increasing
the stiffness about the rotation axis increasingly resists the contact-induced
rotation required for alignment. In comparison, varying the translational
stiffness along \mbox{\(t_2\)} produced a response span equal to only
\mbox{\(0.4\,\%\)} of the TCP-centred reference. Within the investigated range,
the alignment response was therefore considerably more sensitive to rotational
stiffness than to translational stiffness along the surface.

The tangential \abbr{CoC} displacement produced a strongly direction-dependent
response. A displaced \abbr{CoC} favoured alignment for one direction of the
initial angular offset while opposing it for the other. Selecting the
displacement direction according to the initial angular-offset direction
increased the final response only slightly, by approximately
\mbox{\(4\,\%\)}, while the measured time history also indicated a faster
rotation towards alignment. Selecting the opposite displacement direction
reduced the response by approximately \mbox{\(98\,\%\)} to
\mbox{\(99\,\%\)}. The main effect of a displaced \abbr{CoC} was therefore to
select the preferred rotational direction, while a suitably selected
displacement could additionally accelerate the alignment response. An adaptive
\abbr{CoC} strategy can therefore select a temporary tangential \abbr{CoC}
displacement according to the initial angular-offset direction and return the
\abbr{CoC} to the TCP once alignment has been established.

The secondary null-space functions were evaluated separately while the
Cartesian pose was held. Projected damping reduced the cumulative projected
null-space motion \mbox{\(E_N\)} from \mbox{\(7.596^\circ\)} to
\mbox{\(5.687^\circ\)}, while the net displacement decreased from
\mbox{\(7.517^\circ\)} to \mbox{\(5.609^\circ\)}. The cumulative motion
and net displacement were nearly equal, meaning that only a small part of the
measured motion was cancelled by motion in the opposite direction. Projected
damping therefore reduced the null-space motion generated by the disturbance.

Singular-value conditioning almost completely suppressed the net displacement
associated with the virtual disturbance under the tested direction. At
\mbox{\(k_\sigma=1.5\,\mathrm{N\,m}\)}, the absolute net displacement was
\mbox{\(0.015^\circ\)}. At
\mbox{\(k_\sigma=2.0\,\mathrm{N\,m}\)}, the measured net displacement was
negative, so its absolute value was \mbox{\(0.006^\circ\)}. The higher
conditioning torque therefore produced a slightly smaller absolute final
displacement. However, the cumulative motion increased from
\mbox{\(0.283^\circ\)} to \mbox{\(1.619^\circ\)}. Since the final net
displacement remained close to zero while the cumulative motion increased
strongly, the higher \mbox{\(k_\sigma\)} produced considerably more
back-and-forth null-space motion around a similar final configuration.

Both conditioning settings maintained the Cartesian position within the
\mbox{\(2\,\mathrm{mm}\)} acceptance limit. The mean maximum position errors
were \mbox{\(\lVert e_p\rVert_{\max}=0.889\,\mathrm{mm}\)} at
\mbox{\(k_\sigma=1.5\,\mathrm{N\,m}\)} and \mbox{\(0.983\,\mathrm{mm}\)} at
\mbox{\(k_\sigma=2.0\,\mathrm{N\,m}\)}. Across the complete pose-hold study,
the largest measured peak position error was \mbox{\(1.304\,\mathrm{mm}\)},
which also remained below the \mbox{\(2\,\mathrm{mm}\)} limit.

\section{Limitations}
\label{sec:limitations}

\subsection{Scope of the Surface-Contact Evaluation}
\label{subsec:conclusion_experimental_range}

The surface-contact study used one Panda configuration, one rectangular tool
and mounting arrangement, one configured surface reference, and one Contact
Establishment trajectory. The measured effects therefore apply to this
experimental geometry and to the investigated ranges of rotational stiffness,
translational stiffness, initial angular offset, and \abbr{CoC} displacement.

The main repeatable comparison concerned rotation about \mbox{\(t_1\)}.
Measurements about \mbox{\(t_2\)} showed greater variability and did not
provide the same repeatable response. Each contact trial used one selected
\abbr{CoC} displacement throughout Contact Establishment; an adaptive change of
the \abbr{CoC} during alignment was not evaluated. The quantitative evaluation
focused on Contact Establishment and did not include the subsequent grinding
motion.

\subsection{Calibration and Measurement Scope}
\label{subsec:conclusion_measurement_uncertainty}

The initial angular offset \mbox{\(\theta_{\mathrm{init},t_1}\)} was calculated from the
measured end-effector orientation and the calibrated tool normal relative to
the configured surface reference. The contact response
\mbox{\(\gamma_{t_1}\)} was calculated from the measured end-effector
orientations at the beginning and end of Contact Establishment. Both quantities
therefore include uncertainty from the robot pose measurement, the tool-normal
calibration, and the configured surface reference.

The physical tool--surface angle was not measured directly during contact. The
reported angular quantities therefore describe the end-effector response
relative to the configured surface reference. Nevertheless, repeatable rotation
towards the configured surface was observed without direct measurement of the
physical contact angle. The physical pressure centre and instantaneous contact
location were also not measured, so their contribution to the contact
interaction could not be evaluated directly.

\subsection{Tool-Mount Compliance}
\label{subsec:conclusion_tool_mount}

The tool mount exhibited approximately \mbox{\(\pm2^\circ\)} of unloaded
angular play about \mbox{\(y_{\mathrm{EE}}\)}, which is approximately aligned
with \mbox{\(t_2\)} in the flat configuration. This mechanical play may have
contributed to the greater variability of the \mbox{\(t_2\)} measurements.

The reported response \mbox{\(\gamma_{t_1}\)} is calculated from the measured
end-effector rotation. Because the tool can move slightly relative to the end
effector, the physical tool-face rotation may differ from the measured
end-effector rotation.

\subsection{Scope of the Null-Space Evaluation}
\label{subsec:conclusion_nullspace_scope}

Projected damping and singular-value conditioning were evaluated separately in
Cartesian pose hold under one virtual disturbance direction and one robot
configuration. The measured results therefore describe the controller behaviour
for the investigated condition rather than under arbitrary disturbances.

\section{Future Work}
\label{sec:future}

Future work should extend the surface-contact evaluation with a direct
measurement of the physical tool--surface angle during contact. This would
allow the measured end-effector response \mbox{\(\gamma_{t_1}\)} to be compared
directly with the physical tool--surface angle relative to
\mbox{\(n_{\mathrm{phys}}\)}, and would separate the measured end-effector
rotation from motion within the tool mount. A more rigid tool mounting
arrangement would also improve the evaluation about \mbox{\(t_2\)}, where the
present measurements showed greater variability.

The \abbr{CoC} results motivate an adaptive \abbr{CoC} strategy. The controller
could begin with the \abbr{CoC} at the TCP, determine the direction of the
initial angular offset, and temporarily apply a tangential \abbr{CoC}
displacement that supports the required rotation. After alignment, the
\abbr{CoC} could return to the TCP to remove the directional preference and
retain compliant behaviour for both rotational directions. This strategy should
be evaluated for rotations about \mbox{\(t_1\)}, \mbox{\(t_2\)}, and additional
directions in the surface plane.

Projected damping and singular-value conditioning should also be evaluated
together under a physical disturbance. Further variation of
\mbox{\(d_{\mathrm{null}}\)} and \mbox{\(k_\sigma\)} could determine a suitable
balance between reducing null-space motion, improving the robot configuration,
and maintaining the Cartesian position. The conditioning law could also be
modified to reduce the back-and-forth null-space motion observed at the larger
\mbox{\(k_\sigma\)}, while preserving the small final net displacement.
