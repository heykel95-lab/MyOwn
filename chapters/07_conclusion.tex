\chapter{Conclusion and Future Work}
\label{ch:conclusion}

\section{Summary}

This thesis developed and evaluated a Cartesian impedance controller for the Franka Emika Panda.
It combined forward kinematics, differential kinematics, Jacobian-based force and moment mapping, and Cartesian impedance control with a C++ implementation using libfranka and Eigen.
The controller computes Cartesian forces and moments from position, orientation, linear velocity, and angular velocity errors, and maps them to joint torques using the transpose of the geometric Jacobian.
The Coriolis term is added from the libfranka model interface, while gravity compensation is handled by the Franka Control Interface for the external torque command.
For the redundant 7-\abbr{DOF} arm, a projected null-space term adds joint-space damping and a smallest-singular-value optimization, so that secondary joint motion does not disturb the regulated Cartesian task.

The theoretical part showed how the end-effector pose is represented by a homogeneous transformation matrix and how the position and orientation errors are defined.
The Denavit--Hartenberg convention was introduced to explain the structure of forward kinematics, while the implementation itself uses the measured end-effector transformation and Jacobian provided directly by libfranka.
The stiffness and damping matrices were defined in the end-effector frame and transformed into the base frame, allowing direction-dependent impedance behavior.
The initial damping values were selected from a critical-damping approximation
and then treated as controller parameters that must be checked experimentally.
A Lyapunov-based stability analysis was also presented for the ideal compensated closed-loop system, showing that symmetric positive definite stiffness and damping matrices lead to a non-increasing energy function.

The software implementation was organized around the real-time torque-control callback of libfranka.
At each \(1\,\mathrm{ms}\) control cycle, the robot state is read, the Jacobian and Coriolis term are obtained, the Cartesian impedance law is evaluated, and the final torque command is limited before being returned to the robot.
The implementation also logs the relevant measured and computed quantities to CSV files.
These logged values include the end-effector position, desired position, positional error, rotational error, Cartesian velocity, Cartesian force and moment, and commanded joint torques.

The current experimental focus is a surface-contact alignment task. In this
experiment, a tool edge contacts a surface and the external contact wrench
produces both force and moment components. The tool is deliberately started
with a small orientation error relative to the surface so that the experiment
can test whether contact reduces this misalignment passively. Because the robot
is controlled by Cartesian impedance, the contact wrench can create a small
translational and rotational displacement. The thesis proposes to evaluate this
displacement as a screw motion and to use the corresponding instantaneous screw
axis, or the closest point of that axis to the tool edge, as a geometric
description of the passive alignment behavior.
The initial stiffness values are computed from a quasi-static compliance
relation between allowable contact force or moment and allowable displacement
or rotation. The damping values are initialized from a critical-damping
approximation and then checked experimentally for smooth, non-oscillatory
contact behavior.
In addition, the evaluation includes a least-squares identification method for
computing effective translation--rotation stiffness and damping matrices from
the logged contact motion and the estimated contact moment. This gives a
second description of the same alignment behavior: the screw-axis analysis
describes where the motion appears to pivot, while the fitted effective
matrices describe which translational, rotational, and damping terms
contribute to the alignment moment.

Overall, the implemented controller provides the basis for investigating the
main design question of the thesis: how the Cartesian stiffness matrix should
be selected so that contact produces passive self-alignment around a useful
virtual center of rotation. The central trade-off remains the practical
trade-off of impedance control: higher stiffness improves pose regulation but
increases contact forces and commanded torques, while lower stiffness improves
compliance but allows larger displacement. For the selected experiment, the
additional requirement is that the compliance should be located geometrically
in a useful place, not only made softer in an arbitrary Cartesian direction.
This connects the controller implementation to the same design problem treated
in impedance-planning and compliance-center assembly work: the useful gain
setting is the one that combines accuracy, safety, damping, and favorable
contact geometry \citep{Pollayil2023ImpedancePlanning,Wang2019VariableComplianceCenter}.

\section{Main Contributions}
\label{sec:main_contributions}

The main contributions of this thesis are:
\begin{itemize}
    \item a reproducible real-time Cartesian impedance controller for the Franka
    Emika Panda, implemented in C++ with libfranka and Eigen, using
    Jacobian-transpose torque mapping and model-based Coriolis compensation;
    \item a surface-frame control mode that aligns the stiffness and damping with
    an arbitrary contact plane and projects the desired position onto that plane;
    \item null-space damping and a smallest-singular-value optimization for the
    redundant 7-\abbr{DOF} arm;
    \item a passive self-alignment formulation in which the contact moment at the
    offset tool edge rotates the tool toward the surface under compliant
    rotational stiffness, evaluated geometrically through the instantaneous screw
    axis (Chasles' theorem);
    \item a two-part gain-selection methodology combining a quasi-static
    stiffness and damping design with an offline least-squares identification of
    the effective translation--rotation impedance;
    \item a memory-buffered data-logging scheme and an offline analysis pipeline
    for evaluating the contact experiments.
\end{itemize}

\section{Limitations}
\label{sec:limitations}

Several limitations of the work presented in this thesis remain.
First, the evaluation is based on logged robot states and commanded torques.
No dedicated external force/torque sensor is used; the controller relies on the
robot's model-based external wrench estimate \texttt{O\_F\_ext\_hat\_K} for
contact detection, so the surface-contact wrench is estimated rather than
measured directly. The passive alignment behavior is therefore evaluated from
the resulting displacement, the computed impedance wrench, the commanded
torques, and this estimated external wrench rather than from a directly
measured force-displacement relation. A dedicated force/torque sensor or more
elaborate sensorless force and environment-stiffness estimation could reduce
this limitation, but it would require an additional measurement or estimation
layer beyond the implemented controller \citep{Roveda2021SensorlessStiffness}.
For the same reason, the least-squares stiffness and damping matrices should
be interpreted as effective contact models derived from the available logged
signals, not as direct measurements of the physical surface or tool stiffness.

Second, the compensation terms are obtained from the libfranka model interface and depend on the nominal robot model.
Model errors, payload uncertainty, friction, and unmodeled dynamics can influence the measured response.
These effects are especially relevant at low stiffness values, where friction and small disturbances can have a stronger influence on the final position error.

Third, the stability analysis assumes ideal gravity and Coriolis compensation, continuous-time behavior, a constant desired pose, symmetric positive definite stiffness and damping matrices, and no torque saturation.
The real implementation is discrete, torque-limited, and affected by measurement noise and model uncertainty.
For this reason, the stability analysis should be interpreted as an ideal reference case, while the experimental plots are needed to evaluate the practical closed-loop behavior.
Energy-aware impedance-control approaches address this issue more directly by
monitoring and limiting energy or power flow during contact, which is a useful
future extension for safer self-alignment experiments
\citep{Hjorth2023EnergyAwareImpedance}.

Finally, the orientation controller is based on a local rotation-vector error.
This representation is suitable for the tested motions, but rotations close to \(180^\circ\) require special numerical handling.
The controller should therefore be tested carefully before applying it to tasks with very large orientation errors.
The virtual-center formulation is presently treated as a passive gain-design
direction, not as an additional active rotation command. A fully selectable
virtual center of rotation above, below, or on the surface is not yet implemented
as a mature coupled gain construction. Thus, the implemented controller can
test passive alignment from an intentionally tilted start pose with diagonal
Cartesian gains, but complete virtual-center behavior would require the
structured translation--rotation coupling to be included directly in the
stiffness and damping matrices used by the real-time impedance law.

\section{Future Work}
\label{sec:future}

The controller and evaluation can be extended in several directions.
An external force/torque sensor could be added to measure interaction forces directly.
This would allow the compliance behavior to be evaluated using measured force-displacement curves and would make it possible to study contact tasks more accurately.

The implemented null-space control can be extended further.
Since the Franka Emika Panda has seven joints, one redundant degree of freedom remains for a six-dimensional Cartesian task.
At present this redundancy is used for joint-space damping and a smallest-singular-value optimization; it could additionally be used for joint-limit avoidance, posture optimization, or keeping the robot away from singular configurations while maintaining the desired end-effector behavior.

Another possible extension is adaptive impedance control.
Instead of selecting fixed stiffness and damping values before each experiment,
the controller could adjust these parameters online depending on the task
phase, contact error, or estimated external interaction.
This would allow the robot to be stiff during accurate positioning and softer
during surface contact or human interaction.

The surface-contact experiment could be extended with an explicit
virtual-center gain construction. In such a passive impedance design, the user
could choose whether the effective rotation point lies on the surface, above the
surface, or below the surface, and this choice would define coupled stiffness and
damping matrices through the lever arm to that point. The impedance law would
still compute its force and moment from the same spring--damper relation; the
difference would be the structured off-diagonal coupling in \(K\) and \(D\). For
a known surface, the required information would be a point on the surface and
the surface normal. If the surface were unknown before contact, an additional
surface-estimation method would be required before the same self-aligning
impedance formulation could be used.

The experimental evaluation could also be expanded with repeated surface-contact
trials, different tool-edge geometries, different virtual-center locations,
larger initial angular errors, and different payloads. This would make it
possible to quantify repeatability and robustness more precisely.
Finally, further refining the operational-space inertia model and its use in
damping selection could improve the dynamic behavior during contact approach,
contact release, and faster tool motions near the surface.
