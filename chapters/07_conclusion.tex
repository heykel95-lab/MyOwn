\section{Conclusion}
\label{sec:conclusion_summary}

\Revised{A real-time Cartesian impedance controller was implemented for the
contact-induced alignment of a grinding tool with a plane surface. During the
set-up phase, the Cartesian stiffness and damping were spatially shifted from
a selected centre of compliance to the TCP. This shift introduced
translation--rotation coupling. The normal interaction load could therefore
generate a moment about the surface tangents.}

\Revised{The point-shift adjoint and congruence transformation are established
theory. The implementation contribution is their phase-dependent integration
into the real-time contact controller.}

\Revised{The controller was evaluated in eight surface-contact cases covering
commanded tool orientation offsets, directional stiffness, and the position and magnitude of the
compliance-centre lever. Complementary Cartesian pose-hold trials quantified
the projected null-space damping and singular-value-conditioning terms. The
zero-orientation-offset baseline produced an alignment change of
\mbox{\(-0.97^\circ\)}. The nonzero-offset responses were therefore interpreted with
the baseline retained as a separate measurement.}

\Revised{The stiffness variations produced smaller changes than moving the
virtual centre of compliance. Raising the rotational stiffness about the commanded
rotation axis reduced the measured alignment improvement. Reducing the perpendicular
translational stiffness increased the response about \mbox{\(t_2\)} only. The
combined stiffness result was non-additive for the selected settings.}

\Revised{Within the investigated range, the compliance-centre position had the
largest measured influence. For each principal surface tangent, one tangential
lever direction increased the alignment improvement and the opposite direction
produced a response near the zero-lever range. Reversing the commanded tool
orientation offset
also reversed the assisting lever direction. The lever must therefore be
selected from both the signed commanded rotation axis and the required
direction of the induced moment.}

\Revised{With \mbox{\(r_c=p_{\mathrm{TCP}}-p_c\)} and
\mbox{\(m_c=-r_c\times f\)}, the experimentally supported selection rule is
\mbox{\(r_{c,t}=-\rho_c n_s\times u_{\mathrm{rot}}\)}, with
\mbox{\(\rho_c>0\)}. The assisting tangential lever is therefore perpendicular
to the signed commanded rotation axis and reverses with the commanded rotation
direction.}

\Revised{The direction rule was supported for four commanded rotation directions in
the tangent plane. A lever selected perpendicular to the signed commanded
rotation axis assisted
alignment at every tested direction, whereas holding one lever fixed reduced
the response in the matched comparisons. The lever magnitude giving the largest
improvement depended on the commanded tool orientation offset. In three
positive-offset settings,
the improvement decreased after the signed residual crossed the surface
orientation. In the remaining setting, the response was still increasing at
the largest tested magnitude, so its largest measured improvement occurred at
the boundary of the tested range.}

\Revised{Displacement of the compliance centre along the surface normal had a
smaller measured effect than the tangential lever. Orientation offsets commanded about the
grinding-face axes produced responses between the two principal surface-tangent
responses. This agreement is consistent with the surface-frame interpretation
of the directional asymmetry. The result remains bounded by the relative motion
between the tool and its mount.}

\Revised{In the matched Cartesian pose-hold trials, projected null-space damping
reduced the cumulative redundant joint excursion by approximately
\mbox{\(25\,\%\)}. Both singular-value-conditioning settings returned the
minimum singular value to approximately its initial level after the commanded
disturbance. These findings apply to the isolated free-space hold conditions
tested.}

\Revised{The experiments therefore support the use of a point-shifted Cartesian
impedance to convert the normal interaction into an alignment moment without
an explicit rotational trajectory during set-up. The measured results provide
a geometric rule for selecting the lever direction. Its magnitude remains a
condition-dependent design parameter rather than a universal setting.}


\section{Limitations}
\label{sec:limitations}

\subsection{Experimental Range}
\label{subsec:conclusion_experimental_range}

\Revised{The contact experiments used one Panda configuration, tool mount, plane
surface, press trajectory, and bounded parameter range. The direction-and-sign
rule was reproduced on both principal surface tangents and supported at four
in-plane directions. The measured lever magnitudes and stiffness effects remain
specific to the investigated geometry and tested settings.}

\subsection{Measurement and Tool-Mount Uncertainty}
\label{subsec:conclusion_measurement_uncertainty}

\Revised{The alignment metric reconstructs the grinding-face direction from the
end-effector pose and a calibrated tool direction. It therefore describes the
combined robot--gripper--tool response rather than a separately measured
grinding-face orientation. Relative motion within the tool mount bounds the
reported alignment changes, particularly the \mbox{\(t_2\)} results.}

\Revised{The supporting external wrench was supplied by the bias-corrected
libfranka model estimate. It was not measured by an independent contact-load
sensor. The reported force and moment comparisons are consequently limited to
this common model-based measurement chain.}


\subsection{Null-Space Configuration and Real-Time Operation}
\label{subsec:conclusion_nullspace_scope}

\Revised{Projected damping was the active null-space configuration during the
surface-contact experiments. The damping and singular-value terms were isolated
only in free-space pose hold under an internally commanded point-force
equivalent. Their combined mode, response to a physical disturbance, and effect
during contact were not evaluated.}


\section{Future Work}
\label{sec:future}

\Revised{Three extensions to the compliance-centre study are considered most
important. Finer lever-magnitude sampling would extend the measured response
around and beyond the best tested regions. A finer angular sweep, for example in
\mbox{\(15^\circ\)} or \mbox{\(30^\circ\)} increments, should then be combined
with magnitude variation at each direction. Case~H already samples four
directions, including two diagonals between the surface-axis cases; the next
step is therefore a denser joint characterization of direction and magnitude,
not merely the addition of intermediate directions.}

\Revised{An angular measurement of the grinding face taken independently of the
end-effector pose would separate tool rotation from motion within the mount. A
more rigid tool mount would further reduce this relative motion. This bears
particularly on the reversed \mbox{\(t_1\)} result and on the magnitude of the
reported alignment changes. An independent force/torque sensor would
additionally allow the model-estimated external wrench to be compared with a
direct contact-load measurement. Further contact geometries, tool faces, and
press trajectories would establish how far the selection rule extends beyond
the investigated arrangement.}

\Revised{The null-space study can be extended with a physical disturbance applied to
an intermediate robot link and with the combined damping and
singular-value-conditioning mode. A corresponding surface-interaction
comparison would show whether the pose-hold ordering is preserved during
contact.}

\Revised{Finally, the compliance-centre selection can be incorporated into the
controller as a geometry-based scheduling step. After the initial tilt is
estimated at the clearance transition, the lever direction could be selected
so that the induced moment opposes the measured angular error. Such a strategy
would replace a lever fixed in advance with a run-specific placement while
continuing to use the point-shifted Cartesian impedance implemented in this
work.}
