\chapter{Conclusion and Future Work}
\label{ch:conclusion}

A tool tilted against a plane cannot be brought flat by translational compliance
alone. A real-time Cartesian impedance controller with a spatially shifted
centre of compliance was therefore implemented on the 7-\abbr{DOF} Panda. During
set-up, the \(6\times6\) Cartesian stiffness and damping were transformed from a
selected centre of compliance to the TCP, so that the normal contact force also
generated a corrective moment. All reported contact runs completed the set-up
phase, and none was discarded. In the zero-tilt condition the measured
misalignment increased by \(0.97^\circ\) during set-up, so the set-up procedure
itself introduced a systematic orientation change. That response was not
subtracted from the tilted results, which were interpreted alongside it.

Within the tested range, the in-plane compliance-centre position had the largest
influence on contact-induced alignment. For each tilted axis one lever direction
improved alignment and the opposite direction did not, and the improving
direction for a tilt about \(t_1\) was the opposite of the improving direction
for a tilt about \(t_2\). When the lever was applied in the other direction,
almost no alignment improvement was measured. Placing the compliance centre near
the grinding-face centre, a predominantly normal displacement, produced results
within the interpretation threshold of the zero-lever condition. Increased
rotational stiffness reduced the response about \(t_1\). For \(t_2\), the
highest stiffness produced less alignment than the softer settings, but the
relationship between the two softer settings remained uncertain because of one
unusually low repetition. Reduced perpendicular translational stiffness improved
the \(t_2\) response, whereas no clear effect was measured for \(t_1\).
\Cref{ch:results_discussion} reports the corresponding measurements.

Commanding the tilt about the axes of the grinding face rather than about the
surface tangents produced values between the two measured tangent responses,
close to those predicted from the corresponding \(t_1\) and \(t_2\) components.
For the investigated configuration, this is more consistent with an asymmetry
associated with the surface frame than with the geometry of the face.

That sign dependence is a property of the mechanism and not of the particular
placements tested. The rotational spring is driven by the measured orientation
error and therefore reduces a misalignment of either sign, whereas the moment
induced by the lever keeps a direction fixed by the lever alone, because the
press is directed into the surface throughout set-up. A tangential lever is
consequently a bias rather than a feedback term: it assists one direction of
tilt and opposes the other. Placements that induce no moment, at the TCP or
along the tool axis, are symmetric in the tilt direction but recovered an order
of magnitude less angle. A correction that is both large and independent of the
tilt direction therefore cannot come from a fixed compliance centre; the lever
direction has to be selected from the sign of the measured initial tilt. This
follows from the point-shifted law and from the measured loss of correction at
the unfavourable placement, and it remains untested against a negative tilt.

The measured ordering indicates that the compliance-centre position should be
selected before the directional stiffness values are refined. The generality of
that ordering is limited by the single experimental arrangement and by unmeasured
rotational play between the tool and the gripper, which may cause the reported
\(t_2\) improvements to underestimate the physical rotation of the grinding
face. The SVD-based null-space controller was implemented and instrumented,
including the distinction between the smallest Jacobian singular value
\(\sigma_{\min}=\sigma_6\) and the structural null direction \(v_7\), but no
controlled comparison with the singular-value term enabled and disabled was
recorded, so improved singularity conditioning is not an experimental conclusion
of this thesis.

\label{sec:main_contributions}
Against the two kinds of contribution set out in \cref{ch:intro}, the work
delivers the following. Implemented and verified against the running controller:
a real-time Cartesian impedance controller for the 7-\abbr{DOF} Panda with
frame-consistent phase-dependent gains; a spatial compliance-centre formulation
within that controller, in which the \(6\times6\) stiffness and damping are
shifted from a selected centre to the TCP; and a calibrated contact methodology,
in which the surface plane and the grinding-face axis are measured independently
of each other. Established experimentally: an axis-specific measurement campaign,
which separates the \(t_1\) and \(t_2\) responses that a common paired stiffness
setting leaves confounded; and identification of the compliance-centre position
as the parameter with the largest measured influence on contact-induced
alignment within the tested range. The projected joint damping and the two-point
singular-value bias were implemented and instrumented but not experimentally
isolated, and are therefore not claimed as validated contributions.

\label{sec:limitations}
Several limitations bound those conclusions.
\Cref{sec:results_limitations} treats the ones that bear directly on the
measurements; they are summarised here together with those that concern the
implementation. The experiments were conducted with a single robot, tool
geometry, and plane surface. Generalisation to other hardware, surface
conditions, contact locations, and initial angular errors was not assessed, and
exact replication is limited further by the unrecorded payload parameters, robot
transforms, and real-time computer.

Calibration and load estimation carry their own uncertainty. The surface plane
and the grinding-face normal were calibrated with the same robot and tool whose
alignment is then measured, so a systematic error in the tool transform would
largely cancel in the comparison intended to detect it. No dedicated
force/torque sensor was fitted, and the reported load is a model-estimated
quantity rather than a measured one. Approximately \(\pm2^\circ\) of rotational
play about \(y_{\mathrm{EE}}\) also remains in the tool mount and is not
measured by the controller, so the angular results characterise the combined
robot--gripper--tool response. The \(t_2\) condition is the most exposed,
because the commanded spin brings \(y_{\mathrm{EE}}\) within \(25^\circ\) of
\(t_2\). Finally, the null-space terms ran together in every reported run, so
their separate influence on the contact response was not established; the
projector is kinematic rather than dynamically consistent, the geometric
Jacobian is unscaled, and although the recorded callback spacing supports
sustained operation near the nominal \(1\,\mathrm{kHz}\) interface rate, the
worst-case local callback execution time was never measured.

\label{sec:future}
Three extensions follow from those limitations and are considered most
important. First, the lever experiments should be repeated with negative
commanded tilts, to determine whether the improving lever direction depends on
the tilted axis or on the direction of the initial error; every case reported
here used a positive tilt, so the two explanations cannot be separated from the
present data. The same trials would test the bias character of the lever
directly, by measuring whether a placement that assists a positive tilt opposes
a negative one, and would establish whether selecting the lever direction from
the sign of the measured initial tilt recovers the full correction in both
directions. Because a reversed tilt also brings a different corner of the
rectangular face into contact first, the magnitudes need not mirror those
reported here. Second, the physical tool-face orientation should be measured
independently, so that motion within the mount can be separated from robot
motion. Either a rigid mount or an external angular measurement of the face
would determine whether the present EE-inferred \(t_2\) values underestimate the
actual alignment of the grinding face. Third, matched trials with the
singular-value term enabled and disabled should be performed under otherwise
identical contact conditions, reporting \(\Delta\sigma_{\min}\), projected joint
velocity, Cartesian task drift, and projector residual, since that term was
implemented but not experimentally isolated.

The compliance-centre placement itself was sampled in only one of its three
coordinates. A signed sweep was run along the tangent perpendicular to the
tilted axis; the tangent parallel to it was never varied, and the surface normal
was sampled at a single displacement, in one sense, and only in combination with
a small tangential component. A two-dimensional sweep in \(t_1\) and \(t_2\)
would identify an optimum and any interaction between the in-plane coordinates,
and displacements along the normal in both senses would establish
experimentally what \cref{sec:results_pole} argues from the point-shifted law:
that the normal coordinate cannot generate the alignment moment while the press
remains normal to the surface.

A further trial would test that axial relation directly rather than at a single
point. Holding the centre at its face-centre displacement and commanding tilts
of \(5^\circ\), \(10^\circ\) and \(20^\circ\) varies the effective tangential
lever \(d\sin\theta\) from roughly \(2\) to \(7\,\mathrm{mm}\) while the
placement itself is unchanged, so a response that grows with \(\sin\theta\)
would confirm the relation and one that does not would show the account to be
incomplete. Each tilt requires a zero-lever run at the same angle as its
control, because the improvement already depends on the commanded angle
(\cref{tab:results_case_a}); the comparison is between the two placements at
each tilt, not across tilts. The tilt should be commanded about \(t_1\). That
puts the induced component along \(t_2\), the tangent that corrects a \(t_1\)
tilt, and it avoids the \(t_2\) condition, whose within-setting dispersion is
larger and which is the one exposed to the rotational play about
\(y_{\mathrm{EE}}\).

Further work should also include a check of the probe configurations against the
joint limits, direct measurement of the callback execution time, and additional
tests of set-up duration and contact instrumentation.
