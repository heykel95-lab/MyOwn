\chapter{Conclusion and Future Work}
\label{ch:conclusion}

\section{Conclusion}
\label{sec:conclusion_summary}

This thesis developed and experimentally evaluated a real-time Cartesian
impedance controller for contact-induced alignment of a grinding tool with a
plane surface. The controller runs in the Franka Control Interface torque loop
and combines surface-relative Cartesian impedance control, a virtual centre of
compliance, and null-space control. During set-up, the stiffness and damping
defined at the selected centre of compliance \(p_c\) are shifted to the TCP.
The resulting translation--rotation coupling allows the commanded normal press
to generate an alignment moment without prescribing an additional rotational
trajectory.

Two matters were investigated experimentally: whether contact itself rotates
the tool towards the surface, and whether one centre of compliance can be fixed
without knowing the sign and the tangent-plane direction of the initial angular
misalignment. The second follows from the first. The coupling that a displaced
centre introduces is directional, while the misalignment it would have to
correct is unknown before contact.

With the centre of compliance at the TCP, contact still rotated the tool.
Setting \(r_c=0\) removes the coupling moment \(m_{c,K}\) alone: the commanded
rotational moment and the tool-geometry lever \(r_T\) remain, so the physical
contact geometry continues to act. For a commanded \(+10^\circ\) orientation
offset the end effector rotated by \(+7.57^\circ\) about \(t_1\), which is
close to the \(+7.87^\circ\) obtained later with the assisting
\(40\,\mathrm{mm}\) lever. This behaviour is consistent with the contact moment
\(m_{r_T}=r_T\times\Delta\hat f_{\mathrm{ext}}\) and the mounting compliance
already supplying that rotation, and the experiments do not separate their
contributions. About \(t_2\), the same command produced \(-1.63^\circ\), so the
TCP-centred condition produced no alignment-directed end-effector rotation
under this metric, although the final configuration satisfied the TCP-height
flatness criterion. The tool mounting exhibited approximately \(\pm2^\circ\) of
relative rotational play about the corresponding mounting direction, so the
\(t_2\) behaviour is consistent with the tool seating against the surface
through the mounting compliance while the end-effector orientation moved
further the other way. Satisfying the flatness criterion and producing an
alignment-directed end-effector rotation are therefore not the same thing, and
the two tangents differ in exactly that respect.

The Cartesian gains changed the size of that response without changing its
axis dependence. Raising the rotational stiffness about the commanded rotation
axis reduced the set-up rotation, most strongly about \(t_1\). The tested
cross-axis translational-stiffness variation had a smaller effect, and the
combined low-translation, high-rotation condition was consistent with the
individual effects acting approximately additively within the descriptive
comparison scale. No tested stiffness combination produced alignment-directed
rotation about \(t_2\).

Moving the virtual centre of compliance produced the largest measured change.
With
\[
r_c = p_{\mathrm{TCP}}-p_c,
\qquad
m_{c,K} = -\,r_c\times f_K,
\]
a tangential displacement converts part of the commanded normal press into an
additional commanded moment. The assisting tangential lever lies perpendicular
to the commanded rotation axis in the surface tangent plane. Its sign is chosen
so that this moment acts in the required alignment direction.
Reversing the commanded rotation direction reverses the assisting lever
direction. About \(t_2\), the selected \(40\,\mathrm{mm}\) lever changed the
signed set-up rotation from \(-1.63^\circ\) to \(+4.43^\circ\). Three further
properties of a displaced centre were measured. The frame in which the lever is
defined is part of the parameter, since a tool-fixed lever rotates with the end
effector while a surface-fixed lever does not. Displacement along the tool axis
changed the set-up rotation by only \(0.34^\circ\) over the tested \(-40\) to
\(+40\,\mathrm{mm}\) range, so the tangential component is what acts. The
response did not scale proportionally with the commanded orientation-offset
magnitude.

The experiments do not identify a universal non-zero compliance-centre
displacement. They show instead why such a displacement cannot be direction
independent: the assisting lever reverses with the sign of the initial angular
deviation, and it changes with the required tangent-plane rotation direction.
Case~H tested the direction-and-sign rule at four tangent-plane directions with
one common \(40\,\mathrm{mm}\) magnitude. The two diagonal
selected-versus-fixed comparisons differed by only \(0.07^\circ\) and
\(0.05^\circ\), which is within the descriptive experimental scatter and
follows from the projection the fixed lever retains along the selected
direction, while the \(t_2\) comparison separated \(-1.61^\circ\) from
\(+4.43^\circ\). Under the definition introduced in \Cref{ch:intro}, the
TCP-centred condition is therefore identified as the universal fixed centre of
compliance for the investigated task, because it requires no prior selection of
misalignment direction or sign.

That identification does not imply that the TCP produces the largest
alignment-directed rotation in every condition. The \(t_2\) result demonstrates
the opposite: a selected \(40\,\mathrm{mm}\) tangential lever changed the
response from \(-1.63^\circ\) to \(+4.43^\circ\), so a displaced centre
supplies substantially greater alignment authority where the required rotation
is known. The direction-independent fixed centre and the centre with the
greatest condition-specific authority are separate things, and the thesis
claims only the first for the TCP.

The same mechanism bears on the phase that follows alignment. The coupling
moment does not vanish when the tool reaches a flat orientation, so a fixed
tangential displacement retains its preferred rotational direction for as long
as the press is present. Returning the centre to the TCP removes that
additional directional coupling, which is also how the implemented phases are
arranged: the point-shifted impedance is used during set-up and the grinding
branch returns to the decoupled impedance. This is a mechanically consistent
design implication rather than an experimental result, since the reported runs
ended at the pre-grinding gate and no displacement was held through sustained
grinding.

The secondary null-space study showed that the redundant joint motion can be
influenced without changing the main Cartesian task. In matched Cartesian
pose-hold trials, projected null-space damping reduced the cumulative projected
null-space motion by approximately \(25\,\%\), and the net redundant
displacement fell in the same proportion. Damping acts on the projected joint
velocity, so it vanishes once that motion stops and requests no return towards
the initial configuration. Singular-value conditioning was active before and
during the disturbance, and both tested torque magnitudes ended the disturbance
interval with a net redundant displacement more than two orders of magnitude
smaller than without null-space torque. Under the tested disturbance direction
it therefore prevented the displacement rather than correcting it afterwards.
The two terms have different roles: the damping term dissipates redundant
motion that is already present, while the conditioning term drives the
redundant configuration towards the sampled neighbour with the larger
\(\sigma_{\min}\) whether or not such motion is present. The conditioning
law is not dissipative, so a disturbance acting towards larger
\(\sigma_{\min}\) would be accompanied by \(\tau_\sigma\) rather than
opposed by it. Both act through the
null-space projector, so the Cartesian task remains primary. Of the two tested
magnitudes the lower one produced the same suppression of net displacement with
substantially less redundant motion, which is the parameter selection these
trials support. These results apply to the pose-hold experiment; the individual
null-space contributions were not separated during surface contact.

The thesis therefore identifies two distinct roles for the centre of
compliance. The TCP-centred condition provides the direction-independent fixed
centre for the investigated task, and it is the placement that can be selected
before the initial misalignment is known. A non-zero tangential displacement is
not a universal centre but a condition-dependent means of adding rotational
alignment authority, and the geometric direction-and-sign rule states how it
should be chosen when it is required. The implemented contribution is built around these two roles: a
phase-consistent real-time Cartesian impedance architecture in which the
displacement is applied during set-up and removed afterwards. Projected damping
and singular-value conditioning manage the redundant degree of freedom without
displacing the primary task. The magnitude of a
selected displacement remains dependent on the tool geometry, the contact
conditions, and the robot configuration.


\section{Limitations}
\label{sec:limitations}

\subsection{Experimental Range}
\label{subsec:conclusion_experimental_range}

The contact study used one Franka Panda configuration, one rectangular tool
and mount, one plane surface, one press trajectory, and a bounded set of
stiffness and compliance-centre values. The direction-and-sign rule was tested
on both principal surface tangents and at two diagonal directions, but the
measured response magnitudes remain specific to the investigated geometry and
parameter range. In particular, the experiments do not determine one lever
magnitude that suits every direction and geometry. The magnitude is bounded in
practice by the joint-torque limits as well as by the desired coupling: an
exploratory tool-axis displacement beyond the reported Case-F range was stopped
by a joint-torque limit, so the centre position has to be selected together
with those limits.

The identification of the TCP as the universal fixed centre of compliance holds
under the definition given in \Cref{ch:intro} and within that same scope. What
the measurements establish is that every non-zero tangential lever tested here
is direction dependent. For this robot, tool, surface, press trajectory and
range of commanded orientation offsets, the TCP-centred condition is therefore
the placement that can be selected before the misalignment is known. They do not
establish that the TCP is preferable for other robots, tools, contact
geometries, surfaces, or grinding processes, and they do not evaluate any
placement during sustained grinding. No quantitative grinding experiment was
performed. The design question that remains is when additional
compliance-centre coupling is required, and when the controller should return
to the TCP-centred condition, rather than which fixed magnitude to keep.

\subsection{Measurement and Tool-Mount Uncertainty}
\label{subsec:conclusion_measurement_uncertainty}

The primary controller-response quantity, the signed set-up rotation, was
obtained from the measured end-effector motion. The supporting alignment angle
\(\theta_{\mathrm{align}}\) was calculated from the end-effector pose and the
calibrated tool normal, and therefore assumes that the calibrated
tool-to-end-effector relation remains fixed. The tool mounting exhibited
approximately \(\pm2^\circ\) of relative rotational play about
\(y_{\mathrm{EE}}\). That compliance was measured in the unloaded condition,
but the instantaneous relative tool--gripper rotation was not tracked
separately during the contact experiments, so the value is a mounting
characteristic rather than an uncertainty bound under contact load. The
TCP-height quantity provides a geometry-based flatness classification rather
than a direct measurement of the physical tool orientation.

The supporting external wrench was obtained from the libfranka model estimate
relative to the value stored at the clearance transition. No independent
force/torque sensor was used. The reported load quantities should therefore be
interpreted as model-based measurements on one consistent evaluation basis.

\subsection{Null-Space Configuration and Real-Time Operation}
\label{subsec:conclusion_nullspace_scope}

Projected damping and singular-value conditioning were active together during
the surface-contact experiments. They were compared separately only in the
Cartesian pose-hold study under an internally commanded disturbance, and that
comparison carries two bounds of its own. The conditioning torque was active
from the start of each pose-hold trial, so the sigma-only trials entered the
disturbance from a redundant configuration displaced by approximately
\(0.013\,\mathrm{rad}\) from the one at which the trials without
conditioning began. The result therefore describes the closed-loop behaviour of
the complete modes rather than an instantaneous torque comparison at identical
joint configurations. The disturbance also acted in one direction, taken from
the null direction at the initial posture, and the conditioning law is not
dissipative, so the observed rejection cannot be assumed for a disturbance
acting towards larger \(\sigma_{\min}\). The experiments demonstrate
operation through the nominal \(1\,\mathrm{kHz}\) Franka control loop, but
they do not constitute a worst-case execution-time or jitter analysis.


\section{Future Work}
\label{sec:future}

The first direction is to test the fixed-centre conclusion beyond the
arrangement used here. Other tool geometries and surface orientations would show whether the TCP
remains the appropriate direction-independent fixed centre when the contact
geometry changes. Other robot configurations, initial angular directions,
larger commanded offsets and other press trajectories would show whether the
axis asymmetry observed between \(t_1\) and \(t_2\) transfers with it. The
lever study belongs to the same direction. The direction rule should be
evaluated over a denser set of tangent-plane angles while the lever magnitude
is varied independently at each direction, which would show how the required
magnitude changes with the commanded orientation and the contact geometry.

The second direction is the direction-selected extension of the controller,
which the present implementation does not contain. The measurements support the
following sequence, and none of its scheduling was implemented or evaluated
here:

\begin{enumerate}[leftmargin=1.1cm]
\item The run begins with the centre of compliance at the TCP, which requires
no knowledge of the initial deviation.
\item The current tool--surface angular deviation is determined at the
clearance transition.
\item The contact response available at the TCP is assessed against the
alignment the task requires.
\item Where that response is insufficient, the direction-selected tangential
lever is calculated from the geometric rule.
\item Its magnitude \(\rho_c\) is scheduled within the geometric and
joint-torque limits.
\item The centre of compliance is returned smoothly to the TCP once the
alignment criterion is satisfied, before sustained contact or grinding.
\end{enumerate}

Such a strategy would keep the alignment authority the shifted centre
demonstrated while avoiding a persistent directional moment afterwards, and it
would replace a compliance-centre lever chosen before the run with a
geometry-based, run-specific selection. Both the deviation estimate it needs and
the criterion for returning to the TCP remain to be defined and measured.

Independent measurement of the physical tool response would strengthen every
one of these results. An external orientation measurement of the tool face
would separate end-effector rotation from relative motion within the tool
mount, and a direct force/torque sensor would allow the model-estimated
external wrench to be compared with an independent contact-load measurement.
The null-space study could similarly be extended to physical disturbances, to
disturbances acting in other redundant directions, and to experiments that
separate damping from singular-value conditioning during surface contact. The
switching activity observed at the larger conditioning magnitude could be
reduced by adjusting the comparison deadband or its hysteresis, or by replacing
the constant-magnitude direction command with a conditioning torque scaled
smoothly by the sampled singular-value difference. Either change would be a new
controller design and would require the pose-hold trials to be repeated.
