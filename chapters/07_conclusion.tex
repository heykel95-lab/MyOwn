\chapter{Conclusion and Future Work}
\label{ch:conclusion}

\section{Summary}

This thesis developed and experimentally evaluated a real-time Cartesian
impedance controller for contact-induced alignment of a rectangular tool on a
planar surface. The controller runs on a 7-\abbr{DOF} Franka Emika Panda and
uses the joint-torque command
\[
  \tau_{\mathrm{cmd}}
  =
  J^\top w_{\mathrm{cmd}}
  +\tau_{\mathrm{null}}
  +c(q,\dot q).
\]
The Cartesian gains are expressed in explicitly defined base and surface
frames. During set-up, a virtual centre of compliance is introduced by shifting
a compliance-centre \(6\times6\) stiffness and damping matrix to the TCP through an
adjoint congruence. The resulting off-diagonal blocks couple translational
error to rotational moment and rotational error to force.

The experimental study varied the commanded tilt, the rotational
stiffness of each surface tangent, the translational stiffness perpendicular to
the tilted tangent, and the virtual compliance-centre lever. The surface
plane and the grinding-face axis were calibrated independently of one another,
so the commanded tilt is defined relative to a measured physical
surface frame. The reported data comprise \(75\) contact runs, all of which
completed their set-up phase and are admissible. Each experimental case is summarised below.

\begin{enumerate}
  \item \textbf{Case A, commanded tilt.} With no commanded
        tilt the set-up press increased the misalignment by
        \(0.97^\circ\), which bounds the alignment accuracy attainable with the
        present fixture and calibration. Alignment improved for every nonzero
        tilt, and by roughly \(1.6\) times as much for a tilt about
        \(t_2\) as about \(t_1\) at both magnitudes. The fraction of the
        initial error removed fell from \(38\,\%\) at \(5^\circ\) about
        \(t_1\) to \(9\,\%\) at \(10^\circ\), so the response is not
        proportional to the initial error over this range.

  \item \textbf{Case B, axis-specific rotational stiffness.} Raising the
        rotational stiffness of the tilted axis reduced alignment. A ten-fold
        increase from \(5\) to \(50\,\mathrm{Nm/rad}\) reduced the improvement
        about \(t_1\) by roughly a quarter, from \(0.64^\circ\) to
        \(0.49^\circ\), and no tested setting improved on the
        \(5\,\mathrm{Nm/rad}\) baseline. Because the two tangent entries were
        varied separately, this is an axis-specific result rather than a
        property of a common paired setting.

  \item \textbf{Case C, cross-axis translational stiffness.} Reducing the
        translational stiffness perpendicular to the tilted axis by a factor
        of about seven improved alignment about \(t_2\) by roughly a tenth,
        from \(1.59^\circ\) to \(1.79^\circ\), and changed the \(t_1\) response
        by less than the interpretation threshold. Combining the reduced
        translational stiffness with \(50\,\mathrm{Nm/rad}\) of rotational
        stiffness removed the benefit, so the two entries do not act
        independently.

  \item \textbf{Case D, virtual centre of compliance.} Lever placement
        dominated every other parameter examined. Moving the lever
        \(60\,\mathrm{mm}\) along the tangent perpendicular to the tilted axis
        increased the improvement by about eleven times for a tilt about
        \(t_1\), from \(0.56^\circ\) to \(6.05^\circ\), and by about five times
        for a tilt about \(t_2\), from \(1.38^\circ\) to \(6.36^\circ\).
        The residual misalignment fell to \(0.93^\circ\) and \(2.14^\circ\)
        respectively, and the steady model-estimated load fell by roughly a
        fifth. The effect is sign-dependent and the favourable sign is opposite
        for the two tangents; the unfavourable sign removed the correction
        rather than reducing it, giving \(0.02\pm0.06^\circ\). Displacing the
        compliance centre \(20\,\mathrm{mm}\) along the surface normal instead
        produced no change beyond the interpretation threshold.

  \item \textbf{Case E, tilt about the tool axes.} Commanding the same
        tilt about the axes of the grinding face rather than the surface
        tangents produced improvements between the two tangent values, and the
        two tool-axis results differed from each other by about half as much as
        the two tangent results did. Both lie close to the linear combination
        of their tangent components, which associates the measured asymmetry
        with the surface frame rather than with the geometry of the face.
\end{enumerate}

The lever result is consistent with the translation to rotation coupling of the
shifted impedance, in which the sign of the lever sets the direction of the
moment produced by the commanded normal penetration. The reduced load at the
favourable placements follows from the same mechanism: a tool that rotates
further into conformity relieves part of the commanded penetration.

The set-up response reached a settled condition within the \(5\,\mathrm{s}\)
interval. Over the final second the model-estimated load changed by a median of
\(0.10\,\mathrm{N}\) and the tip angle by a median of \(0.00^\circ\) across all
\(75\) runs.

The mounted tool retains approximately \(2^\circ\) of rotational play about
\(y_{EE}\), and the commanded spin places that direction \(25^\circ\) from
\(t_2\). For a \(t_2\) tilt the compliant direction of the mount therefore
nearly coincides with the axis being corrected, and motion of the tool relative
to the end-effector is not observable from the robot pose. One repetition
removed \(0.00^\circ\) where its two counterparts removed \(1.56^\circ\) and
\(1.62^\circ\), and is retained as evidence of this limitation. Because such
motion can only conceal correction that occurred, the reported \(t_2\) results
are lower bounds and the measured tangent-axis asymmetry is not an artefact of
the mount.

The SVD-based null-space controller is implemented and instrumented, including
the distinction between the smallest Jacobian singular value
\(\sigma_{\min}=\sigma_6\) and the structural null direction \(v_7\).
However, no controlled sigma-enabled campaign isolates its effect. Improved
singularity conditioning is therefore not an experimental conclusion of this
thesis.

\section{Main Contributions}
\label{sec:main_contributions}

The main contributions are:

\begin{itemize}
  \item A frame-consistent real-time Cartesian impedance controller with a
        spatially shifted \(6\times6\) stiffness and damping representation for
        the 7-\abbr{DOF} Panda.

  \item A repeatable surface-contact methodology in which the surface plane
        and the grinding-face axis are calibrated independently, so that
        commanded tilt, stiffness, and compliance-centre placement can be
        varied one at a time against a measured physical surface frame.

  \item Axis-specific measurement of the rotational and translational stiffness
        contributions to contact-induced alignment, separating the \(t_1\) and
        \(t_2\) responses that a common paired setting cannot distinguish.

  \item Identification of the compliance-centre lever as the dominant parameter
        for contact-induced alignment, including its sign dependence, the
        reversal of the favourable sign between the two surface tangents, and
        the absence of a comparable effect along the surface normal.
\end{itemize}

\section{Limitations}
\label{sec:limitations}

The measurements were obtained with one robot, one primary tool geometry, one
wooden surface, and one fixture arrangement. The extent to which the
identified response transfers to other tools, surfaces, contact locations,
friction conditions, and initial angular errors is unknown. The exact material
grades, surface finish, active payload parameters, robot transforms, and
real-time-computer hardware were not recorded completely.

The surface plane was fitted from three probed points and checked against a
fourth held-out point lying \(0.82\,\mathrm{mm}\) from the fitted surface, and
the grinding-face normal was calibrated separately with a held-out angular
residual of \(0.50^\circ\). Absolute residual angles inherit these
uncertainties. The probing used the same robot and tool whose alignment is
being measured, so a systematic error in the tool transform would affect the
plane estimate and the alignment metric together.

No dedicated force/torque sensor was used. The reported load and equivalent
contact location are inferred from libfranka's model-estimated external
wrench. Distributed surface contact, model error, and
uncertainty in the selected tool feature prevent treating the inferred
location as an exact physical point contact.

The compliance-centre study used three tangential levels on one axis at a time.
This locates the favourable sign and establishes the magnitude of the effect,
but it does not identify an optimum, does not resolve any interaction between
the two in-plane coordinates, and samples the surface normal at a single
displacement.

The commanded tilt was positive in every case. Any asymmetry between
positive and negative tilt, and therefore the generality of the favourable
lever sign, is unmeasured.

The mounted tool retains approximately \(\pm2^\circ\) of rotational play about
\(y_{\mathrm{EE}}\). The controller reconstructs the tool axis from the EE pose
and a fixed calibrated transform and does not measure this relative motion, so
a tool face that remains physically flat can coexist with a changing
EE-inferred alignment angle. The angular results characterise the combined
robot--gripper--tool response and do not isolate physical tool-face rotation
from gripper compliance. The play direction is not labelled simply as \(t_2\),
because \(y_{\mathrm{EE}}\) is EE-fixed whereas \(t_2\) is defined by the
calibrated plane in the base frame; the commanded spin places the two within
\(25^\circ\) of one another, which is why a tilt about \(t_2\) is the
condition most exposed to it.

The active experiment configuration combines projected joint damping with the
two-point singular-value bias. Without matched null-space-enabled and
null-space-disabled trials, its influence on the contact response cannot be
separated. The geometric Jacobian is unscaled, and the implemented projector
is kinematic rather than dynamically consistent. The recorded callback spacing
supports sustained operation near the nominal \(1\,\mathrm{kHz}\) interface
rate, but worst-case local callback execution time was not measured.

\section{Future Work}
\label{sec:future}

The favourable lever sign should be tested against a reversed commanded
tilt. Every case used a positive tilt, so it is not established whether
the favourable sign follows the tilted axis or the direction of the initial
error. Repeating the Case-D levels at \(-5^\circ\) and \(-10^\circ\) about each
tangent would separate the two.

The compliance-centre response should then be sampled in two dimensions rather
than along one axis at a time. A local grid in \(t_1\) and \(t_2\) around the
favourable placements would identify an optimum and any interaction between the
coordinates, neither of which the present three-level sweep can resolve.
Additional normal displacements would establish whether the absence of a normal
effect holds beyond the single \(20\,\mathrm{mm}\) offset tested.

An independent measurement of the physical tool-face angle would remove the
principal interpretation limit. The reported alignment is inferred from the
end-effector pose through a transform assumed rigid, and the approximately
\(2^\circ\) of play about \(y_{\mathrm{EE}}\) is therefore unobservable. A
rigid mount, or an external angular measurement of the face, would allow the
\(t_2\) results to be reported as values rather than as lower bounds.

A matched null-space comparison remains outstanding. The projected damping and
the singular-value bias are implemented and instrumented, but without trials
that enable and disable the bias under otherwise identical contact conditions,
no experimental claim about singularity conditioning can be made.

The nominal set-up duration should be confirmed at
\(4.5\), \(5\), \(6\), and \(8\,\mathrm{s}\), with repeated trials at
\(5\,\mathrm{s}\). A general phase transition should depend on completion of
the commanded press ramp followed by a short interval of low alignment and
load rates, with a calculated timeout as a fallback.

A dedicated force/torque sensor or independent contact instrumentation would
improve the normal-load estimate and permit a more defensible analysis of
distributed contact and equivalent contact location. Further experiments
should also include additional tools, surface materials, fixture conditions,
and initial orientation errors.

The null-space method requires matched damping-only and
damping-plus-conditioning trials. These experiments should report
\(\Delta\sigma_{\min}\), projected joint velocity, Cartesian task drift,
projector residual, joint motion, and sigma torque. Probe configurations should
be checked against joint limits, and callback wall-clock duration should be
instrumented to establish worst-case real-time execution behaviour.
