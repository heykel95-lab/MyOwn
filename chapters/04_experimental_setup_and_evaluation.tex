\chapter{Experimental Methodology}
\label{ch:methodology}

This chapter defines the experimental methodology used for the current thesis focus. Earlier
set-point, trajectory-tracking, virtual-wall, and stiffness-variation
experiments are treated as archived development material. They were useful for
building and checking the controller, but they are not the central experimental
argument of the thesis in its present form.

The method considered here investigates passive self-alignment during
table contact. The robot is controlled by Cartesian impedance. A tool edge is
brought into contact with a table. At contact, the table applies an external
wrench to the tool. Because the tool is compliant, this wrench produces a small
translational and rotational displacement. The resulting motion can be
interpreted geometrically as a screw motion. The corresponding instantaneous
screw axis, or the closest point of this axis to the tool edge, is used to
describe whether the selected stiffness matrix produces a useful passive
alignment tendency.

\section{Experimental Question}
\label{sec:experimental_question}

The central experimental question is:

\begin{quote}
How should the Cartesian stiffness matrix \(K\) be selected so that the
contact wrench generated by a tool edge on a table produces passive
self-aligning motion around a useful virtual center of rotation, while the
robot remains stable and safe?
\end{quote}

This question connects three parts of the thesis. First, Cartesian impedance
control defines the relation between pose displacement and commanded wrench
\citep{Hogan1985,Ott2008,Albu2003}. Second, stiffness and damping selection
is a practical design problem in its own right, because the selected gains
determine how strongly disturbances, contact forces, and task errors are
converted into motion and commanded torque
\citep{Pollayil2023ImpedancePlanning}. Third, spatial impedance,
remote-center-compliance, and variable compliance-center ideas show that
translation and rotation can be coupled so that contact forces create
favorable alignment behavior instead of jamming or increasing misalignment
\citep{Fasse1997SpatialImpedance,Nevins1989ComputerControlledAssembly,Watson1978RCC,Wang2019VariableComplianceCenter,Hartisch2022FlexureRCC}.
Finally, the Franka Emika Panda implementation must keep the commanded joint
torques bounded and compatible with the real-time torque-control interface
\citep{FrankaFCI2023,Mayr2024CartesianImpedance,Hjorth2023EnergyAwareImpedance}.

\section{Robot and Software Setup}
\label{sec:robot_software_setup_contact}

The experiment is performed on the Franka Emika Panda using the torque-control
interface provided by libfranka. The controller runs inside the Franka Control
Interface with a control period of

\begin{equation}
T_s = 1\,\mathrm{ms}.
\label{eq:sampling_time_contact}
\end{equation}

At each control cycle, the current robot state is read, the Cartesian
impedance control law is evaluated, and the resulting joint torque command is
sent back to the robot. The controller uses the end-effector transformation
\(T_{\mathrm{EE}}\), the joint velocity vector \(\dot{q}\), the geometric
Jacobian \(J(q)\), and the model-based Coriolis term provided by the libfranka
model interface. The commanded torque has the form

\begin{equation}
\tau_{\mathrm{cmd}}
=
J^\top(q)\mathbf{F}_{\mathrm{cmd}}
+
\tau_{\mathrm{null}}
+
\tau_c(q,\dot{q}),
\label{eq:chapter4_contact_commanded_torque}
\end{equation}

where \(\mathbf{F}_{\mathrm{cmd}}\) is the commanded Cartesian impedance
wrench and \(\tau_{\mathrm{null}}\) is the projected null-space torque. The
gravity compensation is not added as a separate term in the user callback,
because the external torque command sent through libfranka is already handled
with robot-side gravity and motor-friction compensation by the Franka Control
Interface.

\section{Contact Geometry}
\label{sec:contact_geometry}

The task geometry is defined by a table plane, a tool edge, and a desired
virtual center of rotation. Let

\begin{equation}
n_s \in \mathbb{R}^3,
\qquad
\|n_s\| = 1
\label{eq:surface_normal}
\end{equation}

be the table normal expressed in the robot base frame. Two orthonormal
tangent directions \(t_1\) and \(t_2\) span the table plane. With the normal as
the first column, the task frame is therefore

\begin{equation}
R_s
=
\begin{bmatrix}
n_s & t_1 & t_2
\end{bmatrix},
\label{eq:surface_task_frame}
\end{equation}

consistent with the surface task frame \(R_{\mathrm{surface}}\) of
\cref{eq:surface_task_frame_matrix} and the implementation, where the normal,
the first tangent, and the second tangent are columns one, two, and three.

The tool edge is represented by a point \(p_e\) on the edge and an edge
direction \(a_e\). The virtual center of rotation is represented by a point
\(p_c\). For passive alignment, \(p_c\) should be chosen near the contact
region. A useful first choice is the point on the table below or close to the
tool edge, because this makes the desired motion resemble the behavior of a
remote center of compliance used in assembly tasks.

The lever arm from the virtual center to the end-effector/tool frame is

\begin{equation}
r_c
=
p_{\mathrm{EE}} - p_c.
\label{eq:contact_virtual_center_lever_arm}
\end{equation}

The lower-case subscript \(c\) is used here for the chosen virtual center
\(p_c\). Later, the upper-case subscript \(C\) is used for the physical
contact point \(p_C\) and the measured or estimated contact wrench \(W_C\).

If a force \(\mathbf{f}\) acts at the contact, the corresponding moment about
the end-effector depends on this lever arm. Therefore, the selected stiffness
matrix should not be interpreted only as three independent translational
springs and three independent rotational springs. For a virtual-center
behavior, translation and rotation must be selected consistently.

\section{Screw-Motion Evaluation}
\label{sec:screw_motion_evaluation}

By Chasles' theorem, any rigid-body displacement can be described as a screw
motion: a rotation about an axis combined with a translation along that same
axis \citep{Murray1994,Doria2019MozziAxis}. The contact-induced motion of the tool is therefore
neither a pure rotation nor a pure translation but a screw motion, and its
screw axis---or, for planar motion, its instantaneous pole---gives a compact
geometric description of how the tool moves during contact. This is the
geometric basis used here to judge where the passive motion pivots.

The measured displacement during contact contains both translation and
rotation. Over a short contact interval, the measured pose change is written
as

\begin{equation}
\Delta x_C
=
\begin{bmatrix}
\Delta p_C\\
\Delta \theta
\end{bmatrix},
\label{eq:contact_displacement_vector}
\end{equation}

where \(\Delta p_C\) is the translational displacement associated with the
contact interval and \(\Delta\theta\) is the small angular displacement. If
\(\|\Delta\theta\|>0\), the instantaneous pole of the measured motion---the
point about which the displacement is, to first order, a pure rotation---follows
from the standard small-displacement screw relation
\citep{Murray1994,Doria2019MozziAxis} as

\begin{equation}
r_{\mathrm{inst}}
=
\frac{
\Delta\theta
\times
\Delta p_C
}{
\|\Delta\theta\|^2
}.
\label{eq:instantaneous_pole_displacement}
\end{equation}

Here \(r_{\mathrm{inst}}\) is the pole position \emph{relative} to the
end-effector point whose displacement \(\Delta p_C\) is used, so the absolute
pole in the base frame is \(p_{\mathrm{pole}}=p_{\mathrm{EE}}+r_{\mathrm{inst}}\).
The estimate is reliable only when the angular displacement is not too small: if
\(\|\Delta\theta\|\) is below a small threshold, the division amplifies
measurement noise and the pole for that interval is discarded.

For the corresponding velocity representation, Chasles' theorem gives the
instantaneous screw axis directly from the measured twist
\citep{Murray1994,Doria2019MozziAxis}: its point closest to the origin and its
direction are

\begin{equation}
\xi
=
\begin{bmatrix}
v\\
\omega
\end{bmatrix},
\qquad
p_{\mathrm{ISA}}
=
\frac{\omega \times v}{\|\omega\|^2},
\qquad
a_{\mathrm{ISA}}
=
\frac{\omega}{\|\omega\|}.
\label{eq:contact_twist_and_isa}
\end{equation}

Both forms describe the same physical idea: the tool moves in a screw-like way,
and the instantaneous pole or screw axis is a compact geometric description of
that motion.

For good passive self-alignment the tool should pivot at the contact edge
rather than about its own frame origin. The desired pole is the tool
edge \(p_e\); in this evaluation the virtual center of rotation is exactly
this desired pole, so that ``virtual center'' and ``desired instantaneous pole
at the edge'' denote the same point. Because \(r_{\mathrm{inst}}\) is expressed
relative to the end-effector, the pole error is computed with the absolute pole
\(p_{\mathrm{pole}}=p_{\mathrm{EE}}+r_{\mathrm{inst}}\) so that both quantities
are in the same base frame:

\begin{equation}
e_P
=
\left\|
p_{\mathrm{EE}} + r_{\mathrm{inst}} - p_e
\right\|,
\label{eq:pole_error_metric}
\end{equation}

and a smaller \(e_P\) means the measured motion pivots closer to the edge.
Equivalently, for the velocity-based screw axis through the absolute point
\(p_{\mathrm{EE}}+p_{\mathrm{ISA}}\), the relevant quantity is the distance from
the tool edge to that axis,

\begin{equation}
d_{\mathrm{ISA},e}
=
\left\|
\left(
p_e - p_{\mathrm{EE}} - p_{\mathrm{ISA}}
\right)
\times
a_{\mathrm{ISA}}
\right\|,
\label{eq:isa_center_distance}
\end{equation}

which is the Chasles distance between the edge and the instantaneous screw
axis. The stiffness setting that minimizes this distance places the passive
pivot at the edge. This metric is evaluated together with the angular error,
displacement magnitude, contact force, and torque signals.

\section{Gain Selection Methodology}
\label{sec:cartesian_stiffness_selection}

The complete local Cartesian stiffness matrix in the task frame can be written
in the standard spatial-stiffness block form
\citep{Fasse1997SpatialImpedance,Ott2008} as

\begin{equation}
K_s
=
\begin{bmatrix}
K_{pp} & K_{pR}\\
K_{Rp} & K_{RR}
\end{bmatrix}.
\label{eq:contact_stiffness_block}
\end{equation}

Here, \(K_{pp}\) relates translational displacement to force,
\(K_{RR}\) relates rotational displacement to moment, and the off-diagonal
blocks \(K_{pR}\) and \(K_{Rp}\) describe translation--rotation coupling.
This block form is useful for explaining the physical coupling between sliding
and rotation during edge contact. In the main experimental method of this
thesis, however, a full coupled stiffness matrix is not identified or optimized
analytically. Instead, a small set of manually selected Cartesian stiffness
settings is tested and compared experimentally. This keeps the method
transparent and compatible with the implemented controller.

For a passive linear stiffness, the complete matrix should be symmetric
positive definite \citep{Fasse1997SpatialImpedance,Ott2008},

\begin{equation}
K_s = K_s^\top,
\qquad
K_s \succ 0.
\label{eq:contact_stiffness_spd}
\end{equation}

This condition is important because it allows the stiffness to be interpreted
as stored elastic energy \citep{Hogan1985,Ott2008,Fasse1997SpatialImpedance},

\begin{equation}
V_K
=
\frac{1}{2}
\Delta x^\top
K_s
\Delta x,
\qquad
\Delta x =
\begin{bmatrix}
\Delta p\\
\Delta \theta
\end{bmatrix}.
\label{eq:contact_elastic_energy}
\end{equation}

This energy interpretation is also the reason why the thesis separates the
geometric usefulness of a stiffness setting from its safety. A setting can
create a plausible virtual center in the measured motion but still be rejected
if it stores too much energy, creates large torque peaks, or requires damping
that is not reliable in contact \citep{Hjorth2023EnergyAwareImpedance}.

The commanded stiffness settings used for the main evaluation are first
computed from the quasi-static estimates in
Section~\ref{subsec:initial_stiffness_damping_values}. They are then grouped
into a small number of diagonal candidate matrices,

\begin{equation}
K_s^{(j)}
=
\mathrm{diag}
\left(
k_x^{(j)},k_y^{(j)},k_z^{(j)},
k_{rx}^{(j)},k_{ry}^{(j)},k_{rz}^{(j)}
\right),
\qquad
j=1,\dots,N_K .
\label{eq:candidate_diagonal_stiffness}
\end{equation}

The diagonal terms define the basic directional behavior. In the table-normal
direction, the stiffness should be high enough to avoid excessive penetration
or sagging, but not so high that the contact wrench creates abrupt torque
peaks. In the tangent directions, lower stiffness can be used when small
sliding or backward motion is acceptable. In the rotational directions, the
stiffness should be lower around the axes that allow the edge to align with
the table and higher around axes that should remain controlled.

This means that the evaluation does not search for a globally optimal
stiffness matrix in the mathematical sense. Instead, the quasi-static
force-displacement and moment-angle relations are used to compute practical
starting values. Around these starting values, the controller is tested with a
small number of meaningful stiffness configurations, for example high
rotational stiffness, medium rotational stiffness, low rotational stiffness,
and low rotational stiffness with additional lateral compliance. The best
setting is then selected from the measured behavior. This is intentionally
simpler than full impedance planning, where stiffness and damping can be
computed from task constraints, robot inertia, and robustness requirements
\citep{Pollayil2023ImpedancePlanning}. Here, the planning idea is used to
turn contact limits into practical candidate gains for the self-alignment
experiment.

\subsection{Quasi-Static Stiffness and Damping Selection}
\label{subsec:initial_stiffness_damping_values}

The candidate stiffness values are initialized from simple contact
requirements. Since the edge-contact experiment is performed at low velocity,
the first stiffness estimate is based on a quasi-static approximation. The
damping and inertial terms are neglected only for this initial estimate, so
the translational contact relation is approximated by the quasi-static spring
relation used in impedance control \citep{Hogan1985,Ott2008},

\begin{equation}
F_i
\approx
k_i \Delta x_i .
\label{eq:quasi_static_translational_stiffness}
\end{equation}

If the maximum acceptable force in direction \(i\) is \(F_{i,\max}\), and the
allowed compliant displacement is \(\Delta x_{i,\max}\), the corresponding
starting stiffness is

\begin{equation}
k_i
=
\frac{F_{i,\max}}{\Delta x_{i,\max}} .
\label{eq:initial_translational_stiffness}
\end{equation}

For example, if the table-normal force should stay near
\(15\,\mathrm{N}\) and the allowed normal displacement is
\(5\,\mathrm{mm}=0.005\,\mathrm{m}\), then

\begin{equation}
k_z
=
\frac{15}{0.005}
=
3000\,\mathrm{N/m}.
\label{eq:normal_stiffness_example}
\end{equation}

The same idea is used for the lateral directions. If a lateral force of
\(10\,\mathrm{N}\) should allow approximately \(10\,\mathrm{mm}\) of sliding
or backward motion, then

\begin{equation}
k_x
=
k_y
\approx
\frac{10}{0.01}
=
1000\,\mathrm{N/m}.
\label{eq:lateral_stiffness_example}
\end{equation}

The rotational stiffness is initialized from the contact moment and the
allowed angular deflection. If the contact edge is separated from the tool
frame origin by the lever-arm length \(l\), the moment magnitude follows from
the wrench moment generated by a force acting through a lever arm
\citep{Murray1994,Siciliano2009} and can be estimated as

\begin{equation}
M_C
\approx
l F_C .
\label{eq:contact_moment_lever_estimate}
\end{equation}

The rotational stiffness around a relevant alignment axis is then chosen as

\begin{equation}
k_{r,i}
=
\frac{M_{i,\max}}{\Delta \theta_{i,\max}} .
\label{eq:initial_rotational_stiffness}
\end{equation}

For example, with \(l=0.05\,\mathrm{m}\) and \(F_C=15\,\mathrm{N}\), the
estimated contact moment is \(M_C \approx 0.75\,\mathrm{Nm}\). If the tool
should be allowed to rotate by about \(10^\circ = 0.1745\,\mathrm{rad}\),
then

\begin{equation}
k_r
\approx
\frac{0.75}{0.1745}
\approx
4.3\,\mathrm{Nm/rad}.
\label{eq:rotational_stiffness_example}
\end{equation}

If only \(5^\circ = 0.0873\,\mathrm{rad}\) is allowed under the same moment,
the resulting stiffness is approximately \(8.6\,\mathrm{Nm/rad}\). These
values are not treated as exact optima. They define reasonable starting
points for the experimental stiffness set.

The damping values are initialized from the critical-damping relation of a
single mass-spring-damper system~\citep{Ogata2010},

\begin{equation}
d_i
=
2\zeta\sqrt{M_i k_i},
\label{eq:critical_damping_with_mass}
\end{equation}

where \(M_i\) is the effective inertia in direction \(i\) and \(\zeta\) is
the damping ratio. A consistent value takes \(M_i=\Lambda_{ii}\) from the
operational-space inertia \(\Lambda=(JM_q^{-1}J^\top)^{-1}\) provided by
libfranka, so that \(d_i=2\zeta\sqrt{\Lambda_{ii}k_i}\). In the implemented
controller the Cartesian inertia is not explicitly shaped for each direction,
so the practical initial value uses the common normalized-inertia approximation

\begin{equation}
d_i
\approx
2\sqrt{k_i}.
\label{eq:normalized_critical_damping}
\end{equation}

This approximation corresponds to setting the effective inertia to
\(M_i=1\); it is therefore not strictly dimensionally consistent and is used
only as a starting point. The damping values are
then checked experimentally by verifying that the table-contact response is
not oscillatory and that the commanded torques remain smooth.

For the values computed above, the normalized approximation gives, for example,

\begin{equation}
d_z = 2\sqrt{3000}\approx 110,
\qquad
d_x = d_y = 2\sqrt{1000}\approx 63,
\qquad
d_r = 2\sqrt{8}\approx 5.7 ,
\label{eq:worked_damping_example}
\end{equation}

in \(\mathrm{N\,s/m}\) for the translational and \(\mathrm{Nm\,s/rad}\) for the
rotational terms. Collecting the translational and rotational stiffness with
the matching damping, one representative complete initial setting is

\begin{equation}
K_x = \operatorname{diag}(1000,\,1000,\,3000,\ 4.3,\,4.3,\,5),
\label{eq:worked_stiffness_set}
\end{equation}
\begin{equation}
D_x \approx \operatorname{diag}(63,\,63,\,110,\ 4.2,\,4.2,\,4.5),
\label{eq:worked_damping_set}
\end{equation}

with translational stiffness in \(\mathrm{N/m}\), rotational stiffness in
\(\mathrm{Nm/rad}\), and damping in the corresponding units. This single
setting is the starting point from which the candidate settings below vary the
gains.

The candidate rotational stiffness values bracket the quasi-static estimate of
\cref{eq:rotational_stiffness_example}: Tests~3--5 lie at or near it (about
\(4\)--\(8\,\mathrm{Nm/rad}\)), while Tests~1--2 are deliberately stiffer to show
how excessive rotational stiffness resists the passive alignment. The
translational values are kept stiff in the normal direction and softer
laterally, following the quasi-static force--displacement estimates.

\paragraph{Intuition.}
When the tool edge is pressed onto the table, the tool rocks into place by
rotating and sliding at the same time, and at each instant it turns about a
single pivot point---the instantaneous pole introduced in
Section~\ref{sec:screw_motion_evaluation} through Chasles' theorem.
Good self-alignment means this pivot sits at the contact edge, so the tool face
settles flat onto the table like a door swinging about its hinge; if the pivot
were at the wrist instead, the edge would scrape rather than the face settling.
There are two ways to obtain this. The implemented controller uses a diagonal
stiffness, for which the pivot is \emph{not} placed directly: it emerges from
the contact geometry and the chosen gains, so the procedure is to try a few
settings, measure where the pivot ends up, and keep the one whose pivot lies
closest to the edge. The ideal target is a \emph{coupled} stiffness that links
translation and rotation so that the pivot lies at the edge \emph{by design},
with no trial needed. The diagonal controller approximates this ideal; the
coupled construction itself is given at the end of this section.

The virtual-center interpretation is still important for understanding why
the stiffness values affect alignment. A small rotation around a virtual
center produces a related translation of the end-effector
\citep{Murray1994,Fasse1997SpatialImpedance},

\begin{equation}
\Delta p
\approx
\Delta \theta \times r_c
=
-
[r_c]_\times \Delta \theta.
\label{eq:virtual_center_kinematic_coupling}
\end{equation}

Therefore, a fully coupled virtual-center stiffness could be constructed by
penalizing the motion of the selected center point rather than only the
end-effector origin. For a point \(p_c\), the displacement of that point is
approximated by

\begin{equation}
\Delta p_c
=
\Delta p
+
[r_c]_\times \Delta \theta.
\label{eq:center_point_displacement}
\end{equation}

Penalizing \(\Delta p_c\) with a translational stiffness \(K_c\) gives the
energy term

\begin{equation}
V_c
=
\frac{1}{2}
\Delta p_c^\top
K_c
\Delta p_c.
\label{eq:center_point_energy}
\end{equation}

This expression generates coupled stiffness terms between translation and
rotation. The coupling is what allows the contact wrench to produce a motion
that appears to rotate around the selected center. The remaining rotational
stiffness \(K_R\) can then be added to keep rotations bounded:

\begin{equation}
V
=
\frac{1}{2}
\left(
\Delta p + [r_c]_\times \Delta \theta
\right)^\top
K_c
\left(
\Delta p + [r_c]_\times \Delta \theta
\right)
+
\frac{1}{2}
\Delta \theta^\top
K_R
\Delta \theta.
\label{eq:virtual_center_total_energy}
\end{equation}

Equation~\eqref{eq:virtual_center_total_energy} is the principled way to place
the pivot at a chosen point. Choosing the center at the tool edge,
\(p_c=p_e\), makes \(\Delta p_c\) the displacement of the edge, so penalizing it
with \(K_c\) builds a stiffness whose centre of compliance is the edge.
Equivalently, a stiffness defined diagonally in an edge-centred frame,
\(K_{\mathrm{edge}}\), is mapped to the end-effector frame by the rigid-body
adjoint \(\mathrm{Ad}_g\) of the end-effector-to-edge offset \(g\)
\citep{Murray1994,Fasse1997SpatialImpedance}. In this
thesis \(g\) is the small-displacement transform from the end-effector origin
to the chosen edge point, and \(r_c=p_c-p_{\mathrm{EE}}\). With the edge frame
chosen parallel to the end-effector frame, the displacement of the edge is

\begin{equation}
\begin{bmatrix}
\Delta p_c\\
\Delta \theta
\end{bmatrix}
=
\underbrace{
\begin{bmatrix}
I_3 & [r_c]_\times\\
0_3 & I_3
\end{bmatrix}}_{\mathrm{Ad}_g}
\begin{bmatrix}
\Delta p\\
\Delta \theta
\end{bmatrix}.
\label{eq:edge_adjoint_definition}
\end{equation}

Thus, \(\mathrm{Ad}_g\) is the matrix that converts an end-effector origin
translation and rotation into the corresponding motion of the selected edge
point. The coupled stiffness in the end-effector coordinates is then

\begin{equation}
K_{\mathrm{coupled}}
=
\mathrm{Ad}_g^{\top}\,K_{\mathrm{edge}}\,\mathrm{Ad}_g,
\label{eq:edge_centered_adjoint_stiffness}
\end{equation}

which produces exactly the translation--rotation coupling of
\eqref{eq:virtual_center_total_energy}. With such a coupled stiffness, the
contact wrench produces a screw motion whose instantaneous pole
(Section~\ref{sec:screw_motion_evaluation}) lies at the edge by construction.
This is the spatial-impedance / remote-centre-of-compliance idea
\citep{Fasse1997SpatialImpedance} expressed through the screw geometry used
in Mozzi-axis descriptions of compliant end-effector motion
\citep{Doria2019MozziAxis}.

The implemented controller, however, uses a diagonal stiffness without this
coupling, so its centre of compliance is the tool-frame origin and the pivot is
not placed analytically. The experiment therefore does not command an active
rotation about the edge. Instead, the tool is intentionally started with a small
tilt, lowered until the edge contacts the table, and the contact wrench is
allowed to rotate the tool passively under the selected compliance. For the
diagonal controller the pivot location is therefore \emph{emergent}: it is set
by the contact geometry and the chosen gains. The practical procedure is then to
choose several representative diagonal settings \(K_s^{(j)}\), run the same
edge-contact experiment for each, compute the instantaneous pole from the
measured motion using Chasles' theorem, and select the setting whose pole lies
closest to the edge---smallest \(e_P\) and \(d_{\mathrm{ISA},e}\)---together
with acceptable displacement, force, and torque. The coupled design
\eqref{eq:virtual_center_total_energy} is thus the passive stiffness design
that the diagonal selection approximates.

\subsection{Pole-Based Gain Design}
\label{subsec:impedance_around_pole}

The relation between the gains and the pole can be read in two directions. In
the \emph{forward} direction one fixes the stiffness and damping, runs the
contact, and observes where the instantaneous pole lands. In the \emph{inverse}
direction---which is the actual design goal---one first \emph{chooses} the pole
and then computes the stiffness and damping that place it there. Choosing where
the tool should pivot and solving for the gains is what it means to select the
impedance \emph{around} a pole, and it is the inverse of the trial-and-compare
procedure above.

This formulation does not add a separate force command, moment command, or
active rotation trajectory. The Cartesian impedance law remains the same; only
the structure of the gain matrices changes. A diagonal or block-diagonal
controller uses independent translational and rotational gains, while a
virtual-center design uses structured off-diagonal terms in \(K\) and \(D\) so
that the same passive spring--damper law behaves as if it were attached at the
chosen point \(p_c\).

\paragraph{Two different kinds of coupling.}
A non-diagonal stiffness can arise in two ways that have very different effects,
and they must not be confused. In the block form of
\cref{eq:contact_stiffness_block}, only the translation--rotation blocks
\(K_{pR},K_{Rp}\) place the pole. Expressing a diagonal surface-frame stiffness
in the base frame uses the \emph{block-diagonal} rotation
\(T_R=\operatorname{diag}(R_{\mathrm{surface}},R_{\mathrm{surface}})\),

\begin{equation}
K_0 = T_R\,K\,T_R^\top
=
\begin{bmatrix}
R_{\mathrm{surface}}K_p R_{\mathrm{surface}}^\top & 0\\[2pt]
0 & R_{\mathrm{surface}}K_R R_{\mathrm{surface}}^\top
\end{bmatrix}.
\label{eq:surface_rotation_keeps_block_diagonal}
\end{equation}

For an inclined surface this makes the translational block \(K_{pp}\) a full
\(3\times3\) matrix, so the \(x\)-, \(y\)- and \(z\)-stiffnesses become coupled;
for a horizontal table the surface frame is axis-aligned and \(K_0\) is simply
diagonal, identical to the surface-frame matrix up to the axis labelling. In
\emph{both} cases, however, the off-diagonal blocks remain zero,
\(K_{pR}=K_{Rp}=0\): the rotation couples translation only among its own axes
and never couples translation to rotation. The centre of compliance therefore
stays at the tool-frame origin, and the surface transform alone does \emph{not}
place the pole---whether the surface is horizontal or inclined.

\paragraph{Placing the pole with a lever-arm offset.}
The translation--rotation coupling that moves the pole comes instead from
referring the impedance to an offset point \(p_c\) rather than to the tool-frame
origin. With the lever arm \(r_c=p_{\mathrm{EE}}-p_c\), the rigid-body adjoint

\begin{equation}
\mathrm{Ad}_g =
\begin{bmatrix}
I & [r_c]_\times\\
0 & I
\end{bmatrix}
\label{eq:adjoint_offset}
\end{equation}

transforms a diagonal impedance defined \emph{about} the pole into the
tool-frame matrices that the controller applies,

\begin{equation}
K_{\mathrm{TCP}} = \mathrm{Ad}_g^\top K_{p_c}\,\mathrm{Ad}_g,
\qquad
D_{\mathrm{TCP}} = \mathrm{Ad}_g^\top D_{p_c}\,\mathrm{Ad}_g.
\label{eq:impedance_around_pole}
\end{equation}

For a diagonal \(K_{p_c}=\operatorname{diag}(K_p,K_R)\) the result has the
non-zero coupling \(K_{pR}=K_p\,[r_c]_\times\), which is exactly the block that
places the pole at \(p_c\). This coupling depends only on the offset \(r_c\) and
not on the surface inclination, so it is required for a horizontal table just as
for an inclined one. The damping is transformed by the \emph{same} adjoint, so
that the spring and the dissipation are both centred at \(p_c\); if only the
stiffness were referred to \(p_c\) and the damping left at the tool frame, the
spring would pivot about \(p_c\) while the damping did not, and the transient
pole would drift away from the chosen point.

\paragraph{Choosing the pole.}
The pole is selected on the surface normal,

\begin{equation}
p_c = p_s + h\,n_s,
\label{eq:pole_choice_h}
\end{equation}

where the offset \(h\) places the pole above (\(h>0\)), on (\(h=0\)), or below
(\(h<0\)) the surface. Each value of \(h\) yields a different coupled
\(K_{\mathrm{TCP}},D_{\mathrm{TCP}}\) through \cref{eq:impedance_around_pole}, so
choosing the pole and computing the gains are the same design step seen from
opposite ends. A pole on the surface lets the tool rock flat onto it; a pole
below the surface gives stronger self-centring, as in remote-centre-of-compliance
assembly \citep{Watson1978RCC}; a pole above, near the tool, gives a gentler
response with more in-plane sliding.

\paragraph{Many instantaneous poles.}
When the contact is run, the instantaneous pole is not a single point but one
value per sample, because each instant has its own twist
\(\xi=[\,v\;\;\omega\,]^\top\) and therefore its own Chasles pole. The design
above fixes the \emph{quasi-static} pole---the centre of compliance---at
\(p_c\); the per-sample instantaneous poles then cluster around \(p_c\) when the
design is good. The screw-motion evaluation of
Section~\ref{sec:screw_motion_evaluation} is therefore used as a \emph{check},
not as a menu of live poles: the measured poles are computed with Chasles'
theorem, the unreliable ones (very small \(\|\Delta\theta\|\)) are discarded, and
the remaining cluster is compared with the chosen \(p_c\) through \(e_P\) and
\(d_{\mathrm{ISA},e}\). Sweeping \(h\) and showing that the measured pole cluster
follows \(p_c\) demonstrates directly that the impedance has been designed around
the selected pole.

\subsection{Selection Order of the Stiffness and Damping Methods}
\label{sec:role_of_gain_methods}

Three related methods are used in this chapter. They are complementary steps of
one stiffness- and damping-selection workflow. The order matters, because each
method answers a different question:

\begin{enumerate}
    \item \textbf{Quasi-static initial selection.} This is the first step,
    because it can be done before contact data exist. Allowable
    force--displacement and moment--angle limits are used to compute safe
    starting values for \(K_{\mathrm{cmd}}\). The damping
    \(D_{\mathrm{cmd}}\) is then chosen from the same stiffness values using a
    critically damped or slightly over-damped design. This method gives practical
    diagonal or block-diagonal gains for the implemented controller. It does
    not prescribe a pivot point directly; the apparent pole is evaluated after
    contact.

    \item \textbf{Pole-based virtual-center selection.} This is the second
    design step once a useful pole or virtual center \(p_c\) has been selected
    from the desired passive alignment behavior. The same Cartesian impedance
    law is kept, but the commanded gains are constructed as structured full
    matrices \(K_{\mathrm{TCP}}\) and \(D_{\mathrm{TCP}}\) using the adjoint
    transformation in \cref{eq:impedance_around_pole}. The purpose is to make
    the passive compliance behave as if the tool tends to rotate around the
    chosen pole. This is still passive gain selection: it does not add an
    active rotation trajectory, a separate commanded moment, or a new commanded
    force.

    \item \textbf{Least-squares effective identification.} This is the third
    step and can only be used after one or more contact trials have been
    logged. The measured displacement, velocity, force, and moment data are
    fitted to estimate effective matrices such as
    \(K_{rt}^{\mathrm{eff}}\), \(D_{rt}^{\mathrm{eff}}\),
    \(K_R^{\mathrm{eff}}\), and \(D_R^{\mathrm{eff}}\). These matrices describe
    how the combined robot--tool--contact system actually behaved. They are
    used to explain the observed passive alignment and to update the next
    commanded gain choice; they are not the gains that were originally sent to
    the controller.
\end{enumerate}

The intended design logic is therefore

\begin{equation}
\begin{aligned}
\text{quasi-static start}
&\;\rightarrow\;
\text{pole-based gain design}
\;\rightarrow\;
\text{contact trial}\\
&\;\rightarrow\;
\text{least-squares effective fit}
\;\rightarrow\;
\text{refined commanded gains}.
\end{aligned}
\label{eq:gain_selection_workflow}
\end{equation}

In other words, the controller receives commanded gains
\(K_{\mathrm{cmd}}\) and \(D_{\mathrm{cmd}}\), while the least-squares fit
estimates apparent or effective gains \(K^{\mathrm{eff}}\) and
\(D^{\mathrm{eff}}\) after the contact has happened. The effective values
describe the behavior of the combined robot--tool--contact system, including
the commanded impedance, the tool offset, the table constraint, friction,
torque limits, and model or measurement errors. They are therefore diagnostic
quantities: they tell how the system really behaved during contact and can be
used to improve the next commanded gain choice, but they are not themselves the
initial gains applied by the controller.

\subsection{Least-Squares Identification of Effective Stiffness and Damping}
\label{sec:effective_stiffness_damping_identification}

The offline identification method identifies an effective contact model from the
logged table-contact data. It is included as part of the thesis work because
the direct stiffness comparison tells which commanded setting behaves better,
but it does not explain how much of the measured self-alignment moment comes
from translational sliding, translational damping, rotational stiffness, or
rotational damping.

During edge contact, the passive alignment behavior is not governed only by
the rotational stiffness. Since the tool can also slide and translate due to
the contact geometry, translational motion contributes to the generated
moment. The contact moment is therefore modeled as the lower rotational part
of an effective Cartesian impedance relation
\citep{Hogan1985,Fasse1997SpatialImpedance}. The identified matrices are
called effective matrices because they describe the behavior observed during
contact; they are not necessarily identical to the diagonal gains commanded in
the controller.

This distinction is important for interpreting the fitted values. The symbols
with the superscript ``eff'' do not mean that the controller internally changed
its stiffness or damping during the experiment. They mean that the logged data
are being approximated by an equivalent linear contact model after the fact.

Repeated trials with the same commanded gains and the same nominal contact
geometry can be used together in this fit. In that case, the screw-axis
calculation is used as a consistency check: trials whose instantaneous pole
clusters around the same useful region are treated as samples of the same
contact behavior. However, Chasles' theorem does not imply that one fixed pole
exists for the whole trial. It guarantees an instantaneous screw axis for each
instantaneous displacement or twist. During real contact the pole can move from
sample to sample because of friction, sliding, changing contact pressure,
torque limits, measurement noise, and the fact that the implemented controller
uses diagonal gains. Therefore the pole is used to filter and interpret the
motion, while the least-squares fit estimates the effective moment model from
the logged samples.

At one sample \(i\), the contact moment around the contact point or tool edge
can be modeled as a linear-in-parameters effective impedance relation
\citep{Hogan1985,Ott2008}

\begin{equation}
M_{C,i}
=
K_{rt}^{\mathrm{eff}}\Delta x_{C,i}
+
D_{rt}^{\mathrm{eff}}v_{C,i}
+
K_R^{\mathrm{eff}}\Delta\theta_i
+
D_R^{\mathrm{eff}}\omega_i .
\label{eq:effective_contact_moment_sample}
\end{equation}

Here, \(\Delta x_{C,i}\) is the translation or sliding displacement of the
contact point, \(v_{C,i}\) is the corresponding linear velocity,
\(\Delta\theta_i\) is the angular displacement, and \(\omega_i\) is the
angular velocity. The moment in the logs, whether the commanded
\(\mathbf{m}\) or the estimated external \(\mathbf{m}_{\mathrm{ext}}\), is
expressed about the tool-frame origin (TCP). The contact moment \(M_{C,i}\) used
here is the moment about the contact edge, obtained by moving the logged moment
to the edge with the moment-transfer theorem
\(M_B=M_A+(p_A-p_B)\times\mathbf{f}\) \citep{Murray1994,Siciliano2009}. With
\(A\) the TCP and \(B\) the edge, and \(r_C=p_C-p_{\mathrm{EE}}\) the lever arm
from the TCP to the edge, \(p_{\mathrm{EE}}-p_C=-r_C\) gives

\begin{equation}
M_{C,i}
=
\mathbf{m}_i - r_C \times \mathbf{f}_i ,
\label{eq:contact_moment_to_edge}
\end{equation}

where \((\mathbf{f}_i,\mathbf{m}_i)\) is the logged force and moment; the same
transformation applies whether the commanded or the measured wrench is used.
The term
\(K_{rt}^{\mathrm{eff}}\Delta x_{C,i}\) represents the moment contribution
caused by translational sliding, while
\(K_R^{\mathrm{eff}}\Delta\theta_i\) represents the usual rotational
impedance contribution. The two damping terms describe moment contributions
caused by linear and angular velocity.

One sample is not enough to identify all matrices. For \(N\) samples, define

\begin{equation}
\phi_i
=
\begin{bmatrix}
\Delta x_{C,i}\\
v_{C,i}\\
\Delta\theta_i\\
\omega_i
\end{bmatrix}
\in
\mathbb{R}^{12},
\label{eq:effective_coupling_regressor}
\end{equation}

and

\begin{equation}
A
=
\begin{bmatrix}
K_{rt}^{\mathrm{eff}} &
D_{rt}^{\mathrm{eff}} &
K_R^{\mathrm{eff}} &
D_R^{\mathrm{eff}}
\end{bmatrix}
\in
\mathbb{R}^{3\times 12}.
\label{eq:effective_coupling_matrix}
\end{equation}

The sample equation is then

\begin{equation}
M_{C,i}
=
A\phi_i .
\label{eq:effective_coupling_compact_sample}
\end{equation}

Stacking all samples gives

\begin{equation}
Y
=
\Phi A^\top,
\label{eq:effective_coupling_stacked}
\end{equation}

with

\begin{equation}
Y
=
\begin{bmatrix}
M_{C,1}^\top\\
M_{C,2}^\top\\
\vdots\\
M_{C,N}^\top
\end{bmatrix},
\qquad
\Phi
=
\begin{bmatrix}
\phi_1^\top\\
\phi_2^\top\\
\vdots\\
\phi_N^\top
\end{bmatrix}.
\label{eq:effective_coupling_stacked_matrices}
\end{equation}

The least-squares estimate is computed with the Moore--Penrose pseudoinverse;
the same damped least-squares idea is commonly used for robust inverse
kinematic and Jacobian problems \citep{Chiaverini1994DampedLeastSquares},

\begin{equation}
A^\top
=
\Phi^\# Y,
\label{eq:effective_coupling_pseudoinverse}
\end{equation}

where \(\Phi^\#\) is the pseudoinverse of \(\Phi\). If \(\Phi^\top\Phi\) is
well conditioned, the same ordinary least-squares estimate can be written as

\begin{equation}
A^\top
=
\left(
\Phi^\top\Phi
\right)^{-1}
\Phi^\top Y.
\label{eq:effective_coupling_normal_equation}
\end{equation}

The matrix \(A\) can then be split into \(K_{rt}^{\mathrm{eff}}\),
\(D_{rt}^{\mathrm{eff}}\), \(K_R^{\mathrm{eff}}\), and
\(D_R^{\mathrm{eff}}\). These blocks are the effective stiffness and damping
matrices computed by the fit.

To make the last step explicit, define

\begin{equation}
\widehat{A}
=
\left(\Phi^\# Y\right)^\top
=
\begin{bmatrix}
\widehat{A}_1 &
\widehat{A}_2 &
\widehat{A}_3 &
\widehat{A}_4
\end{bmatrix},
\qquad
\widehat{A}_j\in\mathbb{R}^{3\times3}.
\label{eq:effective_coupling_final_A}
\end{equation}

The final fitted matrices are the four \(3\times3\) blocks of
\(\widehat{A}\):

\begin{equation}
\boxed{
\begin{aligned}
\widehat{K}_{rt}^{\mathrm{eff}} &= \widehat{A}_1, &
\widehat{D}_{rt}^{\mathrm{eff}} &= \widehat{A}_2,\\
\widehat{K}_{R}^{\mathrm{eff}}  &= \widehat{A}_3, &
\widehat{D}_{R}^{\mathrm{eff}}  &= \widehat{A}_4 .
\end{aligned}}
\label{eq:effective_final_stiffness_damping_blocks}
\end{equation}

Substituting these blocks back into the model gives the fitted moment
prediction

\begin{equation}
\widehat{M}_{C,i}
=
\widehat{K}_{rt}^{\mathrm{eff}}\Delta x_{C,i}
+
\widehat{D}_{rt}^{\mathrm{eff}}v_{C,i}
+
\widehat{K}_{R}^{\mathrm{eff}}\Delta\theta_i
+
\widehat{D}_{R}^{\mathrm{eff}}\omega_i .
\label{eq:effective_final_fitted_moment}
\end{equation}

The identified coupling is useful even when the commanded matrix
\(K_s^{(j)}\) is diagonal. In that case, the commanded off-diagonal stiffness
is zero, but effective coupling can still appear because of the contact
geometry, the tool offset, the Jacobian, friction, table constraints,
null-space behavior, torque limits, and the fact that the contact force acts
at the tool edge rather than at the end-effector origin.

For this reason, the fitted matrices are not expected to reproduce the
configured gains exactly. In an ideal free-space test with clean measurements,
no contact constraints, no saturation, and a model that matches the controller
law exactly, the fitted rotational terms could be close to the commanded
rotational stiffness and damping. In the edge-contact experiment, however, the
fit describes the \emph{effective} contact behavior. A nonzero
\(K_{rt}^{\mathrm{eff}}\) can be found even when the commanded
translation--rotation stiffness block is zero, because the contact geometry and
tool offset convert translational sliding and force into moment about the edge.

The identified model separates the two stiffness-related moment contributions

\begin{equation}
M_{\mathrm{trans}}
=
K_{rt}^{\mathrm{eff}}\Delta x_C,
\qquad
M_{\mathrm{rot}}
=
K_R^{\mathrm{eff}}\Delta\theta
\label{eq:effective_moment_contributions}
\end{equation}

so that the observed self-alignment can be interpreted more precisely. If
\(\|M_{\mathrm{trans}}\|\) is large, then sliding and translation are an
important part of the passive alignment behavior. If
\(\|M_{\mathrm{rot}}\|\) dominates, then the behavior is mainly explained by
rotational compliance. The fitted damping matrices \(D_{rt}^{\mathrm{eff}}\)
and \(D_R^{\mathrm{eff}}\) are used in the same way to check whether velocity
terms are significant during the contact transient.

This least-squares model is not used to command the robot online in this
thesis. Instead, it is an offline analysis method for computing effective
stiffness and damping contributions from the same logged contact trials used
for the direct stiffness comparison. It therefore answers a different question
from the two gain-design methods: the quasi-static and coupled virtual-center
methods compute commanded stiffness and damping matrices, while the
least-squares fit computes the effective translation--rotation and damping
behavior that produced the observed self-alignment.

\section{Simple Contact Model}
\label{sec:simple_contact_model}

The contact experiment is interpreted with the same Cartesian impedance
notation used in the controller derivation \citep{Hogan1985,Ott2008}. The
commanded impedance wrench is

\begin{equation}
W_{\mathrm{imp}}
=
K_s
\begin{bmatrix}
\Delta p\\
\Delta \theta
\end{bmatrix}
+
D_s
\begin{bmatrix}
v\\
\omega
\end{bmatrix},
\qquad
W_{\mathrm{imp}}
=
\begin{bmatrix}
f\\
m
\end{bmatrix}.
\label{eq:contact_impedance_wrench_simple}
\end{equation}

Here, \(K_s\) and \(D_s\) denote the stiffness and damping matrices expressed
in the same task frame.
The scalar entries \(K_i\) and \(D_i\) used below are the corresponding
principal-direction values of these matrices.

For a slow contact motion, the quasi-static compliance relation can be written
as \citep{Hogan1985,Fasse1997SpatialImpedance}

\begin{equation}
\begin{bmatrix}
\Delta p\\
\Delta \theta
\end{bmatrix}
\approx
K_s^{-1} W_C,
\qquad
W_C
=
\begin{bmatrix}
f_C\\
m_C
\end{bmatrix},
\label{eq:contact_quasistatic_compliance}
\end{equation}

where \(W_C\) is the contact wrench. This equation is not used to compute the
controller command directly. It is used to explain the physical effect of the
selected stiffness: for the same type of contact wrench, lower stiffness
allows a larger displacement and higher stiffness resists the motion more
strongly.

If the contact force acts at the tool edge, it also creates a moment. With
\(p_C\) denoting the contact point and \(p_{\mathrm{EE}}\) the end-effector
origin, the contact lever arm is

\begin{equation}
r_C
=
p_C
-
p_{\mathrm{EE}} .
\label{eq:contact_edge_lever_arm}
\end{equation}

The moment caused by the contact force is then \citep{Murray1994,Siciliano2009}

\begin{equation}
m_C
=
r_C
\times
f_C .
\label{eq:contact_edge_moment}
\end{equation}

This relation explains why the table contact can produce rotation even when
the controller is commanded through a Cartesian impedance law at the
end-effector. The force does not act exactly at the end-effector origin; it
acts at the edge. Therefore, the contact produces both a force and a moment.
The observed tool motion is consequently not a pure rotation and not a pure
translation, but a combined screw-like displacement.

\section{Safety Limits and Damping Check}
\label{sec:damping_safety_limits_contact}

The damping matrix \(D\) is selected to dissipate energy and reduce
oscillation after contact. In impedance planning, damping cannot be treated as
an afterthought to stiffness, because both gains shape the transient response,
the energy stored in the virtual spring, and the response to disturbances
\citep{Pollayil2023ImpedancePlanning}. For each principal direction, the critical damping
reference is

\begin{equation}
D_i
=
2\sqrt{M_iK_i},
\label{eq:critical_damping_contact}
\end{equation}

where \(M_i\) is the corresponding directional entry of the effective
Cartesian inertia and \(K_i\) is the selected stiffness. This expression is
used as a reference rather than as an automatic tuning rule, because the real
controller is discrete, torque limited, affected by model uncertainty, and
interacts with a stiff environment.

The following safety checks are part of the experiment:

\begin{itemize}
    \item the commanded torque must remain below the selected torque limit;
    \item the torque-rate limiter must prevent abrupt command changes;
    \item the tool displacement must stay within a small, predefined contact
    range;
    \item the rotational displacement must remain below the selected angular
    limit;
    \item the controller must not generate sustained oscillations after the
    tool touches or leaves the table.
\end{itemize}

These checks are necessary because a stiffness matrix that is geometrically
meaningful can still be unsuitable for real hardware if it produces aggressive
torques or poorly damped contact behavior. Energy-aware impedance control
studies make the same point from a safety perspective: contact tasks should
limit not only position error, but also the energy and power that the
controller can inject during unexpected interaction
\citep{Hjorth2023EnergyAwareImpedance}.

\section{Logged Data}
\label{sec:logged_data_contact}

During the experiment, selected measured and computed quantities are written
to a CSV file for offline evaluation. The main logged quantities are

\begin{equation}
\begin{aligned}
&
\mathbf{p}_{\mathrm{EE}},
R_{\mathrm{EE}},
\mathbf{p}_d,
R_d,
\mathbf{e}_p,
\mathbf{e}_R,
\dot{\mathbf{p}}_{\mathrm{EE}},
\omega_{\mathrm{EE}},
\\
&
\mathbf{f},
\mathbf{m},
\mathbf{f}_C,
\mathbf{m}_C,
\boldsymbol{\tau}.
\end{aligned}
\label{eq:logged_quantities_contact}
\end{equation}

The vectors \(\mathbf{f}\) and \(\mathbf{m}\) are the Cartesian force and
moment generated by the impedance controller. No dedicated external
force/torque sensor is mounted on the robot. Instead, the controller uses the
external wrench estimated by the robot itself, \texttt{O\_F\_ext\_hat\_K},
which is reconstructed from the joint torque sensors and the dynamic model.
This estimate is used for contact detection during the experiment and can be
logged as \(\mathbf{f}_C\) and \(\mathbf{m}_C\). Because it is a model-based
estimate rather than a direct measurement at the contact, it is less accurate
than a dedicated force/torque sensor, especially during fast motion or near
singular configurations. The passive alignment behavior is therefore evaluated
from the measured pose displacement, the computed impedance wrench, the
commanded joint torques, and this estimated external wrench. This limitation
must be kept in mind when interpreting the results. More elaborate sensorless
approaches can additionally estimate environment stiffness for impedance
tuning, including on the Franka Emika Panda, but such estimation is outside the
implemented scope of the present thesis \citep{Roveda2021SensorlessStiffness}.

\subsection{Commanded and Measured Wrench}
\label{subsec:commanded_measured_wrench}

Two different Cartesian wrenches appear in the impedance-controlled contact, and
it is important to keep them apart.

The first is the \emph{commanded} (calculated) impedance wrench. It is computed
directly by the controller from the pose error and velocity,

\begin{equation}
W_{\mathrm{imp}}
=
\begin{bmatrix} \mathbf{f}\\ \mathbf{m}\end{bmatrix}
=
\begin{bmatrix}
K_p\mathbf{e}_p - D_p\dot{\mathbf{p}}_{\mathrm{EE}}\\
K_R\mathbf{e}_R - D_R\omega_{\mathrm{EE}}
\end{bmatrix},
\label{eq:commanded_wrench_logging}
\end{equation}

and it is the wrench the controller imposes on the robot through \(J^\top\). It
is always available, requires no sensor, and is exactly the quantity the
stiffness and damping matrices produce.

The second is the \emph{measured} (estimated external) wrench
\(W_{\mathrm{ext}}\), the contact reaction from the table. It is not commanded;
it is what the environment applies to the tool. The robot provides it directly
as \texttt{O\_F\_ext\_hat\_K}, a model-based estimate computed from the full
robot dynamics and the joint-torque sensors and then filtered. This is the
estimate used in this work.

The same quantity can also be reconstructed by the user from the external joint
torque \(\tau_{\mathrm{ext}}\). Since an external wrench maps to joint torque by
\(\tau_{\mathrm{ext}}=J^\top W_{\mathrm{ext}}\), a simple damped least-squares
inverse \citep{Khatib1987,Chiaverini1994DampedLeastSquares} gives

\begin{equation}
W_{\mathrm{ext}}
\approx
\left(JJ^\top + \lambda^2 I\right)^{-1} J\,\tau_{\mathrm{ext}},
\qquad
W_{\mathrm{ext}}
=
\begin{bmatrix} \mathbf{f}_{\mathrm{ext}}\\ \mathbf{m}_{\mathrm{ext}}\end{bmatrix},
\label{eq:external_wrench_estimate}
\end{equation}

where \(\lambda\) is a small damping factor for robustness near singular
configurations. For the redundant seven-joint arm, however,
\(\tau_{\mathrm{ext}}\) can contain a null-space component that corresponds to
no Cartesian wrench, and the theoretically correct mapping is the dynamically
consistent inverse \cite{Khatib1987}

\begin{equation}
W_{\mathrm{ext}}
=
\left(J M_q^{-1} J^\top\right)^{-1} J M_q^{-1}\,\tau_{\mathrm{ext}},
\label{eq:dynamically_consistent_wrench}
\end{equation}

which weights the inverse by the joint-space mass matrix \(M_q\). The plain
damped pseudoinverse \eqref{eq:external_wrench_estimate} ignores \(M_q\) and is
therefore an approximation. Consequently \texttt{O\_F\_ext\_hat\_K} and the
user-reconstructed wrench estimate the same physical quantity but are
\emph{not} numerically identical: the robot estimate is the better-conditioned,
filtered one and is preferred here, while the reconstruction formulas are useful
mainly when only \(\tau_{\mathrm{ext}}\) is available.

The two wrenches describe the same interaction from opposite sides. During the
slow, quasi-static contact considered here the tool is close to equilibrium, so
the commanded impedance wrench balances the external contact wrench
\citep{Hogan1985,Ott2008},

\begin{equation}
W_{\mathrm{imp}} \approx -\,W_{\mathrm{ext}}.
\label{eq:wrench_balance}
\end{equation}

The restoring spring--damper wrench produced by the controller therefore mirrors
the contact push at the edge. For the effective-moment identification of
Section~\ref{sec:effective_stiffness_damping_identification}, the contact moment
\(M_C\) can be taken from either side: the commanded moment \(\mathbf{m}\) is
always available and needs no estimation, while the measured moment
\(\mathbf{m}_{\mathrm{ext}}\) (from \texttt{O\_F\_ext\_hat\_K} or
\eqref{eq:external_wrench_estimate}) provides an independent check. Because of
\eqref{eq:wrench_balance} the two should agree up to sign and the small dynamic
terms, so the choice does not change the identified behavior; it is only a
matter of which measurement is used.

The balance \eqref{eq:wrench_balance} also serves as a consistency check on the
logged data. During quasi-static contact the commanded and estimated external
moments should be equal and opposite, so their agreement over a contact interval
can be quantified by

\begin{equation}
r_{\mathrm{check}}
=
\frac{\left\|\mathbf{m}+\mathbf{m}_{\mathrm{ext}}\right\|}{\left\|\mathbf{m}\right\|}.
\label{eq:wrench_consistency_check}
\end{equation}

A small \(r_{\mathrm{check}}\) confirms three things at once: the motion is slow
enough for the quasi-static assumption to hold, the impedance model is
consistent with the real contact, and the external-wrench estimate is
trustworthy despite the absence of a dedicated force/torque sensor. A large
\(r_{\mathrm{check}}\) instead flags fast transients, friction, or estimation
error, and marks intervals that should not be used for the quasi-static
analysis.

\section{Evaluation Metrics}
\label{sec:alignment_evaluation}

The orientation error is evaluated using the same rotation-error notation as
the controller. For the contact-alignment task, it can also be interpreted
geometrically from the tool normal and the table normal. If \(z_T\) is the
tool normal and \(n_s\) is the table normal, a simple local alignment error is

\begin{equation}
\mathbf{e}_R
=
z_T
\times
n_s .
\label{eq:contact_alignment_error_cross}
\end{equation}

Good passive alignment means that the contact-induced motion reduces this
orientation error. Therefore, the main before--after condition is

\begin{equation}
\|\mathbf{e}_{R,\mathrm{after}}\|
<
\|\mathbf{e}_{R,\mathrm{before}}\|.
\label{eq:alignment_error_reduction}
\end{equation}

Equivalently, the improvement can be written as

\begin{equation}
\Delta e_R
=
\|\mathbf{e}_{R,\mathrm{before}}\|
-
\|\mathbf{e}_{R,\mathrm{after}}\|.
\label{eq:alignment_error_improvement}
\end{equation}

If \(\Delta e_R>0\), the alignment improved during contact. The passive
rotation itself is described by

\begin{equation}
\Delta\theta_C
=
\theta_{\mathrm{after}}
-
\theta_{\mathrm{before}} .
\label{eq:contact_induced_rotation}
\end{equation}

The alignment error \(\mathbf{e}_R=z_T\times n_s\) points along the axis that
rotates the tool normal toward the table normal, that is, along the corrective
direction. The contact-induced rotation is therefore favorable when it has a
positive component along this corrective axis,

\begin{equation}
\mathbf{e}_{R,\mathrm{before}}^\top
\Delta\theta_C
>
0,
\label{eq:passive_rotation_condition}
\end{equation}

which is the condition for the passive rotation to reduce the misalignment.
(The sign follows from \(\mathbf{e}_R=z_T\times n_s\) being the correction axis;
it is the opposite of a convention in which \(\mathbf{e}_R\) is the
current-minus-desired axis-angle error.) This condition is used only as a local
alignment indicator. The final judgement also includes the pole location, force
level, torque level, and oscillation behavior.

\section{Experimental Procedure}
\label{sec:experimental_procedure_contact}

The experiment is executed in the following steps:

\begin{enumerate}
    \item Move the Panda to a safe initial pose above the table.
    \item Define the table frame, tool edge frame, and selected virtual center
    \(p_c\).
    \item Select one candidate stiffness matrix \(K_s^{(j)}\) from the
    manually defined stiffness set.
    \item Select damping values and torque limits.
    \item Move the tool edge slowly into contact with the table.
    \item Record the pose displacement, impedance wrench, and commanded joint
    torques during contact.
    \item Estimate the instantaneous pole or screw axis from the measured
    translation and rotation.
    \item Compare the orientation-error reduction, pole location, displacement,
    force, and torque signals against the design goal.
\end{enumerate}

The same procedure is repeated for each candidate stiffness setting. The
comparison is not meant to prove a universal optimal stiffness matrix. Instead,
it identifies which of the tested settings produces the most useful passive
self-aligning tendency for the chosen tool, table, and contact configuration.
The stiffness values are therefore selected experimentally rather than through
an analytical optimization.

\section{Evaluation Criteria and Acceptance Conditions}
\label{sec:evaluation_criteria_contact}

The experiment is evaluated using the following criteria:

\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Alignment direction:} the measured rotation should reduce the
    angular error between the tool edge and the table.
    \item \textbf{Virtual-center consistency:} the instantaneous screw axis
    should pass close to the selected virtual center or the useful contact
    region.
    \item \textbf{Contact displacement:} the translational displacement should
    remain small and bounded.
    \item \textbf{Torque behavior:} the commanded torques should remain smooth,
    bounded, and below the selected limits.
    \item \textbf{Damping behavior:} after contact or release, the response
    should not show sustained oscillations.
\end{itemize}

These criteria match the goal of the experiment more directly than the earlier
set-point and trajectory metrics. The central result is not tracking accuracy
alone, but whether the selected Cartesian stiffness matrix creates a safe and
useful passive alignment behavior during contact.
