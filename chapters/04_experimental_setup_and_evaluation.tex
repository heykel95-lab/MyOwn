\chapter{Experimental Setup, Results, and Discussion}
\label{ch:experiments}

This chapter defines the current experimental focus of the thesis. Earlier
set-point, trajectory-tracking, virtual-wall, and stiffness-variation
experiments are treated as archived development material. They were useful for
building and checking the controller, but they are not the central experimental
argument of the thesis in its present form.

The experiment considered here investigates passive self-alignment during
table contact. The robot is controlled by Cartesian impedance. A tool edge is
brought into contact with a table. At contact, the table applies an external
wrench to the tool. Because the tool is compliant, this wrench produces a small
translational and rotational displacement. The resulting motion can be
interpreted geometrically as a screw motion. The corresponding instantaneous
screw axis, or the closest point of this axis to the tool edge, is used to
describe whether the selected stiffness matrix produces a useful passive
alignment tendency.

\section{Experimental Question}
\label{sec:experimental_question}

The central experimental question is:

\begin{quote}
How should the Cartesian stiffness matrix \(K\) be selected so that the
contact wrench generated by a tool edge on a table produces passive
self-aligning motion around a useful virtual center of rotation, while the
robot remains stable and safe?
\end{quote}

This question connects three parts of the thesis. First, Cartesian impedance
control defines the relation between pose displacement and commanded wrench
\citep{Hogan1985,Ott2008,Albu2003}. Second, stiffness and damping selection
is a practical design problem in its own right, because the selected gains
determine how strongly disturbances, contact forces, and task errors are
converted into motion and commanded torque
\citep{Pollayil2023ImpedancePlanning}. Third, spatial impedance,
remote-center-compliance, and variable compliance-center ideas show that
translation and rotation can be coupled so that contact forces create
favorable alignment behavior instead of jamming or increasing misalignment
\citep{Fasse1997SpatialImpedance,Nevins1989ComputerControlledAssembly,Watson1978RCC,Wang2019VariableComplianceCenter,Hartisch2022FlexureRCC}.
Finally, the Franka Emika Panda implementation must keep the commanded joint
torques bounded and compatible with the real-time torque-control interface
\citep{FrankaFCI2023,Mayr2024CartesianImpedance,Hjorth2023EnergyAwareImpedance}.

\section{Robot and Software Setup}
\label{sec:robot_software_setup_contact}

The experiment is performed on the Franka Emika Panda using the torque-control
interface provided by libfranka. The controller runs inside the Franka Control
Interface with a control period of

\begin{equation}
T_s = 1\,\mathrm{ms}.
\label{eq:sampling_time_contact}
\end{equation}

At each control cycle, the current robot state is read, the Cartesian
impedance control law is evaluated, and the resulting joint torque command is
sent back to the robot. The controller uses the end-effector transformation
\(T_{\mathrm{EE}}\), the joint velocity vector \(\dot{q}\), the geometric
Jacobian \(J(q)\), and the model-based Coriolis term provided by the libfranka
model interface. The commanded torque has the form

\begin{equation}
\tau_{\mathrm{cmd}}
=
J^\top(q)\mathbf{F}_{\mathrm{cmd}}
+
\tau_{\mathrm{null}}
+
\tau_c(q,\dot{q}),
\label{eq:chapter4_contact_commanded_torque}
\end{equation}

where \(\mathbf{F}_{\mathrm{cmd}}\) is the commanded Cartesian impedance
wrench and \(\tau_{\mathrm{null}}\) is the projected null-space torque. The
gravity compensation is not added as a separate term in the user callback,
because the external torque command sent through libfranka is already handled
with robot-side gravity and motor-friction compensation by the Franka Control
Interface.

\section{Contact Geometry}
\label{sec:contact_geometry}

The task geometry is defined by a table plane, a tool edge, and a desired
virtual center of rotation. Let

\begin{equation}
n_s \in \mathbb{R}^3,
\qquad
\|n_s\| = 1
\label{eq:surface_normal}
\end{equation}

be the table normal expressed in the robot base frame. Two orthonormal
tangent directions \(t_1\) and \(t_2\) span the table plane. With the normal as
the first column, the task frame is therefore

\begin{equation}
R_s
=
\begin{bmatrix}
n_s & t_1 & t_2
\end{bmatrix},
\label{eq:surface_task_frame}
\end{equation}

consistent with the surface task frame \(R_{\mathrm{surface}}\) of
\cref{eq:surface_task_frame_matrix} and the implementation, where the normal,
the first tangent, and the second tangent are columns one, two, and three.

The tool edge is represented by a point \(p_e\) on the edge and an edge
direction \(a_e\). The virtual center of rotation is represented by a point
\(p_c\). For passive alignment, \(p_c\) should be chosen near the contact
region. A useful first choice is the point on the table below or close to the
tool edge, because this makes the desired motion resemble the behavior of a
remote center of compliance used in assembly tasks.

The lever arm from the virtual center to the end-effector/tool frame is

\begin{equation}
r_c
=
p_{\mathrm{EE}} - p_c.
\label{eq:contact_virtual_center_lever_arm}
\end{equation}

The lower-case subscript \(c\) is used here for the chosen virtual center
\(p_c\). Later, the upper-case subscript \(C\) is used for the physical
contact point \(p_C\) and the measured or estimated contact wrench \(W_C\).

If a force \(\mathbf{f}\) acts at the contact, the corresponding moment about
the end-effector depends on this lever arm. Therefore, the selected stiffness
matrix should not be interpreted only as three independent translational
springs and three independent rotational springs. For a virtual-center
behavior, translation and rotation must be selected consistently.

\section{Cartesian Stiffness Selection}
\label{sec:cartesian_stiffness_selection}

The complete local Cartesian stiffness matrix in the task frame can be written
as

\begin{equation}
K_s
=
\begin{bmatrix}
K_{pp} & K_{pR}\\
K_{Rp} & K_{RR}
\end{bmatrix}.
\label{eq:contact_stiffness_block}
\end{equation}

Here, \(K_{pp}\) relates translational displacement to force,
\(K_{RR}\) relates rotational displacement to moment, and the off-diagonal
blocks \(K_{pR}\) and \(K_{Rp}\) describe translation--rotation coupling.
This block form is useful for explaining the physical coupling between sliding
and rotation during edge contact. In the main experimental method of this
thesis, however, a full coupled stiffness matrix is not identified or optimized
analytically. Instead, a small set of manually selected Cartesian stiffness
settings is tested and compared experimentally. This keeps the method
transparent and compatible with the implemented controller.

For a passive linear stiffness, the complete matrix should be symmetric
positive definite,

\begin{equation}
K_s = K_s^\top,
\qquad
K_s \succ 0.
\label{eq:contact_stiffness_spd}
\end{equation}

This condition is important because it allows the stiffness to be interpreted
as stored elastic energy,

\begin{equation}
V_K
=
\frac{1}{2}
\Delta x^\top
K_s
\Delta x,
\qquad
\Delta x =
\begin{bmatrix}
\Delta p\\
\Delta \theta
\end{bmatrix}.
\label{eq:contact_elastic_energy}
\end{equation}

This energy interpretation is also the reason why the thesis separates the
geometric usefulness of a stiffness setting from its safety. A setting can
create a plausible virtual center in the measured motion but still be rejected
if it stores too much energy, creates large torque peaks, or requires damping
that is not reliable in contact \citep{Hjorth2023EnergyAwareImpedance}.

The commanded stiffness settings used for the main evaluation are first
computed from the quasi-static estimates in
Section~\ref{subsec:initial_stiffness_damping_values}. They are then grouped
into a small number of diagonal candidate matrices,

\begin{equation}
K_s^{(j)}
=
\mathrm{diag}
\left(
k_x^{(j)},k_y^{(j)},k_z^{(j)},
k_{rx}^{(j)},k_{ry}^{(j)},k_{rz}^{(j)}
\right),
\qquad
j=1,\dots,N_K .
\label{eq:candidate_diagonal_stiffness}
\end{equation}

The diagonal terms define the basic directional behavior. In the table-normal
direction, the stiffness should be high enough to avoid excessive penetration
or sagging, but not so high that the contact wrench creates abrupt torque
peaks. In the tangent directions, lower stiffness can be used when small
sliding or backward motion is acceptable. In the rotational directions, the
stiffness should be lower around the axes that allow the edge to align with
the table and higher around axes that should remain controlled.

This means that the evaluation does not search for a globally optimal
stiffness matrix in the mathematical sense. Instead, the quasi-static
force-displacement and moment-angle relations are used to compute practical
starting values. Around these starting values, the controller is tested with a
small number of meaningful stiffness configurations, for example high
rotational stiffness, medium rotational stiffness, low rotational stiffness,
and low rotational stiffness with additional lateral compliance. The best
setting is then selected from the measured behavior. This is intentionally
simpler than full impedance planning, where stiffness and damping can be
computed from task constraints, robot inertia, and robustness requirements
\citep{Pollayil2023ImpedancePlanning}. Here, the planning idea is used to
turn contact limits into practical candidate gains for the self-alignment
experiment.

\subsection{Initial Stiffness and Damping Values}
\label{subsec:initial_stiffness_damping_values}

The candidate stiffness values are initialized from simple contact
requirements. Since the edge-contact experiment is performed at low velocity,
the first stiffness estimate is based on a quasi-static approximation. The
damping and inertial terms are neglected only for this initial estimate, so
the translational contact relation is approximated by

\begin{equation}
F_i
\approx
k_i \Delta x_i .
\label{eq:quasi_static_translational_stiffness}
\end{equation}

If the maximum acceptable force in direction \(i\) is \(F_{i,\max}\), and the
allowed compliant displacement is \(\Delta x_{i,\max}\), the corresponding
starting stiffness is

\begin{equation}
k_i
=
\frac{F_{i,\max}}{\Delta x_{i,\max}} .
\label{eq:initial_translational_stiffness}
\end{equation}

For example, if the table-normal force should stay near
\(15\,\mathrm{N}\) and the allowed normal displacement is
\(5\,\mathrm{mm}=0.005\,\mathrm{m}\), then

\begin{equation}
k_z
=
\frac{15}{0.005}
=
3000\,\mathrm{N/m}.
\label{eq:normal_stiffness_example}
\end{equation}

The same idea is used for the lateral directions. If a lateral force of
\(10\,\mathrm{N}\) should allow approximately \(10\,\mathrm{mm}\) of sliding
or backward motion, then

\begin{equation}
k_x
=
k_y
\approx
\frac{10}{0.01}
=
1000\,\mathrm{N/m}.
\label{eq:lateral_stiffness_example}
\end{equation}

The rotational stiffness is initialized from the contact moment and the
allowed angular deflection. If the contact edge is separated from the tool
frame origin by the lever-arm length \(l\), the moment magnitude can be
estimated as

\begin{equation}
M_C
\approx
l F_C .
\label{eq:contact_moment_lever_estimate}
\end{equation}

The rotational stiffness around a relevant alignment axis is then chosen as

\begin{equation}
k_{r,i}
=
\frac{M_{i,\max}}{\Delta \theta_{i,\max}} .
\label{eq:initial_rotational_stiffness}
\end{equation}

For example, with \(l=0.05\,\mathrm{m}\) and \(F_C=15\,\mathrm{N}\), the
estimated contact moment is \(M_C \approx 0.75\,\mathrm{Nm}\). If the tool
should be allowed to rotate by about \(10^\circ = 0.1745\,\mathrm{rad}\),
then

\begin{equation}
k_r
\approx
\frac{0.75}{0.1745}
\approx
4.3\,\mathrm{Nm/rad}.
\label{eq:rotational_stiffness_example}
\end{equation}

If only \(5^\circ = 0.0873\,\mathrm{rad}\) is allowed under the same moment,
the resulting stiffness is approximately \(8.6\,\mathrm{Nm/rad}\). These
values are not treated as exact optima. They define reasonable starting
points for the experimental stiffness set.

The damping values are initialized from the critical-damping relation of a
single mass-spring-damper system~\citep{Ogata2010},

\begin{equation}
d_i
=
2\zeta\sqrt{M_i k_i},
\label{eq:critical_damping_with_mass}
\end{equation}

where \(M_i\) is the effective inertia in direction \(i\) and \(\zeta\) is
the damping ratio. In the implemented controller, the Cartesian inertia is not
explicitly shaped for each direction. Therefore, the practical initial value
uses the common normalized-inertia approximation

\begin{equation}
d_i
\approx
2\sqrt{k_i}.
\label{eq:normalized_critical_damping}
\end{equation}

This approximation corresponds to setting the effective inertia to
\(M_i=1\); it is therefore not strictly dimensionally consistent and is used
only as a starting point. The damping values are
then checked experimentally by verifying that the table-contact response is
not oscillatory and that the commanded torques remain smooth.

The virtual-center interpretation is still important for understanding why
the stiffness values affect alignment. A small rotation around a virtual
center produces a related translation of the end-effector,

\begin{equation}
\Delta p
\approx
\Delta \theta \times r_c
=
-
[r_c]_\times \Delta \theta.
\label{eq:virtual_center_kinematic_coupling}
\end{equation}

Therefore, a fully coupled virtual-center stiffness could be constructed by
penalizing the motion of the selected center point rather than only the
end-effector origin. For a point \(p_c\), the displacement of that point is
approximated by

\begin{equation}
\Delta p_c
=
\Delta p
+
[r_c]_\times \Delta \theta.
\label{eq:center_point_displacement}
\end{equation}

Penalizing \(\Delta p_c\) with a translational stiffness \(K_c\) gives the
energy term

\begin{equation}
V_c
=
\frac{1}{2}
\Delta p_c^\top
K_c
\Delta p_c.
\label{eq:center_point_energy}
\end{equation}

This expression generates coupled stiffness terms between translation and
rotation. The coupling is what allows the contact wrench to produce a motion
that appears to rotate around the selected center. The remaining rotational
stiffness \(K_R\) can then be added to keep rotations bounded:

\begin{equation}
V
=
\frac{1}{2}
\left(
\Delta p + [r_c]_\times \Delta \theta
\right)^\top
K_c
\left(
\Delta p + [r_c]_\times \Delta \theta
\right)
+
\frac{1}{2}
\Delta \theta^\top
K_R
\Delta \theta.
\label{eq:virtual_center_total_energy}
\end{equation}

Equation~\eqref{eq:virtual_center_total_energy} explains the more general
coupled stiffness idea. In the present thesis it is used as interpretation,
not as the main tuning algorithm. The practical tuning procedure remains:
choose several representative \(K_s^{(j)}\), run the same edge-contact
experiment for each setting, and compare the measured alignment, displacement,
force, and torque behavior directly.

\section{Simple Contact Model}
\label{sec:simple_contact_model}

The contact experiment is interpreted with the same Cartesian impedance
notation used in the controller derivation. The commanded impedance wrench is

\begin{equation}
W_{\mathrm{imp}}
=
K_s
\begin{bmatrix}
\Delta p\\
\Delta \theta
\end{bmatrix}
+
D_s
\begin{bmatrix}
v\\
\omega
\end{bmatrix},
\qquad
W_{\mathrm{imp}}
=
\begin{bmatrix}
f\\
m
\end{bmatrix}.
\label{eq:contact_impedance_wrench_simple}
\end{equation}

Here, \(K_s\) and \(D_s\) denote the stiffness and damping matrices expressed
in the same task frame.
The scalar entries \(K_i\) and \(D_i\) used below are the corresponding
principal-direction values of these matrices.

For a slow contact motion, the quasi-static relation can be written as

\begin{equation}
\begin{bmatrix}
\Delta p\\
\Delta \theta
\end{bmatrix}
\approx
K_s^{-1} W_C,
\qquad
W_C
=
\begin{bmatrix}
f_C\\
m_C
\end{bmatrix},
\label{eq:contact_quasistatic_compliance}
\end{equation}

where \(W_C\) is the contact wrench. This equation is not used to compute the
controller command directly. It is used to explain the physical effect of the
selected stiffness: for the same type of contact wrench, lower stiffness
allows a larger displacement and higher stiffness resists the motion more
strongly.

If the contact force acts at the tool edge, it also creates a moment. With
\(p_C\) denoting the contact point and \(p_{\mathrm{EE}}\) the end-effector
origin, the contact lever arm is

\begin{equation}
r_C
=
p_C
-
p_{\mathrm{EE}} .
\label{eq:contact_edge_lever_arm}
\end{equation}

The moment caused by the contact force is then

\begin{equation}
m_C
=
r_C
\times
f_C .
\label{eq:contact_edge_moment}
\end{equation}

This relation explains why the table contact can produce rotation even when
the controller is commanded through a Cartesian impedance law at the
end-effector. The force does not act exactly at the end-effector origin; it
acts at the edge. Therefore, the contact produces both a force and a moment.
The observed tool motion is consequently not a pure rotation and not a pure
translation, but a combined screw-like displacement.

\section{Damping and Safety Limits}
\label{sec:damping_safety_limits_contact}

The damping matrix \(D\) is selected to dissipate energy and reduce
oscillation after contact. In impedance planning, damping cannot be treated as
an afterthought to stiffness, because both gains shape the transient response,
the energy stored in the virtual spring, and the response to disturbances
\citep{Pollayil2023ImpedancePlanning}. For each principal direction, the critical damping
reference is

\begin{equation}
D_i
=
2\sqrt{M_iK_i},
\label{eq:critical_damping_contact}
\end{equation}

where \(M_i\) is the corresponding directional entry of the effective
Cartesian inertia and \(K_i\) is the selected stiffness. This expression is
used as a reference rather than as an automatic tuning rule, because the real
controller is discrete, torque limited, affected by model uncertainty, and
interacts with a stiff environment.

The following safety checks are part of the experiment:

\begin{itemize}
    \item the commanded torque must remain below the selected torque limit;
    \item the torque-rate limiter must prevent abrupt command changes;
    \item the tool displacement must stay within a small, predefined contact
    range;
    \item the rotational displacement must remain below the selected angular
    limit;
    \item the controller must not generate sustained oscillations after the
    tool touches or leaves the table.
\end{itemize}

These checks are necessary because a stiffness matrix that is geometrically
meaningful can still be unsuitable for real hardware if it produces aggressive
torques or poorly damped contact behavior. Energy-aware impedance control
studies make the same point from a safety perspective: contact tasks should
limit not only position error, but also the energy and power that the
controller can inject during unexpected interaction
\citep{Hjorth2023EnergyAwareImpedance}.

\section{Logged Data}
\label{sec:logged_data_contact}

During the experiment, selected measured and computed quantities are written
to a CSV file for offline evaluation. The main logged quantities are

\begin{equation}
\begin{aligned}
&
\mathbf{p}_{\mathrm{EE}},
R_{\mathrm{EE}},
\mathbf{p}_d,
R_d,
\mathbf{e}_p,
\mathbf{e}_R,
\dot{\mathbf{p}}_{\mathrm{EE}},
\omega_{\mathrm{EE}},
\\
&
\mathbf{f},
\mathbf{m},
\mathbf{f}_C,
\mathbf{m}_C,
\boldsymbol{\tau}.
\end{aligned}
\label{eq:logged_quantities_contact}
\end{equation}

The vectors \(\mathbf{f}\) and \(\mathbf{m}\) are the Cartesian force and
moment generated by the impedance controller. No dedicated external
force/torque sensor is mounted on the robot. Instead, the controller uses the
external wrench estimated by the robot itself, \texttt{O\_F\_ext\_hat\_K},
which is reconstructed from the joint torque sensors and the dynamic model.
This estimate is used for contact detection during the experiment and can be
logged as \(\mathbf{f}_C\) and \(\mathbf{m}_C\). Because it is a model-based
estimate rather than a direct measurement at the contact, it is less accurate
than a dedicated force/torque sensor, especially during fast motion or near
singular configurations. The passive alignment behavior is therefore evaluated
from the measured pose displacement, the computed impedance wrench, the
commanded joint torques, and this estimated external wrench. This limitation
must be kept in mind when interpreting the results. More elaborate sensorless
approaches can additionally estimate environment stiffness for impedance
tuning, including on the Franka Emika Panda, but such estimation is outside the
implemented scope of the present thesis \citep{Roveda2021SensorlessStiffness}.

\section{Screw-Motion Evaluation}
\label{sec:screw_motion_evaluation}

The measured displacement during contact contains both translation and
rotation. Over a short contact interval, the measured pose change is written
as

\begin{equation}
\Delta x_C
=
\begin{bmatrix}
\Delta p_C\\
\Delta \theta
\end{bmatrix},
\label{eq:contact_displacement_vector}
\end{equation}

where \(\Delta p_C\) is the translational displacement associated with the
contact interval and \(\Delta\theta\) is the small angular displacement. If
\(\|\Delta\theta\|>0\), the instantaneous pole of the measured motion can be
approximated using the screw-theory description of rigid-body
motion~\citep{Murray1994} by

\begin{equation}
r_{\mathrm{inst}}
=
\frac{
\Delta\theta
\times
\Delta p_C
}{
\|\Delta\theta\|^2
}.
\label{eq:instantaneous_pole_displacement}
\end{equation}

For the corresponding velocity representation, the same idea can be written
with the measured linear and angular velocities as

\begin{equation}
\xi
=
\begin{bmatrix}
v\\
\omega
\end{bmatrix},
\qquad
p_{\mathrm{ISA}}
=
\frac{\omega \times v}{\|\omega\|^2},
\qquad
a_{\mathrm{ISA}}
=
\frac{\omega}{\|\omega\|}.
\label{eq:contact_twist_and_isa}
\end{equation}

Both forms describe the same physical idea: the tool does not only rotate and
does not only translate. It moves in a screw-like way. The instantaneous pole
or screw axis gives a compact geometric description of the motion caused by
contact.

The desired geometric behavior is that the observed pole lies close to the
tool edge or the selected virtual center. The pole error is therefore

\begin{equation}
e_P
=
\left\|
r_{\mathrm{inst}}
-
r_P
\right\|.
\label{eq:pole_error_metric}
\end{equation}

Here \(r_P\) is the desired pole location, for example a point on the tool
edge or the selected virtual center. A smaller value of \(e_P\) means that the
measured motion is closer to the desired pivot behavior. If the velocity-based
screw axis is used instead of \(r_{\mathrm{inst}}\), the distance from the
axis to the selected virtual center is computed as

\begin{equation}
d_{\mathrm{ISA},c}
=
\left\|
\left(
p_c - p_{\mathrm{ISA}}
\right)
\times
a_{\mathrm{ISA}}
\right\|.
\label{eq:isa_center_distance}
\end{equation}

This metric is evaluated together with the angular error, displacement
magnitude, contact force, and torque signals.

\section{Alignment Evaluation}
\label{sec:alignment_evaluation}

The orientation error is evaluated using the same rotation-error notation as
the controller. For the contact-alignment task, it can also be interpreted
geometrically from the tool normal and the table normal. If \(z_T\) is the
tool normal and \(n_s\) is the table normal, a simple local alignment error is

\begin{equation}
\mathbf{e}_R
=
z_T
\times
n_s .
\label{eq:contact_alignment_error_cross}
\end{equation}

Good passive alignment means that the contact-induced motion reduces this
orientation error. Therefore, the main before--after condition is

\begin{equation}
\|\mathbf{e}_{R,\mathrm{after}}\|
<
\|\mathbf{e}_{R,\mathrm{before}}\|.
\label{eq:alignment_error_reduction}
\end{equation}

Equivalently, the improvement can be written as

\begin{equation}
\Delta e_R
=
\|\mathbf{e}_{R,\mathrm{before}}\|
-
\|\mathbf{e}_{R,\mathrm{after}}\|.
\label{eq:alignment_error_improvement}
\end{equation}

If \(\Delta e_R>0\), the alignment improved during contact. The passive
rotation itself is described by

\begin{equation}
\Delta\theta_C
=
\theta_{\mathrm{after}}
-
\theta_{\mathrm{before}} .
\label{eq:contact_induced_rotation}
\end{equation}

With the sign convention used in this thesis, the contact-induced rotation is
considered favorable if it acts against the initial orientation error,

\begin{equation}
\mathbf{e}_{R,\mathrm{before}}^\top
\Delta\theta_C
<
0.
\label{eq:passive_rotation_condition}
\end{equation}

This condition is used only as a local alignment indicator. The final
judgement also includes the pole location, force level, torque level, and
oscillation behavior.

\section{Experimental Procedure}
\label{sec:experimental_procedure_contact}

The experiment is executed in the following steps:

\begin{enumerate}
    \item Move the Panda to a safe initial pose above the table.
    \item Define the table frame, tool edge frame, and selected virtual center
    \(p_c\).
    \item Select one candidate stiffness matrix \(K_s^{(j)}\) from the
    manually defined stiffness set.
    \item Select damping values and torque limits.
    \item Move the tool edge slowly into contact with the table.
    \item Record the pose displacement, impedance wrench, and commanded joint
    torques during contact.
    \item Estimate the instantaneous pole or screw axis from the measured
    translation and rotation.
    \item Compare the orientation-error reduction, pole location, displacement,
    force, and torque signals against the design goal.
\end{enumerate}

The same procedure is repeated for each candidate stiffness setting. The
comparison is not meant to prove a universal optimal stiffness matrix. Instead,
it identifies which of the tested settings produces the most useful passive
self-aligning tendency for the chosen tool, table, and contact configuration.
The stiffness values are therefore selected experimentally rather than through
an analytical optimization.

\section{Evaluation Criteria}
\label{sec:evaluation_criteria_contact}

The experiment is evaluated using the following criteria:

\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Alignment direction:} the measured rotation should reduce the
    angular error between the tool edge and the table.
    \item \textbf{Virtual-center consistency:} the instantaneous screw axis
    should pass close to the selected virtual center or the useful contact
    region.
    \item \textbf{Contact displacement:} the translational displacement should
    remain small and bounded.
    \item \textbf{Torque behavior:} the commanded torques should remain smooth,
    bounded, and below the selected limits.
    \item \textbf{Damping behavior:} after contact or release, the response
    should not show sustained oscillations.
\end{itemize}

These criteria match the goal of the experiment more directly than the earlier
set-point and trajectory metrics. The central result is not tracking accuracy
alone, but whether the selected Cartesian stiffness matrix creates a safe and
useful passive alignment behavior during contact.

\section{Quasi-Static Stiffness Computation and Direct Comparison}
\label{sec:direct_stiffness_comparison}

The first evaluation method computes initial commanded stiffness and damping
values from a quasi-static compliance assumption and then compares the
resulting settings experimentally. For slow contact, the damping and inertial
terms are small compared with the elastic term, so the principal stiffness in
one direction can be estimated from a force-displacement pair,

\begin{equation}
k_i
\approx
\frac{F_{i,\max}}{\Delta x_{i,\max}} .
\label{eq:direct_method_translational_stiffness}
\end{equation}

Similarly, the rotational stiffness is estimated from an allowable contact
moment and angular deflection,

\begin{equation}
k_{r,i}
\approx
\frac{M_{i,\max}}{\Delta\theta_{i,\max}} .
\label{eq:direct_method_rotational_stiffness}
\end{equation}

These equations can be used with design limits before the experiment, or with
measured contact data after a trial to compute the apparent stiffness that was
actually observed. For example, if the measured contact force and displacement
in direction \(i\) are \(F_i\) and \(\Delta x_i\), then
\(k_i \approx F_i/\Delta x_i\). If the measured contact moment and angular
deflection are \(M_i\) and \(\Delta\theta_i\), then
\(k_{r,i}\approx M_i/\Delta\theta_i\).

The damping values are initialized from the critical-damping approximation,

\begin{equation}
d_i
\approx
2\sqrt{k_i},
\label{eq:direct_method_critical_damping}
\end{equation}

or, when a directional effective inertia \(M_i\) is available,
\(d_i=2\zeta\sqrt{M_i k_i}\). These damping values are not assumed to be final
optimal values. They are starting values that are checked experimentally for
smooth contact, bounded torque, and absence of sustained oscillation.

After the stiffness and damping candidates have been computed, each setting is
tested in the same edge-contact experiment and judged from the measured values
listed in
Table~\ref{tab:stiffness_comparison_template}.

\section{Least-Squares Identification of Effective Stiffness and Damping}
\label{sec:effective_stiffness_damping_identification}

The second evaluation method identifies an effective contact model from the
logged table-contact data. It is included as part of the thesis work because
the direct stiffness comparison tells which commanded setting behaves better,
but it does not explain how much of the measured self-alignment moment comes
from translational sliding, translational damping, rotational stiffness, or
rotational damping.

During edge contact, the passive alignment behavior is not governed only by
the rotational stiffness. Since the tool can also slide and translate due to
the contact geometry, translational motion contributes to the generated
moment. The contact moment is therefore modeled as the lower rotational part
of an effective Cartesian impedance relation. The identified matrices are
called effective matrices because they describe the behavior observed during
contact; they are not necessarily identical to the diagonal gains commanded in
the controller.

At one sample \(i\), the contact moment around the contact point or tool edge
can be modeled as

\begin{equation}
M_{C,i}
=
K_{rt}^{\mathrm{eff}}\Delta x_{C,i}
+
D_{rt}^{\mathrm{eff}}v_{C,i}
+
K_R^{\mathrm{eff}}\Delta\theta_i
+
D_R^{\mathrm{eff}}\omega_i .
\label{eq:effective_contact_moment_sample}
\end{equation}

Here, \(\Delta x_{C,i}\) is the translation or sliding displacement of the
contact point, \(v_{C,i}\) is the corresponding linear velocity,
\(\Delta\theta_i\) is the angular displacement, and \(\omega_i\) is the
angular velocity. The contact moment \(M_{C,i}\) may be obtained from a
measured wrench, or, in the present setup, estimated from the logged impedance
wrench and the known contact geometry. The term
\(K_{rt}^{\mathrm{eff}}\Delta x_{C,i}\) represents the moment contribution
caused by translational sliding, while
\(K_R^{\mathrm{eff}}\Delta\theta_i\) represents the usual rotational
impedance contribution. The two damping terms describe moment contributions
caused by linear and angular velocity.

One sample is not enough to identify all matrices. For \(N\) samples, define

\begin{equation}
\phi_i
=
\begin{bmatrix}
\Delta x_{C,i}\\
v_{C,i}\\
\Delta\theta_i\\
\omega_i
\end{bmatrix}
\in
\mathbb{R}^{12},
\label{eq:effective_coupling_regressor}
\end{equation}

and

\begin{equation}
A
=
\begin{bmatrix}
K_{rt}^{\mathrm{eff}} &
D_{rt}^{\mathrm{eff}} &
K_R^{\mathrm{eff}} &
D_R^{\mathrm{eff}}
\end{bmatrix}
\in
\mathbb{R}^{3\times 12}.
\label{eq:effective_coupling_matrix}
\end{equation}

The sample equation is then

\begin{equation}
M_{C,i}
=
A\phi_i .
\label{eq:effective_coupling_compact_sample}
\end{equation}

Stacking all samples gives

\begin{equation}
Y
=
\Phi A^\top,
\label{eq:effective_coupling_stacked}
\end{equation}

with

\begin{equation}
Y
=
\begin{bmatrix}
M_{C,1}^\top\\
M_{C,2}^\top\\
\vdots\\
M_{C,N}^\top
\end{bmatrix},
\qquad
\Phi
=
\begin{bmatrix}
\phi_1^\top\\
\phi_2^\top\\
\vdots\\
\phi_N^\top
\end{bmatrix}.
\label{eq:effective_coupling_stacked_matrices}
\end{equation}

The least-squares estimate is

\begin{equation}
A^\top
=
\Phi^\# Y,
\label{eq:effective_coupling_pseudoinverse}
\end{equation}

where \(\Phi^\#\) is the pseudoinverse of \(\Phi\). If \(\Phi^\top\Phi\) is
well conditioned, the same estimate can be written as

\begin{equation}
A^\top
=
\left(
\Phi^\top\Phi
\right)^{-1}
\Phi^\top Y.
\label{eq:effective_coupling_normal_equation}
\end{equation}

The matrix \(A\) can then be split into \(K_{rt}^{\mathrm{eff}}\),
\(D_{rt}^{\mathrm{eff}}\), \(K_R^{\mathrm{eff}}\), and
\(D_R^{\mathrm{eff}}\). These blocks are the effective stiffness and damping
matrices computed by the fit.

The identified coupling is useful even when the commanded matrix
\(K_s^{(j)}\) is diagonal. In that case, the commanded off-diagonal stiffness
is zero, but effective coupling can still appear because of the contact
geometry, the tool offset, the Jacobian, friction, table constraints,
null-space behavior, torque limits, and the fact that the contact force acts
at the tool edge rather than at the end-effector origin.

The identified model separates the two stiffness-related moment contributions

\begin{equation}
M_{\mathrm{trans}}
=
K_{rt}^{\mathrm{eff}}\Delta x_C,
\qquad
M_{\mathrm{rot}}
=
K_R^{\mathrm{eff}}\Delta\theta
\label{eq:effective_moment_contributions}
\end{equation}

so that the observed self-alignment can be interpreted more precisely. If
\(\|M_{\mathrm{trans}}\|\) is large, then sliding and translation are an
important part of the passive alignment behavior. If
\(\|M_{\mathrm{rot}}\|\) dominates, then the behavior is mainly explained by
rotational compliance. The fitted damping matrices \(D_{rt}^{\mathrm{eff}}\)
and \(D_R^{\mathrm{eff}}\) are used in the same way to check whether velocity
terms are significant during the contact transient.

This least-squares model is not used to command the robot online in this
thesis. Instead, it is an offline analysis method for computing effective
stiffness and damping contributions from the same logged contact trials used
for the direct stiffness comparison. The two methods therefore answer
different questions: the quasi-static method computes and selects a useful
commanded stiffness and damping setting, while the least-squares fit computes the effective
translation--rotation and damping behavior that produced the observed
self-alignment.

\section{Results}
\label{sec:results_contact}

This section reports the table-contact experiment for the candidate stiffness
settings of Table~\ref{tab:example_candidate_stiffness_sets}. The qualitative
trends described below follow directly from the contact physics and are stated
as the expected behavior; the numerical values and the plots are completed from
the logged data. Quantities still to be inserted are marked
\texttt{[\,\ldots\,]} in the tables, and the figures are placeholders for the
corresponding logged signals.

\subsection{Passive Orientation Alignment}
\label{sec:results_alignment}

The decisive variable is the rotational stiffness about the alignment axes.
With high rotational stiffness (Test~1), the controller holds the commanded
orientation and resists the contact-induced rotation, so the contact moment is
largely reacted as joint torque and the tool barely rotates. The residual
misalignment stays close to the initial value, \(\Delta e_R\approx0\), and the
peak contact force at the leading edge is high. As the rotational stiffness is
reduced (Tests~2 and~3), the moment produced by the downward force at the tool
edge increasingly rotates the tool toward the surface, so \(\Delta e_R\) becomes
positive and the orientation error decreases while the peak contact force drops.
Adding lateral compliance (Test~4) lets the tool both slide and rotate, which
is expected to give the most complete passive alignment. With an over-soft
setting (Test~5), the displacement becomes large and the alignment motion
poorly defined, so this setting is expected to be rejected on the safety and
displacement criteria.

\subsection{Instantaneous Pole and Virtual-Center Consistency}
\label{sec:results_pole}

The instantaneous pole of the measured motion
(\cref{eq:instantaneous_pole_displacement}) indicates where the passive motion
appears to pivot. With stiff rotation, the small rotation occurs essentially
about the tool-frame origin, so the pole lies far from the contact edge and the
pole error \(e_P\) is large. With compliant rotation and added lateral
compliance, the pole is expected to migrate toward the contact edge and the
table surface, reducing \(e_P\) and matching the useful passive pivot near the
contact. This is the geometric signature of good passive self-alignment.

\subsection{Contact Displacement, Force, and Commanded Torque}
\label{sec:results_force_torque}

The normal penetration scales approximately as
\(\Delta x_n\approx \|f_C\|/K_n\), so a stiffer normal direction gives a smaller
displacement but a larger peak contact force, while a more compliant setting
gives the opposite. The peak commanded joint torque grows with the commanded
wrench through \(\boldsymbol{\tau}=J^\top\mathbf{F}\), so the stiffer settings
produce the largest torque peaks. All settings are expected to remain below the
selected joint-torque limit, and the torque-rate limiter keeps the command
smooth across the contact transient.

contact response is expected to settle without sustained oscillation. An
under-damped setting would show oscillation in the orientation error and the
contact force after the tool touches or leaves the table; the selected damping
values are checked against this on the logged signals.

\section{Discussion}
\label{sec:discussion_contact}

The expected trade-off is the same physical trade-off that appears throughout
Cartesian impedance control. Higher stiffness improves pose regulation but
increases contact forces and commanded torques. Lower stiffness improves
compliance but can allow excessive displacement and may make the alignment
motion poorly defined. For the table-contact task, the important design
question is not simply whether the robot is soft or stiff. The important
question is where the compliance appears to be located geometrically.

A purely diagonal stiffness matrix can make the robot compliant, but it does
not directly encode a useful virtual center of rotation. In that case, the
contact wrench may still produce useful alignment because the contact force
acts at the tool edge and because the table constrains the motion. However,
the effective pivot point must then be evaluated from the measured motion
rather than assumed from the commanded stiffness matrix. This is why the
instantaneous pole or screw-axis evaluation is important in the direct
experimental comparison. This interpretation is consistent with the assembly
literature, where remote or variable compliance centers are useful precisely
because they turn contact forces into correcting motion instead of jamming
\citep{Nevins1989ComputerControlledAssembly,Watson1978RCC,Wang2019VariableComplianceCenter,Hartisch2022FlexureRCC}.

A fully coupled stiffness matrix based on a selected center point would encode
the virtual-center behavior more directly. The least-squares identification in
Section~\ref{sec:effective_stiffness_damping_identification} is therefore used
to analyze the effective coupling that appears during contact, even when the
commanded controller gains are first computed from quasi-static contact
limits and then validated experimentally. For the present thesis, the final
stiffness selection should satisfy four practical requirements. It should
reduce the orientation error during contact. It should produce an
instantaneous pole near the useful contact region. It must be damped enough to
avoid oscillation. Finally, it must remain compatible with the Panda torque
limits and the discrete real-time implementation.
