\chapter{Experimental Methodology}
\label{ch:methodology}

\section{Experimental Investigation}
\label{sec:experimental_question}

Four parameters were investigated: the commanded tilt, the rotational stiffness
about each surface tangent, the translational stiffness perpendicular to the
tilted tangent, and the position of the virtual centre of compliance. Each can
change the rotation observed during contact. The commanded tilt sets the initial
angular error. The two stiffness entries determine how strongly the controller
resists the resulting translational and rotational motion. The compliance-centre
position determines how much of the translational contact response is converted
into a moment, through the point-shifted impedance developed in the preceding
chapters.

Two things separate these measurements from the earlier campaign preserved in
\cref{app:previous_campaign}. The surface plane and the grinding-face axis were
calibrated independently of one another, which defines the commanded tilt
against a measured physical surface frame instead of against a configured plane
that differed from it. The rotational stiffness also moved on one tangent at a
time. A paired sweep cannot tell the \(t_1\) and \(t_2\) contributions apart;
varying the entries singly does.

Three probed points fitted the plane, and a fourth held out of the fit lay
\(0.82\,\mathrm{mm}\) from it. A separate set of seatings calibrated the
grinding-face normal, leaving a held-out angular residual of \(0.50^\circ\).
Projecting the direction from the first probed point to the second gives
\(+t_1\), with \(t_2=n_s\times t_1\).

Every condition shares one baseline impedance. Its translational block is
diagonal in base coordinates and its rotational block in the surface frame,
\[
  K_p=\operatorname{diag}(2000,2000,350)\,\mathrm{N/m}\ \text{(base }xyz),
  \qquad
  K_R=\operatorname{diag}(5,5,50)\,\mathrm{Nm/rad}\ \text{(surface)},
\]
the values derived in \cref{eq:setup_base_z_stiffness_design} and
\cref{eq:setup_rotational_stiffness_design},
together with a \(60\,\mathrm{mm}\) virtual press at \(50\,\mathrm{mm/s}\) and a
\(4\,\mathrm{s}\) set-up timeout. Projected null-space damping and the
singular-value-based torque were both active during all reported runs, in the
combined mode of \cref{tab:nullspace_safety_parameters}. Fixing the commanded
spin of the
grinding face at \(115.05^\circ\) from \(t_1\) makes the leading contact feature
follow from the calibrated surface frame rather than from the initial joint
configuration.

The runs fall into five cases. Case A measures the response to the commanded
tilt by itself and is the baseline the rest are read against. Cases B and C
each move one stiffness entry, Case D moves only the compliance-centre lever,
and Case E repeats the Case-A tilt about the axes of the tool face rather than
the surface tangents.

Cases A--E together hold \(75\) contact runs, all of which completed set-up and
all of which are analysed. The null-space records do not form a sixth case: no
matched conditioning-torque comparison was performed, so there is nothing to
compare them against.

Each run followed the same sequence. The robot oriented the grinding-face axis,
then descended to a geometrically defined clearance. Set-up pressed the
selected leading tool feature into the plane, holding the clearance orientation
as a soft reference and commanding no time-varying task-space tipping
trajectory, and the grinding motion followed. Evaluation drew on the
EE-inferred tool-axis angle, the rotation during contact, and the displacements
of the feature and the TCP.
\section{Robot and Software Configuration}
\label{sec:robot_software_setup_contact}

The experiments ran on the Franka Emika Panda through the libfranka
torque-control interface, with the controller executing inside the Franka
Control Interface at the nominal control period

\begin{equation}
T_s = 1\,\mathrm{ms}.
\label{eq:sampling_time_contact}
\end{equation}

One cycle retrieves the robot state, evaluates the Cartesian impedance control
law, and returns a joint torque command. The evaluation draws on the
end-effector transformation \(T_{\mathrm{EE}}\) and the joint velocity
\(\dot{q}\) from the state, and on the geometric Jacobian \(J(q)\) and the
Coriolis term from the libfranka model interface. The commanded torque has the
form

\begin{equation}
\tau_{\mathrm{cmd}}
=
J^\top(q)F_{\mathrm{cmd}}
+
\tau_{\mathrm{null}}
+
\tau_c(q,\dot{q}),
\label{eq:chapter4_contact_commanded_torque}
\end{equation}

where \(F_{\mathrm{cmd}}\) is the commanded Cartesian impedance
wrench and \(\tau_{\mathrm{null}}\) is the projected null-space torque. No
gravity term appears in the user callback. The Franka Control Interface already
applies robot-side gravity and motor-friction compensation to any external
torque command sent through libfranka, so adding one would double it.

Four states make up the active sequence: \texttt{approach\_orient},
\texttt{approach\_descend}, \texttt{set\_up}, and \texttt{grind}. Hold and
manual guidance are available at start-up, but neither is a contact phase.
Null-space mode is selected on its own and, once enabled, stays active for the
whole automatic sequence.

\subsection{Configured and Physical Plane}
\label{subsec:plane_calibration_protocol}

Two plane definitions were used, and they are not interchangeable. The
controller operated with the configured plane alone: the plane point
\(p_s=[\,0.55,\,-0.06,\,0.025\,]^\top\,\mathrm{m}\) and the configured normal
\(n_{\mathrm{cfg}}\equiv n_s\), obtained from \(a=0^\circ\) and \(b=5^\circ\)
in \cref{eq:implemented_surface_normal_angles}. Approach, clearance, feature
projection, surface frame and commanded tool orientation all follow from it.

The physical orientation of the surface was estimated afterwards and only for
the offline alignment metric. Four points on the surface were probed with the
robot; three of them fitted the plane and the fourth was held out of the fit,
lying \(0.82\,\mathrm{mm}\) from it. The procedure was repeated three times,
giving \(a_{\mathrm{phys}}=-0.72\pm0.31^\circ\) and
\(b_{\mathrm{phys}}=14.99\pm1.91^\circ\) in the tilt parametrisation of
\cref{eq:implemented_surface_normal_angles}, from which \(n_{\mathrm{phys}}\)
follows. The controller itself plays no part in this estimate: it is configured
with \(a\) and \(b\) directly and has no calibration routine.

Between \(n_{\mathrm{cfg}}\) and \(n_{\mathrm{phys}}\) lies \(10.01^\circ\), and
that separation is the initial geometric misalignment the alignment study works
on. The repeatability of the probing limits how far an absolute residual angle
can be trusted, the spread on \(b_{\mathrm{phys}}\) being the larger of the two.
A change measured within one run is less exposed to that offset, which is why
the reported outcome is a change rather than a final absolute angle.

\section{Contact Geometry}
\label{sec:contact_geometry}

The task geometry is defined by a surface plane, a tool edge, and a selected
local linearised centre of compliance. Let

\begin{equation}
n_s \in \mathbb{R}^3,
\qquad
\|n_s\| = 1
\label{eq:surface_normal}
\end{equation}

be the surface normal expressed in the robot base frame. In the implemented
configuration it is generated from rotations \(a\) about base \(x\) and \(b\)
about base \(y\),

\begin{equation}
n_s
=
R_y(b)R_x(a)e_z
=
\begin{bmatrix}
\sin(b)\cos(a) & -\sin(a) & \cos(b)\cos(a)
\end{bmatrix}^{\top}.
\label{eq:implemented_surface_normal_angles}
\end{equation}

Here \(a=0^\circ\) and \(b=5^\circ\), with the plane through
\(p_s=[\,0.55,\,-0.06,\,0.025\,]^\top\,\mathrm{m}\). Two orthonormal tangents
\(t_1\) and \(t_2\) span it: projecting the configured auxiliary direction into
the plane gives the first, and \(t_2=n_s\times t_1\) the second. The
implemented column order is

\begin{equation}
R_{\mathrm{surface}}
=
\begin{bmatrix}
t_1 & t_2 & n_s
\end{bmatrix},
\label{eq:surface_task_frame}
\end{equation}

matching the surface task frame of \cref{eq:surface_task_frame_matrix}. Every
surface-frame vector and every diagonal gain in this chapter follows the order
\([\,t_1,t_2,n_s\,]\) unless a table states another semantic order explicitly.
With the active auxiliary direction \(a_s=e_x\) and the angles above, the axes
come out as
\[
t_1=[\,\cos 5^\circ,\ 0,\ -\sin 5^\circ\,]^\top,
\qquad
t_2=[\,0,\ 1,\ 0\,]^\top .
\]
Rotating about \(t_2\) is therefore rotation about base \(y\), while rotating
about \(t_1\) tips the tool about the orthogonal tangent, which lies in the
tilted base \(x\)-\(z\) plane.

One fixed point would not describe the grinding face, so the face is carried
explicitly. Its centre sits \(20\,\mathrm{mm}\) along \(+Z_{\mathrm{EE}}\) from
the end-effector origin, and it measures \(40\,\mathrm{mm}\) along
\(X_{\mathrm{EE}}\) by \(120\,\mathrm{mm}\) along \(Y_{\mathrm{EE}}\). The
controller transforms all four corners into the base frame and picks whichever
corner, edge, or complete face leads along the descent direction, averaging any
features whose projected heights agree to within \(0.1\,\mathrm{mm}\). Without
that tolerance an exact single-axis tilt would pick one endpoint of a physical
edge at random.

Let \(a_{\mathrm{tool,EE}}\) denote the configured physical face-normal axis.
For the current tool \(a_{\mathrm{tool,EE}}=e_z\), and the orientation target
satisfies

\begin{equation}
R_d a_{\mathrm{tool,EE}}=-n_s .
\label{eq:implemented_tool_axis_target}
\end{equation}

The minus sign turns the \(+Z_{\mathrm{EE}}\) grinding face toward the surface.
What the controller applies is the minimum rotation carrying the starting
nominal tool axis onto \(-n_s\), which preserves as much of the remaining twist
as it can. Setting \(R_d=R_{\mathrm{surface}}\) would not: it would command the
twist as well.

Torsionally the parallel-jaw grasp is not rigid. Manual inspection put the
relative tool rotation at roughly \(\pm2^\circ\) about \(y_{\mathrm{EE}}\), a
direction that lies mostly in the surface-tangent plane for the tested mounting
pose and is informally associated with the \(t_2\) response. It is still
written as \(y_{\mathrm{EE}}\). The calibrated plane fixes \(t_2\) in the base
frame, whereas \(y_{\mathrm{EE}}=R_{\mathrm{EE}}e_{y,\mathrm{EE}}\) travels with
the EE orientation, and identifying the two without an explicit frame
transformation would blur exactly the distinction the alignment metric rests
on.

A point \(p_c\) carries the selected centre of compliance, with the lever
\(r_c=p_{\mathrm{TCP}}-p_c\) of \cref{eq:pole_to_tcp_lever}. Its
\(p_{\mathrm{TCP}}\) is the origin of the end-effector pose the robot reports,
so relating that point to the physical hand and tool means carrying any
configured flange-to-end-effector transform along with it.

Case matters in the subscript. Lower-case \(c\) belongs to the chosen virtual
centre \(p_c\); upper-case \(C\) appears later for the physical contact point
\(p_C\), whose lever \(r_C=p_C-p_{\mathrm{TCP}}\) points the other way. The two
are never interchangeable.

A force \(f\) at the contact produces a moment about the TCP through
\(r_C\), which is why the selected stiffness matrix will not read as three
independent translational springs beside three independent rotational ones.
Virtual-centre behaviour requires translation and rotation to be selected
together.

\section{Gain Selection Methodology}
\label{sec:cartesian_stiffness_selection}

Notation follows Chapter~\ref{ch:theory}. A subscript \(\mathrm{task}\) marks a
gain written in a local task frame, and a surface-frame quantity takes the axis
order \([t_1,t_2,n_s]\). No single frame serves every phase and every gain
block, however:

\begin{itemize}
    \item approach translation and rotation are diagonal in the surface frame
    and transformed to the base frame;
    \item set-up and grind translation are diagonal directly in base
    \([x,y,z]\), while their rotational gains are diagonal in
    \([t_1,t_2,n_s]\);
    \item hold gains are isotropic in the base frame; the enabled pre-set-up
    gate overrides only translation with an isotropic base-frame gain and
    retains the isotropic approach rotational gain; and
    \item during set-up only, the selected base-frame translational and
    rotational blocks can be shifted from a commanded centre of compliance to the TCP, producing
    a coupled \(6\times6\) gain.
\end{itemize}

\Cref{tab:active_stiffness_design_points} maps the stiffness across every phase
--- hold, both approach states, the pre-set-up gate, set-up, grind and manual
guidance --- and \cref{tab:active_automatic_damping_policy} gives the matching
automatic-damping policy. The coordinate frame is part of the gain definition
and must be retained whenever the gain is calculated or transformed.

Base-frame errors and base-frame gains drive the control law of
\cref{eq:cartesian_impedance_control_law}. Interpreting an experiment is easier
in the surface task frame, where the same quantities read as

\begin{equation}
e_{\mathrm{task}}
=
\begin{bmatrix}
e_{p,\mathrm{task}}\\
e_{R,\mathrm{task}}
\end{bmatrix}
=
T_R^\top
\begin{bmatrix}
e_p\\
e_R
\end{bmatrix},
\qquad
\dot{x}_{\mathrm{task}}
=
T_R^\top
\begin{bmatrix}
\dot{p}_{\mathrm{EE}}\\
\omega_{\mathrm{EE}}
\end{bmatrix},
\label{eq:experiment_task_frame_errors}
\end{equation}

where \(T_R=\operatorname{diag}(R_{\mathrm{surface}},R_{\mathrm{surface}})\).
The representation serves interpretation, where both blocks are being read in
the surface frame. It says nothing about how the set-up translation block is
stored: that one is diagonal in base XYZ and stays there.

The complete local Cartesian stiffness matrix keeps the task-frame notation of
\cref{eq:full_task_frame_gain_blocks}. In the standard spatial-stiffness block
form \citep{Fasse1997SpatialImpedance,Ott2008},

\begin{equation}
K_{\mathrm{task}}
=
\begin{bmatrix}
K_{p,\mathrm{task}} & K_{pR,\mathrm{task}}\\
K_{Rp,\mathrm{task}} & K_{R,\mathrm{task}}
\end{bmatrix}.
\label{eq:contact_stiffness_block}
\end{equation}

Translational displacement reaches force through \(K_{p,\mathrm{task}}\) and
rotational displacement reaches moment through \(K_{R,\mathrm{task}}\), while
the off-diagonal blocks \(K_{pR,\mathrm{task}}\) and \(K_{Rp,\mathrm{task}}\)
carry the translation--rotation coupling. Written this way, the coupling
between sliding and rotation during edge contact becomes visible instead of
being buried in a \(6\times6\) array. During set-up the controller builds the
full matrix analytically from a manually selected centre of compliance. Two
things are designed: the position of that centre, and the contents of the source
stiffness blocks. The resulting 36 entries are given below.

The construction rests on a symmetric positive-definite stiffness and a
symmetric positive-semidefinite damping matrix,

\begin{equation}
K_{\mathrm{task}} = K_{\mathrm{task}}^\top,
\qquad
K_{\mathrm{task}} \succ 0.
\label{eq:contact_stiffness_spd}
\end{equation}

Both properties survive the congruence, which makes them free checks on the
assembled \(6\times6\) matrices: they cost no experiment-specific tuning
variable and catch a sign or transpose error immediately.

\subsection{Quasi-Static Stiffness Sizing and Automatic Damping}
\label{subsec:initial_stiffness_damping_values}

Sizing the stiffness values was done offline, by hand, and never runs on the
robot; the calculation below estimates no contact force and appears in no
control loop. At low velocity the elastic parts of the implemented command
separate from damping and inertia,

\begin{equation}
f_K=K_pe_p,
\qquad
m_K=K_Re_R .
\label{eq:quasi_static_translational_stiffness}
\end{equation}

For a principal coordinate \(i\), a declared elastic force--error design pair
\((F_i^\star,\Delta x_i^\star)\), or moment--angle pair
\((M_i^\star,\Delta\theta_i^\star)\), gives

\begin{align}
k_{p,i}
&=
\frac{F_i^\star}{\Delta x_i^\star},
\label{eq:initial_translational_stiffness}\\
k_{R,i}
&=
\frac{M_i^\star}{\Delta\theta_i^\star}.
\label{eq:initial_rotational_stiffness}
\end{align}

The starred quantities denote design values used to calculate the initial
stiffness entries; \cref{subsec:design_value_interpretation} states how they are
to be read. Where a moment scale comes from an offset force, its component about
the selected unit axis \(a_i\) is
\[
M_i^\star
=
\left|a_i^\top(r_C\times f_C)\right|
\leq
\lVert r_C\rVert\lVert f_C\rVert ,
\]
rather than the scalar lever-arm bound being used without its axis direction
\citep{Murray1994,Siciliano2009}.

\paragraph{Frame-consistent directional stiffness.}
Outside a principal-gain frame the scalar rule of
\cref{eq:initial_translational_stiffness} no longer applies directly. For a
gain diagonal in frame \(R_F\), the base-frame matrix and the effective slope
along a base-frame unit direction \(u\) are

\begin{equation}
K_{p,0}
=
R_F\operatorname{diag}(k_{p,1},k_{p,2},k_{p,3})R_F^\top,
\qquad
k_{p,\mathrm{eff}}(u)
=
u^\top K_{p,0}u
=
\sum_{i=1}^{3}k_{p,i}(r_i^\top u)^2 .
\label{eq:directional_effective_stiffness}
\end{equation}

An error \(e_p=\delta u\) gives the elastic component
\(\delta k_{p,\mathrm{eff}}(u)\) along \(u\), and along a non-principal
direction it also raises the transverse component \(\delta(I-uu^\top)K_{p,0}u\).
The same construction covers \(K_{R,0}\) and a rotational direction. Set-up
translation needs it: that block is diagonal in base XYZ while the push follows
the tilted surface normal.

\paragraph{Set-up/grind calculation.}
Base \(y\) and the active \(t_2\) coincide, which makes the lateral entry easy
to size. Treating the completely blocked \(30\,\mathrm{mm}\) grind stroke as a
\(60\,\mathrm{N}\) elastic design scale gives

\begin{equation}
k_y=\frac{60}{0.030}=2000\,\mathrm{N/m},
\qquad
k_x=k_y .
\label{eq:setup_lateral_stiffness_design}
\end{equation}

The press direction takes a nominal \(65\,\mathrm{N}\) elastic scale at the
configured \(0.180\,\mathrm{m}\) endpoint, so
\(k_{n,\mathrm{eff}}^\star=65/0.180=361.11\,\mathrm{N/m}\). That coordinate
starts near \(-0.020\,\mathrm{m}\) at the clearance hand-off and is scheduled
to \(+0.180\,\mathrm{m}\), roughly \(0.200\,\mathrm{m}\) of reference travel,
which at \(0.050\,\mathrm{m/s}\) takes \(4\,\mathrm{s}\) --- deliberately the
same as the set-up timeout. Of that travel the first \(20\,\mathrm{mm}\) closes
the geometric clearance, and the remaining \(180\,\mathrm{mm}\) is the endpoint
against which the blocked-load design scale is defined.
With \(a=0^\circ\), \(b=5^\circ\), and
\(K_{p,0}=\operatorname{diag}(k_x,k_y,k_z)\), solving
\[
k_{n,\mathrm{eff}}^\star
=
k_x\sin^2 b+k_z\cos^2 b
\]
gives

\begin{equation}
k_z
=
\frac{k_{n,\mathrm{eff}}^\star-k_x\sin^2(5^\circ)}
     {\cos^2(5^\circ)}
=
348.6\,\mathrm{N/m}
\;\longrightarrow\;
350\,\mathrm{N/m}.
\label{eq:setup_base_z_stiffness_design}
\end{equation}

Rounded, the implemented block is
\(\operatorname{diag}(2000,2000,350)\,\mathrm{N/m}\) in base XYZ. The same
matrix in the active surface frame reads

\begin{equation}
R_{\mathrm{surface}}^\top K_{p,0}R_{\mathrm{surface}}
\approx
\begin{bmatrix}
1987.47 & 0 & 143.26\\
0 & 2000 & 0\\
143.26 & 0 & 362.53
\end{bmatrix}
\mathrm{N/m}.
\label{eq:active_setup_translation_surface_representation}
\end{equation}

A completely blocked \(0.180\,\mathrm{m}\) normal reference change then works
out at roughly \(65.3\,\mathrm{N}\) along the normal, \(25.8\,\mathrm{N}\)
along \(t_1\), and \(70.2\,\mathrm{N}\) of total elastic-force magnitude. All
three assume the tracking error never closes, whereas the robot follows part of
the reference, so they are upper bounds on the virtual spring rather than
predicted loads.

The rotational block starts from a \(15\,\mathrm{N}\) design force acting
through the \(60\,\mathrm{mm}\) tool half-length, a moment scale of about
\(0.90\,\mathrm{Nm}\). Allowing roughly \(10^\circ\) about the two surface
tangents but only \(1^\circ\) about the surface normal, and converting to
radians,

\begin{equation}
k_{R,t_1}=k_{R,t_2}
\approx\frac{0.90}{0.1745}=5.16
\;\longrightarrow\;5,
\qquad
k_{R,n}
\approx\frac{0.90}{0.01745}=51.6
\;\longrightarrow\;50
\quad\mathrm{Nm/rad}.
\label{eq:setup_rotational_stiffness_design}
\end{equation}

The selected ratio provides lower rotational stiffness about the surface
tangents than about the surface normal. During set-up these are source gains at the
selected centre of compliance. Once the congruence shift has been applied they
are, in general, no longer the diagonal rotational slopes a pure rotation at
the TCP would meet. Grind takes the same source blocks with no shift at all.

\paragraph{Automatic damping.}
Where stiffness is selected offline, the controller calculates Cartesian
damping itself, at entry to each phase group. From the joint-space mass matrix
and the Jacobian it forms

\begin{equation}
\begin{aligned}
\mathcal H_0&=JM(q)^{-1}J^\top,\\
\Lambda_0&=\operatorname{sym}\!\left[
\left(\operatorname{sym}(\mathcal H_0)+10^{-9}I_6\right)^{-1}
\right],\\
\Lambda_F&=\operatorname{sym}\!\left(T_F^\top\Lambda_0T_F\right),
\qquad
\operatorname{sym}(A)=\tfrac12(A+A^\top).
\end{aligned}
\label{eq:implemented_cartesian_inertia}
\end{equation}

Only the positive diagonal entries of \(\Lambda_F\) survive. Each principal
stiffness \(k_i\) then takes the coefficient

\begin{equation}
d_i
=
\min\!\left(
d_{\max},
\max\!\left(d_{i,\min},\,2\zeta\sqrt{\Lambda_{F,ii}k_i}\right)
\right).
\label{eq:directional_damping_heuristic}
\end{equation}

Here \(d_{\max}=8000\), and \(d_{i,\min}=0\): the active configuration falls
back on the manual coefficients when the calculation fails, but never floors
the calculated value with them. Writing \(c_i=2\sqrt{\Lambda_{F,ii}k_i}\) for a
fitted phase, the least-squares factor that approaches a target vector
\(d^\star\) is

\begin{equation}
\beta^\star
=
\frac{c^\top d^\star}{c^\top c}.
\label{eq:fitted_automatic_damping_factor}
\end{equation}

which is why the controller prints it at phase entry. It cannot be reproduced
from the stiffness table alone, and the general sequence CSV does not yet carry

Two things limit \cref{eq:directional_damping_heuristic}. It reads only the
diagonal of the TCP task-inertia estimate and drops the off-diagonal coupling,
and its \(\zeta\) is a dimensionless tuning factor rather than an identified
modal damping ratio. During set-up the source damping blocks are calculated at
the TCP and then shifted through the centre of compliance by the same
congruence as the stiffness. Symmetry and positive semidefiniteness survive
that shift. Critical damping of the compliance-centre/contact system does not
follow from \(\zeta=1\).

\subsection{Compliance-Centre Gain Design}
\label{subsec:impedance_around_pole}

Only \texttt{set\_up} uses the compliance-centre construction. Rotating a gain
into the surface frame will not do the job on its own: it turns the principal
directions of the translational and rotational blocks and leaves the
translation--rotation blocks at zero, which keeps the centre of compliance
sitting on the TCP. The coupling has to be built, and the point shift of
\cref{eq:offset_adjoint,eq:coupled_tcp_stiffness,eq:coupled_tcp_damping} builds
it. \Cref{sec:coupled_pole_stiffness} carries the signs and the block-matrix
derivation in full.

The active source stiffness, written wholly in base-frame coordinates, is

\begin{equation}
K_{c,0}
=
\operatorname{blkdiag}\!\left(
\operatorname{diag}(2000,2000,350),
R_{\mathrm{surface}}\operatorname{diag}(5,5,50)
R_{\mathrm{surface}}^\top
\right).
\label{eq:active_mixed_frame_pole_stiffness}
\end{equation}

Its translational entries carry \(\mathrm{N/m}\) and its rotational entries
\(\mathrm{Nm/rad}\). Written out this way the mixed-frame construction shows
plainly: translation diagonal in base XYZ, rotation alone diagonal in surface
coordinates \([t_1,t_2,n_s]\).

Nothing is added to the command by this. No extra force, no extra moment, no
time-varying task-space rotation --- only the gain matrices change. Its
experimental effect is judged from the EE-inferred tool-axis alignment, the
selected-feature and TCP displacements, and the commanded joint torques.

\paragraph{Choosing the centre of compliance.}
The implementation accepts an arbitrary base-frame offset from the projected
leading-feature reference captured at clearance:

\begin{equation}
p_c=p_{\Pi,\mathrm{clr}}+\Delta p_c^0,
\qquad
\Delta p_c^0=
\begin{bmatrix}-0.04&0.08&0\end{bmatrix}^{\top}\mathrm{m}.
\label{eq:active_pole_choice}
\end{equation}

Its superscript \(0\) is a reminder that the three components are base XYZ and
not \([t_1,t_2,n_s]\). The reference is held constant for the run. A scalar
choice \(p_c=p_s+h n_s\) is a theoretical special case that the active
parameterisation does not use. Holding the centre of compliance constant from
first contact holds the lever constant too,
\[
r_c=p_{\mathrm{TCP,clr}}-p_c,
\]
where \(p_{\mathrm{TCP,clr}}\) is the TCP position captured at the same
clearance hand-off. A different \(\Delta p_c^0\) means a different coupled pair
\(K_{\mathrm{TCP}},D_{\mathrm{TCP}}\) out of
\cref{eq:coupled_tcp_stiffness,eq:coupled_tcp_damping}, so the sign and the
frame of the vector are stored with every trial. Neither can be recovered from
the alignment record afterwards.

Resolved in surface coordinates on the configured \(a=0^\circ\),
\(b=5^\circ\) surface, the base-XYZ offset becomes

\begin{equation}
R_{\mathrm{surface}}^\top\Delta p_c^0
\simeq
\begin{bmatrix}-39.85&80.00&-3.49\end{bmatrix}^{\top}\mathrm{mm}.
\label{eq:active_pole_offset_surface_components}
\end{equation}

The centre of compliance therefore lies mainly in the \(-t_1,+t_2\) quadrant
relative to the projected selected feature. The offset is not itself the lever
the adjoint uses; the exact lever picks up the captured TCP-to-feature geometry
as well. Writing \(f=K_{p,0}e_p\) for a purely translational error, the moment
the coupling block produces gives a direct sign check,

\begin{equation}
m_c=[r_c]_\times^\top f=-r_c\times f.
\label{eq:active_pole_moment_sign_check}
\end{equation}

At the nominal aligned geometry the transverse lever comes to approximately
\(0.03985t_1-0.08t_2\), and a blocked press with \(f\approx-Fn_s\), \(F>0\),
gives \(m_c\approx-F(0.08t_1+0.03985t_2)\). The selected quadrant thus biases
negative rotation about both surface tangents, and reversing either transverse
offset reverses the corresponding bias. That is the geometric reason for
choosing this direction, and it is as far as the reasoning goes. The
base-diagonal force need not come out perfectly normal, and the bias helps only
where its signed components oppose the signed residual orientation error ---
a comparison that waits on the physical alignment signal reaching the run
record.

What \(p_c\) names is a local linearised centre of compliance, not a hinge. The
tool need not pivot about it during contact, and the relation between the two is
affected by four factors: the finite rotational stiffness, the lever itself,
friction and constraints at the surface, and the active null-space sigma torque.

\subsection{Gain Construction and Implementation Sequence}
\label{sec:role_of_gain_methods}

Calculation and implementation follow the controller's own order, so the five
steps below are applied in the sequence given:

\begin{enumerate}
    \item State a phase-specific force--error or moment--angle design pair and
    calculate the principal source stiffness using
    \cref{eq:initial_translational_stiffness}.
    \item Attach each diagonal to its implemented frame, form the base-frame
    block, and check the commanded path with
    \cref{eq:directional_effective_stiffness}. For set-up, the base-XYZ
    translation and surface-frame rotation blocks remain distinct.
    \item At phase-group entry, calculate and cache the damping from the
    current TCP task inertia using
    \cref{eq:implemented_cartesian_inertia,eq:directional_damping_heuristic}.
    \item During set-up only, shift the selected source stiffness and damping
    through the centre of compliance using
    \cref{eq:coupled_tcp_stiffness,eq:coupled_tcp_damping}. Grind immediately
    returns to the decoupled source blocks.
    \item Check the resulting elastic load and joint-torque scales, then change
    one declared gain family at a time while retaining the frame labels,
    computed damping, compliance-centre offset, and remaining parameters with the run
    record.
\end{enumerate}

The resulting workflow is

\begin{equation}
\begin{aligned}
\text{phase design envelope}
&\rightarrow \text{frame-labelled source }K
\rightarrow \text{task-inertia }D\\
&\rightarrow \text{set-up-only compliance-centre shift}\\
&\rightarrow \text{load/torque check}
\rightarrow \text{controlled trial}.
\end{aligned}
\label{eq:gain_selection_workflow}
\end{equation}

\subsection{Experimental Gain Variation Strategy}
\label{sec:direct_stiffness_comparison}

The effect of each stiffness entry was investigated by varying one parameter
while the remaining controller settings were held fixed. This
one-factor-at-a-time procedure separates the response associated with the
selected gain from changes caused by other controller parameters, which the
paired sweep of the earlier campaign could not do.

In Case B the rotational stiffness of the tilted surface tangent was tested at
\(5\), \(15\) and \(50\,\mathrm{Nm/rad}\). When \(K_{R,t_1}\) was varied,
\(K_{R,t_2}\) was held at its baseline value, and the roles were reversed for
the \(t_2\) study; moving both at once would leave their individual effects
unidentifiable. The normal rotational stiffness \(K_{R,n}\) was excluded from
the comparison, because it resists twist about the surface normal rather than
tipping about a tangent.

In Case C the translational stiffness perpendicular to the tilted tangent was
tested at \(300\), \(800\) and \(2000\,\mathrm{N/m}\), and one further condition
combined the lowest translational stiffness with the highest tested rotational
stiffness to examine their interaction. Everything else stayed as it was: the
other source gains, the compliance-centre offset, the phase timing and the
null-space parameters. A systematic change in the measured response can then be
associated with the one stiffness coordinate that was varied.

Damping needed no separate tuning. At the next entry to the relevant phase group
the controller recalculated it from the changed stiffness and the task inertia.
Because \cref{eq:directional_damping_heuristic} takes the square root of the
stiffness, scaling an entry by \(\alpha\) scales its damping coefficient by
\(\sqrt{\alpha}\),

\begin{equation}
d_{i,\mathrm{test}}
=
\sqrt{\alpha}\,d_{i,\mathrm{base}} ,
\label{eq:damping_change_with_stiffness}
\end{equation}

so doubling a stiffness raises the damping by about \(41\,\%\) rather than by
\(100\,\%\). This is an algebraic consequence of the damping law and not a
measured result. It holds while the Cartesian inertia \(\Lambda_{F,ii}\) and the
factor \(\zeta\) are unchanged and the coefficient stays between the configured
floor and ceiling; since the inertia depends on the pose at phase-group entry,
the realised coefficients were recorded for every run. The value is cached at
phase-group entry and held for the rest of that phase, and the stiffness alone
does not reconstruct it without the phase-entry pose, the Jacobian \(J\) and the
joint-space mass matrix \(M(q)\).

Approach, gate and hold gains follow the same one-at-a-time rule against the
design pairs of \cref{tab:active_stiffness_design_points}. Where a translational
entry is assigned from a desired effective normal stiffness, the base-\(z\)
value follows \cref{eq:setup_base_z_stiffness_design}, which carries the surface
orientation into the assignment.

\paragraph{Evaluation of candidate gains.}
Candidate stiffnesses were compared through the quantities of
\cref{sec:alignment_evaluation}, with the physical-plane alignment improvement
\(\Delta\theta_{\mathrm{align,phys}}\) as the primary outcome. The
rotational-stiffness study of \cref{sec:results_case_b} was evaluated using the
alignment improvement, the physical-plane residual angle and the
model-estimated normal load; the contact-induced rotation and the
selected-feature and TCP displacements remained available as run diagnostics.
Each setting was repeated three times and summarised by its mean and standard
deviation. A change was interpreted as a parameter effect only when it exceeded
the interpretation threshold of \cref{sec:results_provenance} and was not
accompanied by an incompatible change in contact load or displacement.

\subsection{Interpretation of Design Values}
\label{subsec:design_value_interpretation}

The quantities used above to size the gains are design values, and several of
them invite a reading they will not support. They are collected here rather than
qualified individually at each equation.

Starred quantities such as \(F_i^\star\), \(M_i^\star\), \(\Delta x_i^\star\)
and \(\Delta\theta_i^\star\) denote design values used to calculate the initial
stiffness entries. They were not obtained from the measured contact response and
were not imposed as force or moment limits during the experiments.

The virtual displacements that appear in those pairs are reference offsets, not
distances the tool travels. The \(180\,\mathrm{mm}\) endpoint of the set-up ramp
fixes the blocked-load scale of the virtual spring; the tool remains in contact
with the surface throughout, and the reference continues past it while the
actual motion does not.

Blocked-load figures calculated from these scales assume a tracking error that
never closes. They therefore bound the elastic force the virtual spring can
command, and are not predictions of the load that was measured.

The coordinate frame is part of each gain definition. A translational block
diagonal in base coordinates is not diagonal in the surface frame, and the
transformed matrix in
\cref{eq:active_setup_translation_surface_representation} shows the off-diagonal
entries this produces.

The null-space parameters of
\cref{tab:nullspace_parameter_rationale} were held fixed for every reported run.
They are configuration values rather than an experimental case, and no result in
\cref{ch:results_discussion} is attributed to them.

\section{Experimental Procedure}
\label{sec:experimental_procedure_contact}

Every run followed the sequence below, in this order:

\begin{enumerate}
    \item The active parameter set is recorded, and the Panda moves to the
    configured initial joint pose. Optionally it is repositioned under damped
    manual guidance, and the selected start pose is recaptured.
    \item The configured gripper action is executed: in the current
    configuration a grasp at \(70\,\mathrm{mm}\) width and \(60\,\mathrm{N}\)
    rather than an open command. The tool is seated as consistently as its
    approximately \(\pm2^\circ\) rotational play about \(y_{\mathrm{EE}}\)
    allows, and that non-rigid mounting condition is recorded with the run
    metadata.
    \item The known plane and \(R_{\mathrm{surface}}=[t_1,t_2,n_s]\) are
    constructed, together with the \(40\times120\) mm tool face and the active
    base-XYZ compliance-centre offset. The Franka collision behaviour is
    configured before torque control starts.
    \item In \texttt{approach\_orient} the TCP position is held while the
    physical \(+Z_{\mathrm{EE}}\) axis rotates toward \(-n_s\). The phase
    continues only after at least \(0.5\,\mathrm{s}\) and a tool-axis error no
    greater than \(0.035\,\mathrm{rad}\).
    \item In \texttt{approach\_descend} the reference moves along \(-n_s\) at
    \(0.1\,\mathrm{m/s}\), with a maximum commanded travel of
    \(0.640\,\mathrm{m}\), and the leading face, edge or corner is selected
    continuously. The transition occurs when the exact most-leading corner
    height is at most \(20\,\mathrm{mm}\) above the plane; the averaged tied
    feature supplies the projected reference and can differ by at most the
    \(0.1\,\mathrm{mm}\) tie tolerance. Estimated force is logged and printed
    but does not trigger this transition. The enabled pre-set-up gate holds the
    reached pose until the operator presses Enter.
    \item At this clearance hand-off the selected tool feature, TCP pose,
    orientation reference, external-wrench baseline and projected
    leading-feature surface reference are frozen. The source variable names
    contain \texttt{first\_contact}, but no force contact has yet been declared.
    \item In \texttt{set\_up} the signed push coordinate ramps continuously from
    its captured value, approximately \(-0.020\,\mathrm{m}\), toward
    \(+0.180\,\mathrm{m}\) at \(0.050\,\mathrm{m/s}\) along \(-n_s\), under the
    coupled compliance-centre \(6\times6\) stiffness and damping. The
    approximately \(0.200\,\mathrm{m}\) coordinate span takes \(4\,\mathrm{s}\),
    matching the timeout; the final \(+0.180\,\mathrm{m}\) coordinate is a
    virtual post-plane spring reference and not a claim of
    \(180\,\mathrm{mm}\) physical penetration. After \(2\,\mathrm{s}\), a
    \(150\,\mathrm{Nm}\) change in the model-estimated external-moment norm may
    end the phase. This is a permissive placeholder rather than a calibrated
    contact criterion, so unless such a large change occurs the independent
    \(4\,\mathrm{s}\) timeout governs the transition.
    \item In \texttt{grind} the final set-up preload is retained and a quintic
    smooth ping-pong sweep of \(0.03\,\mathrm{m}\) amplitude at
    \(0.2\,\mathrm{Hz}\) is commanded along \(t_2\). In the callback that
    satisfies the set-up exit condition, the controller switches immediately,
    without gain interpolation, to the decoupled set-up gain blocks; the
    point-shifted branch is no longer active. A wrench and
    \(J^\top w_{\mathrm{cmd}}\) step is therefore possible at this hand-off.
    \item The run stops with \texttt{e+Enter}, the buffered log is persisted,
    and the EE-inferred tool-axis error, edge and TCP displacement, commanded
    joint-torque trace and phase timing are evaluated.
\end{enumerate}

Between repetitions of a controlled comparison, only the declared independent
variable may move --- block-diagonal against point-shifted impedance, say, or
the base-XYZ compliance-centre offset. Runs pool only when the controller
configuration, tool geometry, phase duration and fixed gains all match;
anything else is reported as a separate configuration. What such a comparison
delivers is narrow. It says which of the tested centre-of-compliance settings
gave the largest alignment for this tool, this plane and this contact sequence,
and it will not yield a universally optimal stiffness matrix.

\section{Evaluation Metrics}
\label{sec:alignment_evaluation}

Two angles are easy to confuse here: the tool-axis alignment metric and the
controller's own rotation error. They are different quantities and the section
keeps them apart throughout. Write
\(\widehat a_T=R_{\mathrm{EE}}a_{\mathrm{tool,EE}}^0\) for the grinding-face
axis inferred from the nominal calibrated tool-to-EE transform. With
\(Q_g(t)\) standing for the unmeasured relative rotation of the tool in the
gripper, the actual physical axis is

\begin{equation}
a_T
=
R_{\mathrm{EE}}Q_g(t)a_{\mathrm{tool,EE}}^0,
\qquad
\lvert\theta_g(t)\rvert\lesssim2^\circ
\ \text{about }y_{\mathrm{EE}} .
\label{eq:tool_mount_play}
\end{equation}

What reaches the log is \(\widehat a_T\). Every alignment angle below is built
from it and is an EE-inferred quantity, coinciding with a physical tool-face
angle only where \(Q_g(t)=I\). Two signed target axes are required:

\begin{equation}
\begin{aligned}
a_{\mathrm{cfg}}&=-n_s, &
a_{\mathrm{phys}}&=-n_{\mathrm{phys}},\\
\theta_{\mathrm{align},j}
&=
\cos^{-1}\!\left(
\operatorname{clip}(\widehat a_T^\top a_j,-1,1)
\right),&
e_{\mathrm{align},j}&=\widehat a_T\times a_j,
\quad j\in\{\mathrm{cfg},\mathrm{phys}\}.
\end{aligned}
\label{eq:contact_alignment_error_cross}
\end{equation}

The minus signs come from the current tool-axis convention. Online, the
controller logs \(\theta_{\mathrm{align,cfg}}\), the angle to the configured
normal, which verifies the implemented command geometry and does no more than
that. Its relation to a physical-tool alignment is affected by two sources of
uncertainty: \(n_s\) and \(n_{\mathrm{phys}}\) differ across the recorded
campaign, and \(Q_g(t)\) was never measured.

The primary metric is instead computed offline as the EE-inferred angle
\(\theta_{\mathrm{align,phys}}\). Set-up improves alignment when

\begin{equation}
\theta_{\mathrm{align,phys,after}}
<
\theta_{\mathrm{align,phys,before}}.
\label{eq:alignment_error_reduction}
\end{equation}

The corresponding physical-plane improvement is

\begin{equation}
\Delta\theta_{\mathrm{align,phys}}
=
\theta_{\mathrm{align,phys,before}}
-
\theta_{\mathrm{align,phys,after}}.
\label{eq:alignment_error_improvement}
\end{equation}

A positive \(\Delta\theta_{\mathrm{align,phys}}\) means alignment with the
measured plane improved, on the assumption that the mount is rigid. The mount
carries roughly \(2^\circ\) of play, so this metric describes the combined
robot--gripper--tool response and does not isolate rotation of the grinding face
within the gripper. The rotation during contact itself is the base-frame rotation
vector of the finite before--after relative rotation,

\begin{equation}
\Delta\boldsymbol{\theta}_C
=
\operatorname{Log}\!\left(
R_{\mathrm{after}}R_{\mathrm{before}}^\top
\right)^\vee .
\label{eq:contact_induced_rotation}
\end{equation}

The physical-plane corrective vector
\(e_{\mathrm{align,phys}}=\widehat a_T\times(-n_{\mathrm{phys}})\) points
locally along the rotation that carries the EE-inferred tool axis toward the
measured plane normal. The rotation during contact is favorable when it has a
positive component along this corrective axis,

\begin{equation}
e_{\mathrm{align,phys,before}}^\top
\Delta\boldsymbol{\theta}_C
>
0,
\label{eq:contact_rotation_condition}
\end{equation}

which checks the sign locally and is not evaluated for large rotations.

Displacement of the selected physical feature is

\begin{equation}
\Delta p_C
=
p_{C,\mathrm{after}}-p_{C,\mathrm{before}},
\qquad
s_C=\lVert\Delta p_C\rVert .
\label{eq:contact_feature_displacement}
\end{equation}

TCP displacement is reported the same way. Both catch the translation and
sliding that an orientation-only metric would let through.

During set-up the controller logs something else again:
\(e_R
=R_{\mathrm{EE}}\operatorname{Log}
(R_{\mathrm{EE}}^\top R_{\mathrm{clr}})^\vee\), expressed in the base frame
after masking, with \(R_{\mathrm{clr}}\) the orientation held from the
clearance hand-off. This control error starts near zero and can
\emph{increase} as the tool tips into contact, which is the opposite of the
trend expected from an alignment trace. It is therefore not plotted as
\(\theta_{\mathrm{align},j}\) unless the nominal tool axis is first
reconstructed. The general log also records the angle of the EE-inferred tool
axis to the configured normal, which serves as the online geometry check.
Because the physical-plane normal was estimated separately, the corresponding
alignment value was calculated offline, as \cref{sec:results_alignment}
describes; the unmeasured \(Q_g(t)\) remains outside that calculation.

\subsection{Metric Use by Experimental Case}
\label{sec:evaluation_criteria_contact}

Physical-plane alignment improvement is the primary response in all five cases.
What separates them is the independent variable stored with each run, and the
supporting quantities carried alongside that primary outcome.

\noindent\textbf{Primary---physical-plane alignment improvement.}
\(\Delta\theta_{\mathrm{align,phys}}\), with a positive value denoting
improvement.

\noindent\textbf{Physical-plane residual.}
\(\theta_{\mathrm{align,phys,after}}\), reported as a range when the
physical-plane calibration uncertainty affects its absolute value.

\noindent\textbf{Contact-induced rotation.}
The magnitude and direction of the finite before--after rotation
\(\Delta\boldsymbol{\theta}_C\).

\noindent\textbf{Contact displacement.}
Selected-feature and TCP displacement during set-up.

\noindent\textbf{Load diagnostic.}
The steady model-estimated normal load, explicitly distinguished from a direct
force measurement.

\noindent\textbf{Settling check.}
The residual changes in tip angle, configured-normal angle, and estimated load
after the selected evaluation time.

Commanded tilt, stiffness entry and compliance-centre lever are stored with

\section{Safety Limits}
\label{sec:damping_safety_limits_contact}

Phase-specific damping comes from the task inertia through
\cref{eq:implemented_cartesian_inertia,eq:directional_damping_heuristic}.
Nothing about that directional calculation establishes safe contact. The real
controller stays discrete, model dependent, and coupled to a stiff environment
\citep{Pollayil2023ImpedancePlanning}.

Ahead of the torque callback the reported configuration calls
\texttt{setCollisionBehavior} with equal joint-side acceleration and nominal
thresholds of \(140\,\mathrm{Nm}\), and equal Cartesian force and moment
thresholds of \(240\,\mathrm{N}\) or \(\mathrm{Nm}\) across all six components.
Those are the thresholds at which Franka's own reflex fires; they saturate
neither the commanded torque nor the commanded wrench. Nor does the user
callback add a limiter of its own --- no Cartesian-force clipping, no moment
clipping, no joint-torque clipping, no torque-rate limit. Read the values as a
record of how the robot was configured for these runs and not as a claim about
human safety.

Procedure and geometry supply the remaining guards:

\begin{itemize}
    \item a \(0.640\,\mathrm{m}\) maximum approach travel and a geometric
    \(20\,\mathrm{mm}\) clearance hand-off;
    \item an operator gate before set-up, using a
    \(5000\,\mathrm{N/m}\) isotropic position hold with automatically fitted
    damping;
    \item a \(4\,\mathrm{s}\) set-up timeout independent of the permissive
    \(150\,\mathrm{Nm}\) external-moment-change stopping threshold; the timeout
    is expected to govern unless the estimated moment changes exceptionally;
    \item the configured set-up stiffness and push must be checked against the
    collision settings before a run; the scalar product
    \(350\,\mathrm{N/m}\times0.180\,\mathrm{m}\approx63\,\mathrm{N}\) is only a
    base-\(z\) approximation, while the frame-consistent blocked-reference
    scales are \(65.3\,\mathrm{N}\) normal and \(70.2\,\mathrm{N}\) in total
    from \cref{eq:active_setup_translation_surface_representation}; none is a
    contact-force measurement; and
    \item after a normally completed sequence run, the general CSV is inspected
    for phase transitions, commanded torque, displacement, and oscillation.
\end{itemize}

None of the 112 columns of the sequence log records a Franka reflex or contact
indication, so a run terminated by a robot-side reflex is identified from the
hardware and controller event records rather than from the logged data.

A geometrically valid stiffness matrix does not by itself ensure acceptable
hardware behaviour, which is what these checks are for: the same matrix can
still command aggressive torques or leave contact poorly damped. The
\(150\,\mathrm{Nm}\) set-up threshold requires particular care. It watches the moment change in the
model-estimated external wrench --- libfranka's model-based estimate at
stiffness frame \(K\), never a separately measured TCP contact moment --- and
it sits high enough that ordinary model fluctuation cannot pass for a contact
event, which leaves the timeout as the working transition. The number is an
engineering placeholder. It marks no measured safety boundary and no validated
contact threshold. Across the reported runs it was never reached, so set-up
always ended at the configured timeout.
