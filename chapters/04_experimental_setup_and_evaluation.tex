\chapter{Experimental Setup and Evaluation}
\label{ch:experiments}

This chapter describes the experimental setup and the evaluation method for the implemented Cartesian impedance controller.
The controller implementation was introduced in Chapter~3.
The focus here is on the definition of the experiments, the logged quantities, and the evaluation of the recorded data.

The evaluation is based on the CSV files generated during the experiments.
These files contain the measured end-effector position, desired position, positional error, rotational error, Cartesian velocity, impedance forces and moments, and commanded joint torques.
The logged data are used to analyze convergence, settling time, steady-state error, overshoot, phase delay, oscillation behavior, torque limits, and compliance behavior.

\section{Experimental Setup}

The experiments are performed on the Franka Emika Panda using the torque-control interface provided by libfranka.
The controller runs inside the Franka Control Interface with a control period of

\begin{equation}
T_s = 1\,\mathrm{ms}.
\label{eq:sampling_time}
\end{equation}

At each control cycle, the current robot state is read, the Cartesian impedance control law is evaluated, and the resulting joint torque command is sent back to the robot.
The controller uses the end-effector transformation \(T_{\mathrm{EE}}\), the joint velocity vector \(\dot{q}\), the geometric Jacobian \(J(q)\), and the model-based compensation terms provided by the libfranka model interface.
The measured Cartesian position \(\mathbf{p}_{\mathrm{EE}}\) used in the plots and error calculations is the position of the libfranka end-effector/tool-frame origin extracted from this transformation.

The same controller structure is used for all experiments.
Only the desired end-effector motion and the controller parameters are changed.
Depending on the experiment, the desired motion is either a fixed set-point,

\begin{equation}
p_d, \qquad R_d,
\label{eq:fixed_setpoint}
\end{equation}

or a time-dependent trajectory,

\begin{equation}
p_d(t), \qquad R_d(t).
\label{eq:time_dependent_trajectory}
\end{equation}

The stiffness and damping matrices are selected according to the experiment.
The positional stiffness matrix \(K_{p,0}\), positional damping matrix \(D_{p,0}\), rotational stiffness matrix \(K_{R,0}\), and rotational damping matrix \(D_{R,0}\) are expressed in the base frame \(\{0\}\).
The commanded torque is computed as

\begin{equation}
\tau_{\mathrm{cmd}}
=
J^\top(q)\mathbf{F}
+
\tau_{\mathrm{null}}
+
\tau_c(q,\dot{q}),
\label{eq:chapter4_commanded_torque}
\end{equation}

where \(\mathbf{F}\) is the Cartesian force/moment vector generated by the impedance controller and \(\tau_{\mathrm{null}}\) is the projected null-space torque.
The gravity compensation is not added as a separate term in the user callback,
because the external torque command sent through libfranka is already handled
with robot-side gravity and motor-friction compensation by the Franka Control
Interface.

\section{Experiment Definition}

Each experiment is defined by a desired end-effector position, a desired orientation, and a set of stiffness and damping values.
The desired position is given by

\begin{equation}
p_d
=
\begin{bmatrix}
p_{d,x}\\
p_{d,y}\\
p_{d,z}
\end{bmatrix},
\label{eq:desired_position_chapter4}
\end{equation}

and the desired orientation is given by the rotation matrix

\begin{equation}
R_d.
\label{eq:desired_orientation_chapter4}
\end{equation}

The desired orientation is constructed from roll, pitch, and yaw angles using the ZYX convention,

\begin{equation}
R_d
=
R_z(\gamma_d)R_y(\beta_d)R_x(\alpha_d),
\label{eq:desired_orientation_rpy}
\end{equation}

where \(\alpha_d\), \(\beta_d\), and \(\gamma_d\) are the desired roll, pitch, and yaw angles, respectively.

The positional stiffness values are defined along the desired end-effector axes as

\begin{equation}
K_{1,p}, \qquad K_{2,p}, \qquad K_{3,p}.
\label{eq:positional_stiffness_values_chapter4}
\end{equation}

The rotational stiffness values are defined as

\begin{equation}
K_{1,R}, \qquad K_{2,R}, \qquad K_{3,R}.
\label{eq:rotational_stiffness_values_chapter4}
\end{equation}

The corresponding critical damping reference depends on the directional entry
\(M_i=(M_x)_{ii}\) of the virtual Cartesian mass matrix computed from the
libfranka model quantities~\cite{Ogata2010}

\begin{equation}
D_i
=
2\sqrt{M_iK_i}
=
2\sqrt{(M_x)_{ii}K_i}.
\label{eq:critical_damping_chapter4}
\end{equation}

In the implemented experiments, the damping values are selected explicitly rather than computed from this expression. Thus, for each experiment, the stiffness values determine how strongly the end-effector reacts to position and orientation errors, while the damping values influence how quickly oscillations are reduced.

The experiments are divided into three main types:

\begin{itemize}
    \item Set-point experiments, where the desired end-effector pose remains constant.
    \item Trajectory experiments, where the desired end-effector pose changes over time.
    \item Compliance experiments, where an external force is applied to the end-effector while the desired pose remains fixed.
\end{itemize}

In the set-point experiments, the controller response is evaluated by observing how the end-effector converges from its initial pose to the desired pose.
In the trajectory experiments, the controller response is evaluated by comparing the measured end-effector motion with the desired time-dependent trajectory.
In the compliance experiment, the robot is first allowed to reach the desired set-point.
After the positional error has decreased to a small steady-state value, an external force is manually applied to the end-effector.
The resulting displacement from the desired pose is recorded.
After the external force is removed, the return motion toward the desired pose is evaluated.

A lower stiffness is expected to produce a larger displacement under the same external interaction, while a higher stiffness should result in a smaller displacement.
After the external force is removed, the end-effector should return smoothly to the desired pose.

\section{Logged Quantities}

During each experiment, selected measured and computed quantities are written to a CSV file.
The logged values are used for offline evaluation.
The main logged quantities are

\begin{equation}
\mathbf{p}_{\mathrm{EE}},
\qquad
\mathbf{p}_d,
\qquad
\mathbf{e}_p,
\qquad
\mathbf{e}_R,
\qquad
\dot{\mathbf{p}}_{\mathrm{EE}},
\qquad
\omega_{\mathrm{EE}},
\qquad
\mathbf{f},
\qquad
\mathbf{m},
\qquad
\boldsymbol{\tau}.
\label{eq:logged_quantities}
\end{equation}

Here, \(\mathbf{p}_{\mathrm{EE}}\) is the measured end-effector position, \(\mathbf{p}_d\) is the desired position, \(\mathbf{e}_p\) is the positional error, and \(\mathbf{e}_R\) is the rotational error.
The vectors \(\dot{\mathbf{p}}_{\mathrm{EE}}\) and \(\omega_{\mathrm{EE}}\) represent the linear and angular velocity of the end-effector.
The vectors \(\mathbf{f}\) and \(\mathbf{m}\) are the Cartesian impedance force and moment, and \(\boldsymbol{\tau}\) is the commanded joint torque vector after limiting.

The positional error is evaluated as

\begin{equation}
\mathbf{e}_p
=
\mathbf{p}_d
-
\mathbf{p}_{\mathrm{EE}}.
\label{eq:logged_position_error}
\end{equation}

The rotational error \(\mathbf{e}_R\) is obtained from the relative rotation matrix between the desired and current end-effector orientations, as described in Chapter~2.

The logged CSV data allow the controller behavior to be analyzed after the experiment without affecting the control computation itself.
The plots and numerical values presented in this chapter are obtained from these logged data.

\section{Evaluation Criteria}

The controller performance is evaluated using several criteria.
These criteria are chosen to describe both the transient behavior and the final accuracy of the controller.

\subsection{Maximum Error}

The maximum positional error describes the largest deviation between the measured and desired end-effector position during the experiment.
It is computed from the norm of the positional error,

\begin{equation}
\|\mathbf{e}_p(t)\|
=
\sqrt{
e_{p,x}^2(t)
+
e_{p,y}^2(t)
+
e_{p,z}^2(t)
}.
\label{eq:position_error_norm}
\end{equation}

The maximum positional error is then

\begin{equation}
e_{p,\max}
=
\max_t \|\mathbf{e}_p(t)\|.
\label{eq:maximum_position_error}
\end{equation}

Similarly, the rotational error norm is computed as

\begin{equation}
\|\mathbf{e}_R(t)\|
=
\sqrt{
e_{R,x}^2(t)
+
e_{R,y}^2(t)
+
e_{R,z}^2(t)
},
\label{eq:rotation_error_norm}
\end{equation}

and the maximum rotational error is

\begin{equation}
e_{R,\max}
=
\max_t \|\mathbf{e}_R(t)\|.
\label{eq:maximum_rotation_error}
\end{equation}

These values indicate the largest deviation from the desired pose during the experiment.

\subsection{Settling Time and Steady-State Error}

In addition to the maximum error, the transient response is evaluated using the settling time.
The settling time describes how long the controller needs until the error remains inside a predefined tolerance band around the desired pose.
For the positional error, the settling time \(t_{s,p}\) is defined as the first time at which the position error norm remains below the tolerance \(\varepsilon_p\) for the rest of the experiment,

\begin{equation}
t_{s,p}
=
\min t
\quad
\text{such that}
\quad
\|\mathbf{e}_p(\tau)\|
\leq
\varepsilon_p
\quad
\forall \tau \geq t .
\label{eq:settling_time_position}
\end{equation}

Similarly, the rotational settling time \(t_{s,R}\) is defined as

\begin{equation}
t_{s,R}
=
\min t
\quad
\text{such that}
\quad
\|\mathbf{e}_R(\tau)\|
\leq
\varepsilon_R
\quad
\forall \tau \geq t .
\label{eq:settling_time_rotation}
\end{equation}

The tolerances \(\varepsilon_p\) and \(\varepsilon_R\) are selected according to the accuracy required for the experiment.
In this work, the positional tolerance is chosen as

\begin{equation}
\varepsilon_p = 1\,\mathrm{mm}
=
0.001\,\mathrm{m},
\label{eq:position_tolerance}
\end{equation}

and the rotational tolerance is chosen as

\begin{equation}
\varepsilon_R = 0.01\,\mathrm{rad}.
\label{eq:rotation_tolerance}
\end{equation}

The steady-state error describes the remaining error after the transient response has decayed.
In contrast to the settling time, which describes how fast the controller reaches the tolerance band, the steady-state error describes how accurately the end-effector remains near the desired pose.

After the settling time has been determined, the steady-state positional error is computed as the mean position error norm over the samples after \(t_{s,p}\),

\begin{equation}
e_{p,\mathrm{ss}}
=
\frac{1}{N_{s,p}}
\sum_{k \in \mathcal{S}_p}
\|\mathbf{e}_p(k)\|,
\label{eq:steady_state_position_error_after_settling}
\end{equation}

where \(\mathcal{S}_p\) is the set of samples satisfying \(t_k \geq t_{s,p}\), and \(N_{s,p}\) is the number of samples in this set.

The steady-state rotational error is computed analogously as

\begin{equation}
e_{R,\mathrm{ss}}
=
\frac{1}{N_{s,R}}
\sum_{k \in \mathcal{S}_R}
\|\mathbf{e}_R(k)\|.
\label{eq:steady_state_rotation_error_after_settling}
\end{equation}

where \(\mathcal{S}_R\) is the set of samples satisfying \(t_k \geq t_{s,R}\), and \(N_{s,R}\) is the number of samples in this set.

If the error does not enter and remain inside the tolerance band during the experiment, no settling time is assigned.
In that case, the steady-state error is instead computed over the final part of the experiment, typically over the final \(20\%\) of the recorded samples,

\begin{equation}
e_{p,\mathrm{ss}}
=
\frac{1}{N_{\mathrm{ss}}}
\sum_{k \in \mathcal{S}_{\mathrm{ss}}}
\|\mathbf{e}_p(k)\|.
\label{eq:steady_state_position_error_final_interval}
\end{equation}

The same fallback definition is used for the rotational steady-state error.
Thus, the settling time evaluates how quickly the controller reaches the desired pose, while the steady-state error evaluates the remaining accuracy after the transient response.

\subsection{Phase Delay in Trajectory Experiments}

For trajectory experiments, the desired end-effector position changes with time.
Therefore, the tracking behavior is not only evaluated by the magnitude of the tracking error, but also by the delay between the desired and measured motion.
If the measured end-effector position follows the desired trajectory with a time shift, this indicates a phase delay.

This effect can be observed by comparing the desired trajectory \(\mathbf{p}_d(t)\) with the measured end-effector position \(\mathbf{p}_{\mathrm{EE}}(t)\).
For one Cartesian direction, a visible shift between \(p_{d,x}(t)\) and \(p_{\mathrm{EE},x}(t)\) means that the robot follows the desired motion with a delay.

The delay can be described as a time delay \(\Delta t\).
For a sinusoidal reference trajectory, the desired and measured positions can be approximated as

\begin{equation}
\mathbf{p}_d(t)
=
A\sin(\omega t),
\label{eq:desired_sinusoidal_trajectory}
\end{equation}

and

\begin{equation}
p_{\mathrm{EE}}(t)
=
A_{\mathrm{EE}}
\sin(\omega t - \varphi),
\label{eq:measured_sinusoidal_trajectory}
\end{equation}

where \(A\) is the desired amplitude, \(A_{\mathrm{EE}}\) is the measured amplitude, \(\omega\) is the angular frequency, and \(\varphi\) is the phase delay.
The corresponding time delay is

\begin{equation}
\Delta t
=
\frac{\varphi}{\omega}.
\label{eq:phase_to_time_delay}
\end{equation}

A larger phase delay means that the measured end-effector motion follows the desired trajectory later in time.
This effect becomes more important for faster trajectories, because the controller has less time to react to changes in the desired motion.

In this work, the phase delay is evaluated qualitatively by comparing the desired and measured trajectory plots.
For fixed set-point and compliance experiments, phase delay is not considered because the desired pose remains constant.

\subsection{Overshoot}

Overshoot describes whether the response exceeds the desired value before settling.
In the error plots, overshoot appears when an error component crosses zero and reaches a peak with the opposite sign.

Because the norm of the error is always non-negative, overshoot is evaluated using the individual error components,

\begin{equation}
e_{p,x}, \qquad e_{p,y}, \qquad e_{p,z},
\label{eq:position_error_components}
\end{equation}

and

\begin{equation}
e_{R,x}, \qquad e_{R,y}, \qquad e_{R,z}.
\label{eq:rotation_error_components}
\end{equation}

For one error component \(e_i(t)\), the overshoot is evaluated after the first zero crossing.
If no zero crossing occurs, the overshoot is considered to be zero for that component.
A large overshoot indicates an underdamped response, while little or no overshoot indicates a more strongly damped response.

\subsection{Oscillation Behavior}

The oscillation behavior is evaluated from the error curves.
A well-damped response is characterized by errors that decrease smoothly toward zero without repeated large peaks.
In contrast, repeated sign changes and large alternating peaks indicate oscillatory behavior.

This evaluation is mainly qualitative and is based on the plotted positional and rotational error components.
The damping matrices \(D_{p,0}\) and \(D_{R,0}\) are expected to reduce oscillations by dissipating energy from the virtual spring-damper system.

\subsection{Compliance Behavior}

The compliance behavior describes how much the end-effector yields when an external force is applied.
In the compliance experiments, the desired pose is kept constant while the end-effector is manually displaced by an external interaction.

The displacement caused by the external interaction is evaluated from the positional error.
Since

\begin{equation}
\mathbf{e}_p
=
\mathbf{p}_d
-
p_{\mathrm{EE}},
\end{equation}

the displacement from the desired position can be described by the error norm \(\|\mathbf{e}_p(t)\|\).
The maximum displacement during the interaction is therefore

\begin{equation}
\Delta p_{\max}
=
\max_t \|\mathbf{e}_p(t)\|.
\label{eq:maximum_compliance_displacement}
\end{equation}

A larger value of \(\Delta p_{\max}\) indicates a more compliant behavior, while a smaller value indicates a stiffer behavior.
To evaluate the influence of stiffness, the compliance experiment is repeated with different positional stiffness values.
For the same type of external interaction, the lower-stiffness setting is expected to show a larger displacement than the higher-stiffness setting.

After the external force is removed, the end-effector should return smoothly to the desired pose.
The return motion is evaluated using the positional error, overshoot, and oscillation behavior.

\subsection{Commanded Joint Torques}

The commanded joint torques are evaluated to verify that the controller remains within safe actuation limits.
For each joint, the maximum absolute commanded torque is computed as

\begin{equation}
\tau_{i,\max}
=
\max_t |\tau_i(t)|,
\qquad
i=1,\dots,n.
\label{eq:maximum_joint_torque}
\end{equation}

The torque signals should remain bounded and smooth.
Sudden peaks or large oscillations in the torque signals may indicate overly high stiffness values, insufficient damping, or numerical issues in the controller.

\section{Offline Evaluation Procedure}

The logged CSV data are evaluated offline using Python.
First, the CSV file is loaded and the positional and rotational error norms are computed.
Then, the maximum errors, settling times, steady-state errors, overshoot values, phase delay, compliance displacement, and maximum joint torques are calculated.

The following signals are plotted over time:

\begin{equation}
\mathbf{e}_p(t),
\qquad
\|\mathbf{e}_p(t)\|,
\qquad
\mathbf{e}_R(t),
\qquad
\|\mathbf{e}_R(t)\|,
\qquad
\mathbf{f}(t),
\qquad
\mathbf{m}(t),
\qquad
\tau(t).
\label{eq:evaluation_plotted_signals}
\end{equation}

The position and orientation error plots are used to analyze convergence and overshoot.
For trajectory experiments, the desired and measured position plots are additionally used to evaluate phase delay qualitatively.
For the compliance experiments, the position error plot is additionally used to determine the displacement caused by the external interaction.
The force and moment plots show how the impedance controller reacts to the error.
The torque plots are used to check whether the commanded torques remain smooth and within acceptable limits.

The evaluation procedure therefore relates the control law from Chapter~2 and the real-time implementation from Chapter~3 to the recorded error, force, moment, and torque signals.

\section{Null-Space Optimization Comparison}
\label{sec:nullspace_optimization_comparison}

In an additional comparison, the influence of the null-space optimization on
the axis-constrained controller was investigated.
The goal was to evaluate whether the redundant joint motion can be influenced
without disturbing the desired free motion in the virtual plane.

The test was performed using the virtual \(y\)-\(z\) plane configuration.
In this setup, the translational \(x\)-axis is fixed while the \(y\)- and
\(z\)-directions are free:

\begin{equation}
\mathrm{fix}_p
=
\begin{bmatrix}
1 & 0 & 0
\end{bmatrix}.
\end{equation}

For the rotational part, rotations about the \(x\)- and \(y\)-axes were
regulated, while rotation about the \(z\)-axis was left free:

\begin{equation}
\mathrm{fix}_R
=
\begin{bmatrix}
1 & 1 & 0
\end{bmatrix}.
\end{equation}

Therefore, the active Cartesian task consisted only of the components
\(x\), \(R_x\), and \(R_y\).
The corresponding active Jacobian was constructed from the rows of the full
Jacobian that belong to these active components:

\begin{equation}
J_{\mathrm{active}}
=
\begin{bmatrix}
J_x\\
J_{R_x}\\
J_{R_y}
\end{bmatrix}
\in
\mathbb{R}^{3 \times 7}.
\end{equation}

The null space of this active Jacobian describes joint motions that do not
significantly affect the constrained task:

\begin{equation}
J_{\mathrm{active}}
\dot{q}_{\mathrm{null}}
\approx
0.
\end{equation}

Three controller variants were compared:

\begin{enumerate}
    \item axis-constrained controller without null-space optimization,
    \item axis-constrained controller with null-space return-to-start behavior,
    \item axis-constrained controller with only sigma-min-based null-space
    optimization.
\end{enumerate}

\subsection{Variant 1: Without Null-Space Optimization}

In the first variant, the null-space term was disabled:

\begin{equation}
\tau_{\mathrm{null}}
=
0.
\end{equation}

The torque command was therefore given by

\begin{equation}
\tau_{\mathrm{cmd}}
=
J^{\top}
F_{\mathrm{cmd}}
+
\tau_c.
\end{equation}

This variant was used as a reference.
The robot followed the virtual-plane behavior: the \(x\)-direction was
regulated, while motion in the \(y\)- and \(z\)-directions remained free.
However, the redundant joint configuration was not actively influenced.

\subsection{Variant 2: Null-Space Return to the Start Configuration}

In the second variant, a joint-space return term was added in the null space.
The intention was to keep the robot close to its initial joint configuration.
This return-to-start feedback was combined with null-space damping.

This term was projected into the null space of the active task:

\begin{equation}
\tau_{\mathrm{null}}
=
N
\tau_{\mathrm{return}}.
\end{equation}

The projector was computed as

\begin{equation}
N
=
I
-
J_{\mathrm{active}}^{\top}
\left(
J_{\mathrm{active}}
J_{\mathrm{active}}^{\top}
+
\lambda^{2}
I
\right)^{-1}
J_{\mathrm{active}}.
\end{equation}

During the experiment, this variant showed that the return-to-start term
conflicted with the desired free motion in the virtual plane.
When the robot was manually displaced in the \(y\)- or \(z\)-direction, the
controller attempted to recover the initial joint posture.
Since motion in the \(y\)- and \(z\)-axes was intended to be free, this made
the robot behave as if it wanted to move back toward the initial configuration
instead of staying naturally at the displaced position.

Therefore, the return-to-start feedback was removed from the final
controller.

\subsection{Variant 3: Sigma-Min-Based Null-Space Optimization}

In the final variant, the null-space term was used only for damping and
singular-value-based optimization.
No return-to-start term was used.
The null-space torque was defined as

\begin{equation}
\tau_{\mathrm{null}}
=
\tau_{\mathrm{damp}}
+
\tau_{\sigma}.
\end{equation}

The damping term was

\begin{equation}
\tau_{\mathrm{damp}}
=
-
D_{\mathrm{null}}
N
\dot{q}.
\end{equation}

The singular-value-based optimization was based on the smallest singular value
of the active Jacobian:

\begin{equation}
\sigma_{\min}
=
\min_i
\sigma_i
\left(
J_{\mathrm{active}}
\right).
\end{equation}

A larger value of \(\sigma_{\min}\) indicates a better conditioned
configuration.
Therefore, two small null-space motions were compared:

\begin{equation}
q_{+}
=
q
+
\alpha
n,
\qquad
q_{-}
=
q
-
\alpha
n.
\end{equation}

For both candidate configurations, the active Jacobian was recalculated and
the corresponding smallest singular values were compared:

\begin{equation}
\sigma_{\min}^{+}
=
\sigma_{\min}
\left(
J_{\mathrm{active},+}
\right),
\qquad
\sigma_{\min}^{-}
=
\sigma_{\min}
\left(
J_{\mathrm{active},-}
\right).
\end{equation}

The direction that increased the smallest singular value was selected:

\begin{equation}
s
=
\begin{cases}
+1, & \text{if } \sigma_{\min}^{+} > \sigma_{\min}^{-},\\
-1, & \text{otherwise}.
\end{cases}
\end{equation}

The resulting optimization torque was

\begin{equation}
\tau_{\sigma}
=
N
\left(
K_{\sigma}
s
\alpha
n
\right).
\end{equation}

Thus, the final torque command became

\begin{equation}
\tau_{\mathrm{cmd}}
=
J^{\top}
F_{\mathrm{cmd}}
+
\tau_{\mathrm{null}}
+
\tau_c.
\end{equation}

\subsection{Observed Behavior}

The comparison showed that the return-to-start term was not suitable for the
desired virtual-plane behavior.
Although it reduced arbitrary joint configurations, it also caused the robot
to actively move back toward the initial posture.
This conflicted with the requirement that the robot should remain freely
movable in the \(y\)-\(z\) plane.

After removing the return-to-start term, the robot behaved as intended.
It could be manually displaced in the free \(y\)- and \(z\)-directions and
remained at the displaced position instead of being pulled back to the start
configuration.
At the same time, the null-space term still provided damping and a small
singular-value-based bias toward better conditioned configurations.

The final parameter configuration was therefore chosen as

\begin{align}
\texttt{use\_nullspace\_optimization} &= 1,\\
\texttt{nullspace\_damping} &= 1.0,\\
\texttt{nullspace\_k\_sigma} &= 0.5,\\
\texttt{nullspace\_alpha} &= 0.01,\\
\texttt{nullspace\_tau\_max} &= 1.0.
\end{align}

This result shows that the null space should not be used to force the robot
back to its initial joint posture when a free virtual-plane behavior is
desired.
Instead, the null space is used only to damp redundant motion and to bias the
configuration toward a larger smallest singular value of the active Jacobian.

\section{Discussion}

The plotted results show the main properties expected from the implemented Cartesian impedance controller.
In the set-point experiment, the end-effector errors decrease from their initial values toward zero.
The position error components show zero crossings, so overshoot is present, but the oscillation amplitudes decrease with time.
This indicates that the damping terms dissipate energy and prevent sustained oscillations.
The error norms confirm the same behavior: the maximum errors occur at the beginning of the experiment, while the final part of the response has only small remaining errors.
Thus, the steady-state error is small for the selected stiffness and damping values.

The trajectory experiment shows that the measured Cartesian position follows the desired position closely.
The tracking error remains bounded and stays in the millimeter range throughout the motion.
The largest tracking errors occur during parts of the trajectory where the desired position changes direction or velocity.
This is expected because the impedance controller generates restoring forces from position and orientation errors rather than enforcing the trajectory as a rigid constraint.
Therefore, some tracking error is a natural consequence of compliant control.
The important observation is that the error remains bounded and decreases again after the transient parts of the motion.

The compliance experiment demonstrates the influence of stiffness on interaction behavior.
Before the external push, the position error is close to zero, which means that the robot has reached the desired set-point.
During the push interval, the low-stiffness setting produces a noticeably larger displacement than the high-stiffness setting.
This confirms the physical interpretation of the stiffness matrix: lower stiffness creates a softer virtual spring, while higher stiffness creates a more rigid response.
After the external force is removed, both responses return toward the desired pose.
The return motion is smooth and does not show large overshoot, which indicates that the selected damping values are suitable for the tested compliance behavior.

The torque plots provide an additional safety-related evaluation.
The largest commanded torques occur during the initial transient, when the Cartesian errors are largest.
As the end-effector approaches the desired motion or pose, the torque magnitudes decrease.
The torque signals remain smooth and bounded in the shown experiments.
No sudden discontinuities or high-frequency oscillations are visible.
This suggests that the stiffness and damping parameters used for the evaluation do not create aggressive torque commands and remain compatible with safe torque-control operation.

The influence of stiffness on the transient response can also be interpreted using a simplified one-dimensional model.
For one Cartesian direction, the impedance behavior can be approximated as a second-order spring-damper system,

\begin{equation}
M\ddot{x}
+
D\dot{x}
+
Kx
=
0.
\label{eq:second_order_impedance_direction}
\end{equation}

The natural frequency of this system is

\begin{equation}
\omega_n
=
\sqrt{\frac{K}{M}},
\label{eq:natural_frequency_stiffness}
\end{equation}

where \(M\) is the virtual mass and \(K\) is the stiffness in the considered direction.
For a well-damped second-order system, the two-percent settling time can be approximated by~\cite{Ogata2010}

\begin{equation}
t_s
\approx
\frac{4}{\zeta \omega_n},
\label{eq:settling_time_second_order}
\end{equation}

where \(\zeta\) is the damping ratio.
Substituting Eq.~\eqref{eq:natural_frequency_stiffness} into Eq.~\eqref{eq:settling_time_second_order} gives

\begin{equation}
t_s
\approx
\frac{4}{\zeta}
\sqrt{\frac{M}{K}}.
\label{eq:settling_time_stiffness_relation}
\end{equation}

Thus, for constant virtual mass \(M\) and constant damping ratio \(\zeta\), the settling time is inversely proportional to the square root of the stiffness,

\begin{equation}
t_s
\propto
\frac{1}{\sqrt{K}}.
\label{eq:settling_time_proportional_stiffness}
\end{equation}

This means that a higher stiffness generally leads to faster settling, while a lower stiffness leads to slower settling.
However, increasing the stiffness also increases the generated Cartesian forces and the commanded joint torques.
If the damping is not increased accordingly, higher stiffness can also lead to overshoot or oscillatory behavior.

The critical damping expression gives the reference value

\begin{equation}
D
=
2\sqrt{MK}.
\label{eq:critical_damping_mass_settling}
\end{equation}

If the selected damping value equals this reference, it corresponds approximately to a damping ratio of

\begin{equation}
\zeta
=
1.
\label{eq:critical_damping_ratio}
\end{equation}

In that special case, the settling time can be approximated as

\begin{equation}
t_s
\approx
\frac{4}{\sqrt{K/M}}.
\label{eq:settling_time_critical_damping}
\end{equation}

Consequently, increasing the stiffness reduces the reference settling time, but only with a square-root relation.
In particular, increasing the stiffness by a factor of four approximately halves the settling time,

\begin{equation}
K
\rightarrow
4K
\qquad
\Rightarrow
\qquad
t_s
\rightarrow
\frac{t_s}{2}.
\label{eq:stiffness_factor_settling_time}
\end{equation}

Overall, the results illustrate the central trade-off in Cartesian impedance control.
Increasing stiffness improves position regulation and reduces displacement during external interaction, but it can also increase commanded torques and may lead to stronger overshoot if the damping is not adjusted accordingly.
Lower stiffness improves compliance and makes the robot easier to displace under external forces, but it also allows larger position errors.
The controller parameters must therefore be selected according to the task: accurate tracking requires higher stiffness, while human interaction or contact tasks require a softer behavior.
The logged CSV data and the offline evaluation procedure provide a practical basis for comparing these choices using maximum error, settling time, steady-state error, overshoot, phase delay, oscillation behavior, compliance displacement, and commanded torque limits.
