\chapter{Results and Discussion}
\label{ch:results_discussion}

Seventy-five contact runs make up the measurement set, and every one of them
reached and completed set-up. Nothing was discarded. Each mean reported below
therefore rests on the full three repetitions of its setting. One repetition
departed from its two companions; it is kept in the mean and taken up in
\cref{sec:results_limitations}, where it is read as evidence about the fixture
rather than as a recording fault.

The case labels of \cref{tab:experimental_test_cases} carry through the
chapter, and Case A supplies the baseline the other four are read against.

\section{Measurement Campaign and Data Integrity}
\label{sec:results_provenance}

Every run stores its raw signals next to the one parameter set that produced
them. Each metric was then calculated twice over, once from the general data
log and once from the controller's own set-up summary; where the two
disagreed the record was inspected rather than quietly averaged.

Repeatability comes from the three repetitions of each of the \(25\) settings.
Across settings tilted about \(t_1\) the median within-setting standard
deviation of the alignment improvement is \(0.02^\circ\); about \(t_2\) it is
\(0.03^\circ\). Anything below roughly \(0.1^\circ\) is read as scatter and not
as a parameter effect, and that threshold governs every comparison in the
chapter without being restated at each one.

The values are settled ones. Over the last second of set-up the
model-estimated load moved by a median of \(0.10\,\mathrm{N}\) and the tip
angle by a median of \(0.00^\circ\); the worst of the \(75\) runs gave
\(1.10\,\mathrm{N}\) and \(0.60^\circ\). Preload does not confound the
comparisons either. Wherever the lever kept its baseline placement the steady
model-estimated normal load stayed between \(44.5\) and \(49.4\,\mathrm{N}\).

\section{EE-Inferred Alignment Against the Physical Plane}
\label{sec:results_alignment}

The primary response is the change, over the set-up interval, in the angle
between the EE-inferred tool axis and the calibrated physical plane; a positive
value means the angle closed. Roughly \(2^\circ\) of the tool's freedom about
\(y_{EE}\) sits in the gripper rather than in the robot, so the quantity is a
combined robot--gripper--tool response and leaves controller compliance and
gripper compliance unseparated.

Commanded and measured tilts do not agree, and the gap widens with the
command. Approach hands over to the descent once the EE-inferred tool axis is
within \(2^\circ\) of the commanded direction, which leaves a residual that
grows with the commanded angle: \(5^\circ\) about \(t_1\) arrived at contact as
\(2.45^\circ\), and \(10^\circ\) about \(t_2\) as \(8.45^\circ\). Every
comparison below is made against the measured first-contact angle.

\section{Case A: Commanded Tilt}
\label{sec:results_summary_table}

Pressing the tool with no commanded tilt at all made the alignment worse. The
EE-inferred misalignment grew by \(0.97^\circ\), from \(1.07^\circ\) to
\(2.04^\circ\), and that figure sets the floor under everything that follows:
with this fixture and this calibration, an improvement of about a degree or
less cannot be credited to the commanded condition.

Two things hold across the case. Tilting the tool about \(t_2\) always
recovered more than tilting it about \(t_1\) --- \(1.52^\circ\) against
\(0.94^\circ\) at the \(5^\circ\) command, \(1.06^\circ\) against
\(0.64^\circ\) at \(10^\circ\). And the larger the commanded angle, the less of
it came back: the fraction of the initial error removed fell from
\(38\,\%\) at \(5^\circ\) about \(t_1\) to \(9\,\%\) at \(10^\circ\) about the
same axis. Over this range the correction is not proportional to the error that
provokes it.

One setting misbehaves. At \(10^\circ\) about \(t_2\) the standard deviation is
\(0.92^\circ\), an order of magnitude above anything else in the table, and a
single repetition accounts for it: that run removed \(0.00^\circ\) where its
two companions, under an identical configuration, removed \(1.56^\circ\) and
\(1.62^\circ\). It stays in the mean, and \cref{sec:results_limitations}
attributes it to the mount.

Stiffening the axis being corrected returned less angle on both tangents. With
the tool tilted about \(t_1\), the improvement falls monotonically as

No tested setting beat the \(5\,\mathrm{Nm/rad}\) baseline. Since the two
tangent entries moved one at a time, the finding is axis-specific and says
nothing about a common paired value.

Softening the perpendicular direction helped on one tangent only. With the tool

\section{Case D: Virtual Centre of Compliance}
\label{sec:results_pole}

Case D holds every gain at its baseline and moves only the lever
\(r_c=p_{\mathrm{TCP}}-p_c\). The lever runs along the surface tangent
perpendicular to the tilted one, which pairs a \(t_1\) tilt with
\(r_{c,t_2}\) and a \(t_2\) tilt with \(r_{c,t_1}\). A fourth column moves the
compliance centre onto the centre of the grinding face, \(20\,\mathrm{mm}\) along
the tool axis from the end-effector origin and so off the tangent plane the
sweep lies in.

Nothing else tested came close to the lever. Placed on the favourable side it
took the residual misalignment from \(6.99^\circ\) to \(0.93^\circ\) with the
tool tilted about \(t_1\), and from \(8.50^\circ\) to \(2.14^\circ\) about
\(t_2\) --- improvements of \(6.05^\circ\) and \(6.36^\circ\), about ten and
five times what the same gains recovered with the lever at zero.

Which side the lever sits on decides the outcome, and the favourable side is
not the same for the two axes: correcting a \(t_1\) tilt calls for
\(r_{c,t_2}=-60\,\mathrm{mm}\), correcting a \(t_2\) tilt for
\(r_{c,t_1}=+60\,\mathrm{mm}\). The wrong side removes the correction rather
than reducing it. Tilted about \(t_2\) with \(r_{c,t_1}=-60\,\mathrm{mm}\), the
tool recovered \(0.02\pm0.06^\circ\), indistinguishable from being left where
it started.

Contact was lighter at the two favourable placements as well: a steady
model-estimated normal load of \(36.5\,\mathrm{N}\) and \(39.2\,\mathrm{N}\)
against roughly \(45\) to \(47\,\mathrm{N}\) at zero lever, with the commanded
virtual press unchanged throughout.

Moving the centre off the plane did nothing. The face-centre runs gave
\(0.59^\circ\) and \(1.35^\circ\) where the matching zero-lever runs gave
\(0.56^\circ\) and \(1.38^\circ\), differences below the interpretation
threshold. Within the investigated configuration and parameter range, a
displacement of the compliance centre along the surface normal produced no
detectable change, while \(60\,\mathrm{mm}\) along a tangent moved the response
by several degrees.

\section{Case E: Tilt About the Tool Axes}
\label{sec:results_case_e}

If the asymmetry between the two tangents belonged to the tool rather than to
the surface, commanding the tilt about the tool's own axes ought to reproduce
it. The commanded spin leaves the long axis of the grinding face \(25^\circ\)
from \(t_2\), so a tangent-axis tilt tips the long and the short axis of the
face together; Case E instead commands the same \(10^\circ\) about
\(y_{EE}\) and \(x_{EE}\).

Both tool-axis results fall between the two tangent-axis ones and lie
\(0.22^\circ\) apart, against the \(0.42^\circ\) that separates the tangent

\(1.17^\circ\) for \(y_{EE}\); the runs gave \(1.29^\circ\) and

The asymmetry therefore belongs to the surface frame and not to the geometry of
the grinding face, and a mixed command lands near the linear combination of its
tangent components. Four settings support that reading. It remains a
model-based interpretation, and superposition outside the tested range does not
follow from it.

\section{Null-Space Validation Status}
\label{sec:results_nullspace}

The null-space damping term was active in every reported run, and no sustained
oscillation was observed with the selected damping. That is the whole of the
null-space evidence. The active singular-value bias stayed disabled and no
matched conditioning comparison was recorded, so the chapter makes no
experimental claim about improved singularity conditioning.

\section{Discussion}
\label{sec:discussion_contact}

\subsection{Relative Influence of the Investigated Parameters}
\label{sec:discussion_pole_height}

The three parameters are not of comparable weight. A tenfold change in the
rotational stiffness of the tilted axis moved the improvement by at most
\(0.44^\circ\); a roughly sevenfold change in the perpendicular translational
stiffness, by at most \(0.73^\circ\). Sixty millimetres of lever moved it by up
to \(5.5^\circ\) and, taken the wrong way, removed the correction outright.

A tuning order follows from that ranking. Place the virtual centre of
compliance first, sign included, and refine the stiffness entries afterwards.
Within the investigated configuration and parameter range the normal coordinate
of the centre added little once the in-plane placement had been accounted for.

\subsection{Geometric Interpretation of the Lever}

None of the sign dependence is unexpected once the point-shifted law is written
out. Shifting the compliance centre along the tangent perpendicular to the
tilted axis converts part of the commanded normal penetration into a moment
about that axis, and the sign of the lever sets the direction of the moment:
one sign reinforces the rotation that brings the face towards the surface, the
other opposes it. Exchanging the roles of \(t_1\) and \(t_2\) reverses the
cross product relating lever to tilted axis, which is why the favourable side
swaps between them.

The lighter contact at the favourable placements has the same source. A tool
that rotates further into conformity takes up part of the commanded penetration
itself, so the model-estimated load falls even though the press was never
changed.

\subsection{Practical Implications for Controller Tuning}

In practice the measured ordering prescribes a sequence. Choose the
compliance-centre lever first, on the tangent perpendicular to the expected
tilt and with the sign that suits that axis, since it sets how much correction
is available at all. Then leave the rotational stiffness of the tilted axis as
low as the required angular compliance and the allowable displacement permit:
every increase tested gave back less angle. The perpendicular translational
stiffness comes last, and only where the tool is tilted about \(t_2\); on
\(t_1\) it did not move the response measurably.

\section{Limitations}
\label{sec:results_limitations}

Within the gripper the tool can turn by approximately \(2^\circ\) about
\(y_{EE}\), and the commanded spin leaves \(y_{EE}\) only \(25^\circ\) from
\(t_2\). Tilted about \(t_2\), then, the compliant direction of the mount
nearly coincides with the axis being corrected, and part of the contact moment
can be taken up by the tool turning relative to the end-effector. Alignment is
inferred from the end-effector pose. Motion of that kind is invisible to the
metric and shows up as a smaller improvement.

The \(10^\circ\) \(t_2\) repetition that removed \(0.00^\circ\) is read as an
instance of exactly this. It is retained because it is a property of the
fixture rather than a recording fault, and because it is the clearest evidence
in the measurement set that the limitation bites. The consequence runs one way
only: unobserved motion at the mount can conceal correction that occurred but
cannot manufacture it, which makes the reported \(t_2\) improvements lower
bounds and leaves the measured tangent-axis asymmetry standing.

The dispersion statistics fit that account. The median within-setting standard
deviation is \(0.02^\circ\) on \(t_1\) and \(0.03^\circ\) on \(t_2\), and the
two largest dispersions in the measurement set both occur at \(t_2\).
Dispersion grows with the size of the correction on both axes, though, so the
tilt axis does not account for it alone.

Three further limitations apply. The alignment metric describes the combined
robot--gripper--tool response and cannot separate controller compliance from
gripper compliance; a physically flat tool may coexist with a nonzero inferred
residual. Every commanded tilt was positive, so any asymmetry between positive
and negative tilt remains unmeasured. And the compliance-centre sweep used
three tangential levels on one axis at a time, which locates the favourable
sign and establishes the size of the effect, but identifies neither an optimum
nor any interaction between the two in-plane coordinates.
