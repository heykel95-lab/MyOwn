\chapter{Results and Discussion}
\label{ch:results_discussion}

The surface-contact experiments evaluate how the controller parameters
influence the end-effector rotation generated during contact set-up. The
primary response quantity is the signed set-up rotation
\mbox{\(\Delta\theta_i\)} about the investigated surface tangent
\mbox{\(t_i\)}. It is measured from the beginning to the end of set-up, and
its sign follows the surface-frame rotation convention of
\mbox{\Cref{sec:experimental_evaluation}}.

The commanded tool orientation offset \mbox{\(\theta_{t_i}\)} defines the
initial experimental condition, whereas the measured response is the signed
end-effector set-up rotation \mbox{\(\Delta\theta_i\)}. The main response
figures therefore report \mbox{\(\Delta\theta_i\)}, while the corresponding
numerical values are provided in \mbox{\Cref{app:additional_plots}}. The
pose-based alignment estimate and the TCP-height flatness classification are
retained only as supporting checks in the same appendix.

Each parameter setting was repeated three times. The response figures show the
mean over the repetitions, with the observed spread reported where relevant. The preceding orientation phase terminated with a finite tracking
tolerance, so the initial angular magnitude differed slightly from the
commanded value. A commanded \mbox{\(+10^\circ\)} offset reached approximately
\mbox{\(9.3^\circ\)} about \mbox{\(t_1\)} and \mbox{\(10.0^\circ\)} about
\mbox{\(t_2\)}. The commanded offset identifies each experimental condition.

\section{Main Surface-Contact Results}
\label{sec:results_main_contact_cases}

The cases are reported in the order in which they constrain the choice of
compliance-centre position. The TCP-centred reference is reported in Case~A,
followed by the stiffness sensitivities in Cases~B and~C. In Case~D the
tangential compliance-centre displacement was tested over both surface tangents
and both signs of the commanded orientation offset. The orientation-magnitude,
definition-frame, tool-axis, and intermediate-direction comparisons are
reported as supporting checks in
\mbox{\Cref{app:supporting_contact_checks}}.

\subsection{Baseline Contact Response with the CoC at the TCP: Case~A}
\label{sec:results_case_a}

In Case~A the \abbr{CoC} was placed at the TCP, so
\mbox{\(r_c=0\)}. This condition removes the additional
translation--rotation coupling introduced by the virtual point shift. The
ordinary rotational impedance and physical contact geometry remain. The
measured response therefore provides the baseline for the stiffness and
compliance-centre-position comparisons.

\begin{figure}[H]
\centering
\input{figures/results_case_a_bars.tex}
\caption{Measured set-up rotation with the CoC at the TCP.}
\label{fig:results_case_a}
\end{figure}

With the \abbr{CoC} at the TCP, the measured response differs strongly between
the two surface tangents. For the positive \mbox{\(10^\circ\)} conditions,
\Cref{fig:results_case_a} gives \mbox{\(+7.57^\circ\)} about
\mbox{\(t_1\)} and \mbox{\(-1.63^\circ\)} about \mbox{\(t_2\)}.
Reversing the commanded orientation changes the response to
\mbox{\(-10.83^\circ\)} and \mbox{\(-1.25^\circ\)}, respectively.
The figure shows all five baseline conditions. The TCP-centred
condition therefore provides the reference against which the stiffness and
compliance-centre-position variations are compared.

\subsection{Effect of Cartesian Stiffness: Cases~B and~C}

\subsubsection{Rotational Stiffness: Case~B}
\label{sec:results_case_b}

In Case~B the rotational stiffness about the investigated tangent was varied
while the other controller settings remained unchanged. The
\mbox{\(5\,\mathrm{N\,m/rad}\)} condition is the corresponding TCP-centred
reference from Case~A.

\begin{figure}[H]
\centering
\includegraphics[width=0.85\textwidth]{figures/MAIN_B_KR.pdf}
\caption{Measured set-up rotation by rotational stiffness.}
\label{fig:results_case_b}
\end{figure}

Increasing \mbox{\(K_{R,t_i}\)} reduced the measured rotation over the tested
range. About \mbox{\(t_1\)}, \Cref{fig:results_case_b} shows a decrease from
\mbox{\(+7.57^\circ\)} at \mbox{\(5\,\mathrm{N\,m/rad}\)} to
\mbox{\(+2.73^\circ\)} at \mbox{\(50\,\mathrm{N\,m/rad}\)}. About
\mbox{\(t_2\)}, the response remained small and changed from
\mbox{\(-1.63^\circ\)} to \mbox{\(-0.48^\circ\)}. Rotational stiffness
therefore changed the response magnitude without removing the directional
difference observed in Case~A.

\subsubsection{Cross-Axis Translational Stiffness: Case~C}
\label{sec:results_case_c}

In Case~C the translational stiffness perpendicular to the investigated
rotation axis was varied. The rotational stiffness about that axis was held at
\mbox{\(5\,\mathrm{N\,m/rad}\)}. The \mbox{\(2000\,\mathrm{N/m}\)}
condition is the corresponding TCP-centred reference from Case~A.

\begin{figure}[H]
\centering
\includegraphics[width=0.85\textwidth]{figures/MAIN_C_KP.pdf}
\caption{Measured set-up rotation by cross-axis translational stiffness.}
\label{fig:results_case_c}
\end{figure}

The influence of perpendicular translational stiffness was considerably
smaller than that of rotational stiffness. About \mbox{\(t_1\)}, the measured
rotation remained within \mbox{\(0.03^\circ\)} across the tested range. About
\mbox{\(t_2\)}, \Cref{fig:results_case_c} shows a change of
\mbox{\(0.61^\circ\)}, from \mbox{\(-2.24^\circ\)} to
\mbox{\(-1.63^\circ\)}. Varying this stiffness alone therefore did not account
for the directional difference in the baseline response.

\subsection{Effect of Tangential CoC Position: Case~D}
\label{sec:results_case_d}

In Case~D the tangential position of the \abbr{CoC} was varied while the
Cartesian gains remained unchanged. For each rotation axis, the centre was
moved along the surface tangent perpendicular to that axis. The TCP-centred
condition is included as the zero-position reference.

\begin{figure}[H]
\centering
\input{figures/results_case_d_panels.tex}
\caption{Set-up rotation by tangential CoC position (mean \(\pm\) SD).}
\label{fig:results_case_d}
\end{figure}

Tangential CoC position produced the largest response variation among the
tested controller parameters. As shown in
\mbox{\Cref{fig:results_case_d}}, reversing the initial
orientation condition changes which side of the TCP produces the larger
response. About \mbox{\(t_1\)}, the positive condition gives
\mbox{\(+7.87^\circ\)} at \mbox{\(+40\,\mathrm{mm}\)} and
\mbox{\(+0.18^\circ\)} at \mbox{\(-40\,\mathrm{mm}\)}. The reversed
condition exchanges the larger response between the two sides.

Changing the rotation axis also changes the required tangential direction.
For a positive condition about \mbox{\(t_2\)}, the response changes from
\mbox{\(-1.63^\circ\)} at the TCP to \mbox{\(+4.43^\circ\)} at the selected
\mbox{\(+40\,\mathrm{mm}\)} position. The displacement lies along
\mbox{\(+t_2\)} for rotation about \mbox{\(t_1\)} and along
\mbox{\(-t_1\)} for rotation about \mbox{\(t_2\)}. Within the investigated
configuration, a non-zero tangential \abbr{CoC} therefore cannot be selected
independently of the direction and sign of the initial angular condition.

The error bars in \mbox{\Cref{fig:results_case_d}} show that two transition conditions had larger spread than the other lever
positions. The positive \mbox{\(t_1\)} condition at
\mbox{\(-20\,\mathrm{mm}\)} had a standard deviation of
\mbox{\(0.44^\circ\)}. The positive \mbox{\(t_2\)} condition at
\mbox{\(-40\,\mathrm{mm}\)} had a standard deviation of
\mbox{\(2.36^\circ\)}.

The positive \mbox{\(t_1\)} condition was examined in the time domain to
relate the rotation response to the commanded wrench. Its two outer positions
produce a large difference in \mbox{\(\Delta\theta_1\)}, while the
TCP-centred condition provides the zero-position reference.

\begin{figure}[H]
\centering
\includegraphics[width=0.85\textwidth]{figures/MAIN_D_wrench.pdf}
\caption{Measured set-up rotation, commanded normal force, and commanded TCP
moment for three tangential CoC positions.}
\label{fig:results_case_d_wrench}
\end{figure}

\Cref{fig:results_case_d_wrench} places the set-up rotation
\mbox{\(\Delta\theta_1\)}, the commanded normal force
\mbox{\(F_n\)}, and the commanded TCP moment
\mbox{\(M_{t_1}\)} on a common time axis for three
centre positions. Every condition shown carries a commanded orientation offset
of \mbox{\(+10^\circ\)} about \mbox{\(t_1\)}. At \mbox{\(-40\)}, \mbox{\(0\)}, and
\mbox{\(+40\,\mathrm{mm}\)}, the commanded normal force settles in a similar
range of approximately \mbox{\(-77\)} to \mbox{\(-82\,\mathrm{N}\)}, while the
commanded TCP moment about \mbox{\(t_1\)} settles at approximately
\mbox{\(+3.28\)}, \mbox{\(+0.66\)}, and \mbox{\(-2.44\,\mathrm{N\,m}\)}. The
outer positions therefore produce moments of similar magnitude and opposite
sign while their set-up rotations differ by \mbox{\(7.73^\circ\)}. The
difference in set-up rotation is therefore associated primarily with the moment
generated by the displaced impedance reference rather than with a substantial
change in the normal pressing force.

A tangential displacement of the \abbr{CoC} moves the impedance reference
point away from the TCP, so the translational press is coupled to the rotational
part of the impedance. The complete commanded moment is
\mbox{\(m=m_R+r_c\times f\)}, with
\mbox{\(r_c=p_c-p_{\mathrm{TCP}}\)}. A non-zero tangential displacement
therefore adds a moment contribution from the commanded press. This explains
why the commanded moment changes strongly with centre position while
\mbox{\(F_n\)} remains within a similar range. Reversing the tangential
displacement reverses the contribution \mbox{\(r_c\times f_n\)} generated by
the normal press.

The measured sign reversal follows the relation derived in
\mbox{\Cref{subsec:coc_tangential_displacement}}. A fixed tangential
\mbox{\(r_c\neq0\)} retains one preferred moment direction while the press
continues. At \mbox{\(r_c=0\)}, the point-shift contribution vanishes and the
TCP selects no tangential direction. The TCP-centred condition is therefore
the direction-neutral fixed reference among the tested positions. A displaced
centre provides greater condition-specific rotational authority where the
required direction is known.

The grinding phase returns to the decoupled impedance branch described in
\mbox{\Cref{subsec:implementation_grinding_phase}}. This phase structure is
consistent with removing the direction-selective coupling after set-up. It is
an implementation property rather than an experimental result, because the
reported runs ended at the pre-grinding gate.

Across Cases~A--D, tangential CoC position produced the largest measured
response variation among the tested controller parameters.

\section{Null-Space Pose-Hold Results}
\label{sec:results_nullspace}

The remaining experiment is separate from the contact cases and analyses the
redundant joint-space direction while the Cartesian pose is held. Matched
trials compare the selectable null-space terms under the same internally
commanded point-force-equivalent disturbance. The commanded disturbance
reached \mbox{\(20.00\,\mathrm{N}\)} in all twelve trials, with peak equivalent
joint-torque norms between \mbox{\(2.17\)} and
\mbox{\(2.45\,\mathrm{N\,m}\)}. The torque-limit scale remained equal to one,
so the waveform was applied without clipping.

\begin{figure}[H]
\centering
\includegraphics[width=0.96\textwidth]{figures/MAIN_NS_nullspace_automatic.pdf}
\caption{Null-space pose-hold response.}
\label{fig:results_nullspace_automatic}
\end{figure}

\Cref{fig:results_nullspace_automatic} reports the response during the
Cartesian pose-hold disturbance test in two panels. Panel (a) gives the mean
cumulative projected null-space motion \mbox{\(E_N\)} without null-space torque
and with projected damping, the shaded bands showing \mbox{\(\pm\)} one
standard deviation across the three repetitions. Panel (b) gives the change in
the minimum Jacobian singular value over the disturbance interval,
\mbox{\(\Delta\sigma_{\min,\mathrm{dist}}\)}, together with the peak Cartesian
position error, for the two singular-value conditioning magnitudes; its error
bars show \mbox{\(\pm\)} one standard deviation and the dashed line marks the
\mbox{\(2\,\mathrm{mm}\)} task-retention limit. The values for all four
settings are given below. Projected null-space damping
reduced \mbox{\(E_N\)} from \mbox{\((7.596\pm0.916)^\circ\)} without
null-space torque to \mbox{\((5.687\pm0.228)^\circ\)} at
\mbox{\(d_{\mathrm{null}}=2\,\mathrm{N\,m\,s/rad}\)}, a reduction of
approximately \mbox{\(25\,\%\)}. The net redundant displacement fell in the
same proportion, from \mbox{\(0.131\pm0.016\,\mathrm{rad}\)} to
\mbox{\(0.098\pm0.004\,\mathrm{rad}\)}, and the change in
\mbox{\(\sigma_{\min}\)} across the disturbance interval became less
negative, from \mbox{\((-2.13\pm0.47)\times10^{-3}\)} to
\mbox{\((-1.26\pm0.10)\times10^{-3}\)}. Damping acts on the projected joint
velocity through \mbox{\(\tau_d=-d_{\mathrm{null}}N_\tau\dot q\)}, so it
vanishes as the redundant motion stops and requests no return towards the
initial configuration.

Singular-value conditioning behaved differently from both the uncontrolled and
the damping-only settings. It was already active during the
\mbox{\(5\,\mathrm{s}\)} preceding the disturbance and remained active
throughout it, so it did not act as a term applied after the redundant
configuration had been displaced. Both sigma-only settings ended the
disturbance interval with a net redundant displacement whose magnitude was
smaller than the scatter across the three repetitions:
\mbox{\((0.3\pm0.3)\times10^{-3}\,\mathrm{rad}\)} at
\mbox{\(k_\sigma=1.5\,\mathrm{N\,m}\)} and
\mbox{\((-0.1\pm0.2)\times10^{-3}\,\mathrm{rad}\)} at
\mbox{\(2.0\,\mathrm{N\,m}\)}. Both are smaller than the
\mbox{\(0.131\,\mathrm{rad}\)} measured without null-space torque by more
than two orders of magnitude. Over the same interval
\mbox{\(\sigma_{\min}\)} changed by
\mbox{\((1.95\pm0.07)\times10^{-5}\)} and
\mbox{\((1.93\pm0.03)\times10^{-5}\)}, where it fell by
\mbox{\((-2.13\pm0.47)\times10^{-3}\)} without null-space torque. Under the
tested disturbance direction, the conditioning term acted concurrently with
the commanded disturbance and strongly suppressed the resulting redundant
displacement.

The two sigma magnitudes differed in motion activity rather than in net
displacement. At \mbox{\(k_\sigma=2.0\,\mathrm{N\,m}\)} the cumulative
projected null-space motion was \mbox{\((1.619\pm0.130)^\circ\)}, against
\mbox{\((0.283\pm0.007)^\circ\)} at \mbox{\(1.5\,\mathrm{N\,m}\)}, a
factor of about six, while both net displacements stayed near zero.
The larger torque magnitude was associated with greater switching activity near
the locally preferred region. This is consistent with the form of the law: the
conditioning term commands a fixed torque magnitude along the selected
direction, and the selector \mbox{\(s_\sigma\)} is set to zero only inside
the comparison deadband. On that reading the larger cumulative motion is
switching activity around the same region rather than poorer disturbance
rejection. At \mbox{\(1.5\,\mathrm{N\,m}\)} the mean
peak Cartesian position error was also lower,
\mbox{\(0.889\pm0.024\,\mathrm{mm}\)} against
\mbox{\(0.983\pm0.053\,\mathrm{mm}\)}. The largest position error measured
in the complete pose-hold study was \mbox{\(1.304\,\mathrm{mm}\)}, below
the predefined \mbox{\(2\,\mathrm{mm}\)} acceptance limit. Of the two
tested magnitudes, \mbox{\(1.5\,\mathrm{N\,m}\)} achieved the same
suppression of net disturbance-induced displacement with substantially less
redundant motion.

The pose-hold trials compare the four selectable null-space modes under the
repeatable commanded disturbance. The two terms act on different quantities.
Projected damping dissipates redundant joint motion that is already present,
whereas the singular-value term drives the redundant configuration towards the
sampled neighbour with the larger \mbox{\(\sigma_{\min}\)} whether or not
such motion is present. The conditioning law is not dissipative: a disturbance
acting towards larger \mbox{\(\sigma_{\min}\)} would move the
configuration in the same direction as \mbox{\(\tau_\sigma\)} rather than
against it, so the rejection observed here is specific to the tested
disturbance direction. Both terms act through the null-space projector, so the
Cartesian task stays primary.

Several bounds apply to these results. Because the conditioning torque was
active before the disturbance, the sigma-only trials entered it from a redundant
configuration displaced by approximately
\mbox{\(0.013\,\mathrm{rad}\)} from the one at which the trials without
conditioning began. The four settings therefore compare the closed-loop
behaviour of the complete modes. They do not isolate an instantaneous opposing
torque at identical joint configurations. The surface-contact experiments use
both contributions together in the combined secondary-torque mode and therefore
do not separate their individual effects during contact. The geometric Jacobian
combines translational and rotational rows without a common length scale, so
the reported \mbox{\(\sigma_{\min}\)} is used only as a relative indicator
under the fixed Jacobian convention.
