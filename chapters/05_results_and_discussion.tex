\chapter{Results and Discussion}
\label{ch:results_discussion}

Every contact run reached and completed set-up, and all runs were retained for
analysis. Each mean reported below therefore rests on the full three repetitions
of its setting. One repetition
departed from its two companions; it is kept in the mean and taken up in
\cref{sec:results_limitations}, where it is read as evidence about the tool
mount rather than as a recording fault.

The case labels of \cref{tab:experimental_test_cases} carry through the
chapter, and Case A supplies the baseline the other four are read against.

\section{Measurement Campaign and Data Integrity}
\label{sec:results_provenance}

The raw signals of every run are stored next to the one parameter set that
produced them. Each metric was then calculated twice over, once from the
general data log and once from the controller's own set-up summary. When the
two calculations differed, the corresponding record was inspected and the
discrepancy was resolved before analysis.

Repeatability comes from the three repetitions of each of the \(25\) settings.
Across settings tilted about \(t_1\) the median within-setting standard
deviation of the alignment improvement is \(0.02^\circ\); about \(t_2\) it is
\(0.03^\circ\). Anything below roughly \(0.1^\circ\) is read as scatter and not
as a parameter effect, and that threshold governs every comparison in the
chapter without being restated at each one.

The reported values are settled ones. Over the last second of set-up the
model-estimated load moved by a median of \(0.10\,\mathrm{N}\) and the tip
angle by a median of \(0.00^\circ\); the worst run gave
\(1.10\,\mathrm{N}\) and \(0.60^\circ\). Preload does not confound the
comparisons either. Wherever the lever kept its baseline placement the steady
model-estimated normal load stayed between \(44.5\) and \(49.4\,\mathrm{N}\).

\section{EE-Inferred Alignment Against the Physical Plane}
\label{sec:results_alignment}

The primary response is the change, over the set-up interval, in the angle
between the EE-inferred tool axis and the calibrated physical plane; a positive
value means the angle closed. Roughly \(2^\circ\) of the tool's freedom about
\(y_{EE}\) sits in the gripper rather than in the robot, so the quantity is a
combined robot--gripper--tool response and leaves controller compliance and
gripper compliance unseparated.

Commanded and measured tilts do not agree, and the gap widens with the
command. Approach hands over to the descent once the EE-inferred tool axis is
within \(2^\circ\) of the commanded direction, which leaves a residual that
grows with the commanded angle: \(5^\circ\) about \(t_1\) arrived at contact as
\(2.45^\circ\), and \(10^\circ\) about \(t_2\) as \(8.45^\circ\). Every
comparison below is made against the measured first-contact angle.

\section{Case D: Virtual Centre of Compliance}
\label{sec:results_pole}

\(r_c=p_{\mathrm{TCP}}-p_c\) was moved. The lever was directed along the surface

\(r_{c,t_2}\) and a \(t_2\) tilt with \(r_{c,t_1}\). In a fourth column the

\(20\,\mathrm{mm}\) along the tool axis from the end-effector origin and
therefore off the tangent plane in which the sweep lies.

Each tilted axis has one lever direction that improves alignment and one that
does not. The improving direction is called the \emph{favourable} direction
here, and it is the one for which the moment generated by the commanded
penetration rotates the face towards the surface.

With the lever in that direction, the residual misalignment fell from
\(6.99^\circ\) to \(0.93^\circ\) with the tool tilted about \(t_1\), and from
\(8.50^\circ\) to \(2.14^\circ\) about \(t_2\), as \cref{tab:results_case_d}
shows. These improvements of \(6.05^\circ\) and \(6.36^\circ\) are about ten and
five times what the same gains recovered with the lever at zero. Within the
tested range they are the largest effect produced by any parameter investigated
in this campaign.

The favourable direction is not the same for the two tangents. Correcting a
\(t_1\) tilt required a negative lever along \(t_2\),
\(r_{c,t_2}=-60\,\mathrm{mm}\), whereas correcting a \(t_2\) tilt required a
positive lever along \(t_1\), \(r_{c,t_1}=+60\,\mathrm{mm}\), so the favourable
lever signs were opposite for the two tilted axes.

Applying \(r_{c,t_1}=-60\,\mathrm{mm}\) in the \(t_2\) condition produced only
\(0.02\pm0.06^\circ\), so no improvement above the repeatability scale was
measured. The sign of the in-plane lever therefore determined whether the
induced moment assisted the corrective rotation.

Contact was also lighter at the two favourable placements, at steady
model-estimated normal loads of \(36.5\,\mathrm{N}\) and \(39.2\,\mathrm{N}\)
against roughly \(45\) to \(47\,\mathrm{N}\) at zero lever, with the commanded
virtual press unchanged throughout.

The face-centre settings moved the centre \(20\,\mathrm{mm}\) along the tool
axis, from the TCP towards the surface, which places it at the centre of the
grinding face. Resolved in the surface frame at the \(10^\circ\) commanded tilt
this gives \(r_c=(0,-3.5,19.7)\,\mathrm{mm}\) for the \(t_1\) condition and
\((3.5,0,19.7)\,\mathrm{mm}\) for \(t_2\); the tangential component is not a
design choice but \(20\sin10^\circ\), the projection of an axial offset onto the
tangent plane. They
produced \(0.59^\circ\) and \(1.35^\circ\), compared with \(0.56^\circ\) and
\(1.38^\circ\) at zero lever, and these differences remained below the
interpretation threshold.

That outcome is what the point-shifted law predicts, and it is worth separating
from the tangential result rather than reading as a second null finding. With
the press directed along the surface normal, \(f\approx-Fn_s\), the component of
\(r_c\) along \(n_s\) is parallel to \(f\) and contributes nothing to
\(m_c=-r_c\times f\) of \cref{eq:pole_translation_moment_sign_check}. Only the
tangential components of the lever generate the coupling moment. The
face-centre setting therefore tests \(3.5\,\mathrm{mm}\) of effective
tangential lever, about a seventeenth of the \(60\,\mathrm{mm}\) used in the
sweep, and its null result is consistent with that magnitude rather than with a
property of the normal coordinate itself.

An axial displacement contributes an effective tangential lever of
\(d\sin\theta\) for a displacement \(d\) and a commanded tilt \(\theta\), so at
\(10^\circ\) it takes \(345\,\mathrm{mm}\) along the tool axis to match the
\(60\,\mathrm{mm}\) reached directly in the tangent plane. The axial coordinate
is therefore an inefficient way to generate the alignment moment, whichever
sense it is moved in.

Only the displacement towards the surface was tested. Moving the centre the
other way, from the TCP back towards the flange, reverses the sign of that small
tangential component and would be expected to bias the rotation in the opposite
sense; this was not measured. Within this campaign, then, the axial coordinate
was sampled at one displacement in one sense, and the measurements support only
the statement that the alignment moment is generated by the tangential lever, of
which \(60\,\mathrm{mm}\) moved the response by several degrees.

\section{Null-Space Validation Status}
\label{sec:results_nullspace}

Null-space damping was active in all reported runs, and no sustained oscillation
was observed with the selected damping. However, no matched trials were
performed with the singular-value term enabled and disabled. Its effect on
conditioning or on contact alignment therefore cannot be concluded from the
present campaign.

\section{Discussion}
\label{sec:discussion_contact}

\subsection{Relative Influence of the Investigated Parameters}
\label{sec:discussion_pole_height}

In the investigated configuration, the compliance-centre position had a
considerably stronger effect than either the rotational or the translational
stiffness. Changing the rotational stiffness of the tilted axis over the tested
tenfold range altered the alignment improvement by no more than \(0.44^\circ\)
(\cref{tab:results_case_b}). The largest change caused by the perpendicular
translational stiffness, over a roughly sevenfold range, was \(0.73^\circ\)
(\cref{tab:results_case_c}). By comparison, shifting the compliance centre by
\(60\,\mathrm{mm}\) changed the improvement by as much as \(5.5^\circ\), and on
with the lever in the unfavourable direction almost no alignment improvement was
measured (\cref{tab:results_case_d}).

These results suggest that the compliance centre, sign included, should be
placed before the stiffness entries are refined. Within the investigated
configuration and parameter range the normal coordinate of the centre added
little once the in-plane placement had been accounted for.

\subsection{Geometric Interpretation of the Lever}

The sign dependence follows from the point-shifted law. Shifting the compliance
centre along the tangent perpendicular to the tilted axis converts part of the
commanded normal penetration into a moment about that axis, and the sign of the
lever sets the direction of that moment: one sign reinforces the rotation that
brings the face towards the surface, the other opposes it. Exchanging the roles
of \(t_1\) and \(t_2\) reverses the cross product relating the lever to the
tilted axis, which is consistent with the favourable direction being opposite
for the two tangents.

The lighter contact at the favourable placements is consistent with the same
mechanism. A tool that rotates further into conformity takes up part of the
commanded penetration itself, so the model-estimated load falls even though the
press was never changed.

Two contributions to the commanded wrench therefore respond differently to a
change in the sign of the tilt. The rotational spring \(K_{R,0}e_R\) is driven
by the measured orientation error and reverses with it, so it acts to reduce the
misalignment whichever way the tool is tilted. The moment induced by the lever
does not. With the press directed into the surface throughout set-up, the
coupling moment \(m_c=-r_c\times f\) of
\cref{eq:pole_translation_moment_sign_check} keeps a direction fixed by \(r_c\)
alone. The lever is therefore a bias whose sense is chosen in advance, not a
feedback term that adapts to the error it is correcting.

The measurements are consistent with that reading. Displacing the compliance
centre along the tool axis, a lever nearly parallel to the press and so one that
induces little moment, changed the response by less than the interpretation
threshold on both tangents. A tangential lever applied against the required
rotation instead removed the correction that the rotational spring alone had
produced, \(0.02^\circ\) against \(1.38^\circ\) at zero lever. A placement that
induces no moment leaves the response symmetric in the tilt direction; a
tangential lever selects one direction of correction and opposes the other.

Every commanded tilt in this campaign was positive, so the behaviour of a fixed
lever under a reversed tilt was not measured. The asymmetry stated here is a
model-based interpretation of
\cref{eq:pole_translation_moment_sign_check} together with the wrong-sign
measurement, and it is taken up in \cref{ch:conclusion}.

\subsection{Placement of the Virtual Centre of Compliance}
\label{sec:discussion_placement}

The centre of compliance has three spatial coordinates, and the campaign swept
only one of them. What the measurements settle, and what they leave open, is
therefore worth separating.

The coordinate that was swept is the surface tangent perpendicular to the tilted
axis, and it determines the size of the correction. Sixty millimetres in the
assisting direction raised the improvement from \(0.56^\circ\) to
\(6.05^\circ\) about \(t_1\) and from \(1.38^\circ\) to \(6.36^\circ\) about
\(t_2\), while the same distance in the other direction left \(0.41^\circ\) and
\(0.02^\circ\). Placement in this coordinate is the design decision that matters.

The coordinate along the tool axis was sampled at one displacement,
\(20\,\mathrm{mm}\) towards the surface, and produced no change above the
interpretation threshold. The point-shifted law accounts for that: while the
press stays normal to the surface, the axial part of the lever is parallel to
the applied force and generates no moment, so an axial displacement acts only
through its projection \(d\sin\theta\) onto the tangent plane. Raising the
centre above the TCP rather than lowering it towards the face reverses the sign
of that projection but not its magnitude. The axial coordinate is consequently
not a useful design freedom for this task, and the height at which the centre
sits may be chosen on other grounds.

The third coordinate, the surface tangent parallel to the tilted axis, was never
varied. A lever there would generate a moment about the perpendicular tangent,
that is about the axis that is not being corrected, so it would be expected to
introduce a misalignment rather than remove one. Nothing in this campaign tests
that expectation.

Four questions therefore remain open. The tangential sweep used three levels, so
it locates the assisting direction without identifying an optimum magnitude;
\(60\,\mathrm{mm}\) is the largest value tested, not a value shown to be best.
The axial relation rests on a single point at an effective lever of
\(3.5\,\mathrm{mm}\), which cannot separate ``the axial coordinate does not
matter'' from ``\(3.5\,\mathrm{mm}\) does not matter''. The two in-plane
coordinates were never varied together, so no interaction between them is
resolved. And every commanded tilt was positive, which leaves the assisting
direction tied to the tilted axis and the sign of the initial error at once.
\Cref{sec:future} sets out the trials that would close each of them.

\subsection{Practical Implications for Controller Tuning}

The measured ordering suggests a tuning sequence. The compliance-centre lever is
chosen first, on the tangent perpendicular to the expected tilt and with the
sign appropriate to that axis, since it determines how much correction is
available at all. The rotational stiffness of the tilted axis is then kept as
low as the required angular compliance and the allowable displacement permit,
because every increase tested returned less angle. The perpendicular
translational stiffness is adjusted last, and only where the tool is tilted
about \(t_2\); about \(t_1\) it did not move the response measurably. This
sequence is drawn from one configuration and one parameter range, and is offered
as a starting point rather than as a general rule.

That sequence assumes the direction of the initial tilt is known before contact.
Where it is not, the bias character of the lever sets a limit on what a single
fixed placement can achieve, and two options remain. If the correction must be
independent of the tilt direction, the compliance centre is left where it
induces no moment, at the TCP or on the tool axis, and the alignment then comes
from the rotational spring alone: \(0.56^\circ\) about \(t_1\) and
\(1.38^\circ\) about \(t_2\) in this configuration, symmetric in the tilt
direction but an order of magnitude below what the favourable lever recovered.
If the larger correction is required, the lever cannot be fixed at all; its
direction has to be selected for each run from the sign of the measured initial
tilt. The controller can do this, because the centre of compliance is defined in
software and is held constant only from the clearance hand-off onwards. Neither
option was tested against a negative tilt, and the trade-off between them is
stated here as a design consequence of the mechanism rather than as a measured
comparison.

\section{Limitations}
\label{sec:results_limitations}

The tool could rotate by approximately \(\pm2^\circ\) about \(y_{EE}\) relative
to the gripper. For the tested mounting orientation, \(y_{EE}\) was
approximately \(25^\circ\) from \(t_2\). Contact-induced rotation at the
gripper--tool interface may therefore have been more influential in the \(t_2\)
conditions than in the \(t_1\) conditions. Because the alignment metric was
calculated from the end-effector pose, this relative tool motion was not
observed directly.

The \(10^\circ\) \(t_2\) repetition that produced \(0.00^\circ\) of EE-inferred
improvement is consistent with this explanation, but it cannot be attributed
conclusively to the tool mount. The run was retained because no logging or
configuration error was identified, and because excluding it would understate
the observed variability.

Unmeasured motion of the tool within the gripper may cause the EE-inferred
\(t_2\) values to underestimate the physical rotation of the grinding face. The
direction and magnitude of that effect were not measured, so the \(t_2\) results
are described as potentially underestimated rather than as established lower
bounds.

The dispersion statistics are consistent with that account. The median
within-setting standard deviation is \(0.02^\circ\) on \(t_1\) and
\(0.03^\circ\) on \(t_2\), and the two largest dispersions in the measurement
set both occur at \(t_2\). Dispersion also grows with the size of the correction
on both axes, however, so the tilt axis does not account for it alone.

Three further limitations apply. The alignment metric describes the combined
robot--gripper--tool response and cannot separate controller compliance from
gripper compliance, so a physically flat tool may coexist with a nonzero
inferred residual. Every commanded tilt was positive, which leaves any asymmetry
between positive and negative tilt unmeasured. And in the compliance-centre
sweep three tangential levels were used on one axis at a time, which locates the
favourable direction and establishes the size of the effect but identifies
neither an optimum nor any interaction between the two in-plane coordinates.
