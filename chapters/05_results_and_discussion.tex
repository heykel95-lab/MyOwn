\chapter{Results and Discussion}
\label{ch:results_discussion}

The measurement set contains \(92\) run records, of which \(89\) are usable
for the quantities for which they were recorded. Three hold records are
excluded from quantitative comparison because the general log is missing or
the Cartesian task moved beyond the registered invariance limit. Each
admissible record contains the effective controller parameters and raw signals
required for its analysis.

Three validation checks precede the parameter studies: settling of the set-up
phase, equivalence of the decoupled and block-diagonal coupled formulations,
and repeatability of the nominal configuration. The subsequent studies
quantify the influence of tangential rotational stiffness and the three
surface-frame components of the virtual centre of compliance.

\section{Measurement Campaign and Data Integrity}
\label{sec:results_provenance}

Each run uses one parameter set and records the raw signals together with that
configuration. Metrics are calculated independently from the general data log
and from the controller's set-up summary. A disagreement flags the record for
inspection rather than being averaged silently. Quantitative results exclude
records with missing general data or excessive Cartesian task drift.

\section{Experimental Validation and Repeatability}
\label{sec:results_gates}

The validation measurements establish that the reported values represent a
settled response, that the two equivalent impedance formulations agree within
experimental repeatability, and that observed parameter effects exceed the
run-to-run variation of the nominal configuration.

\subsection{Equilibrium of the Set-Up Phase}
\label{sec:results_equilibrium}

The set-up phase was originally terminated by a \(4\,\mathrm{s}\) timeout. The
commanded press is a ramp whose duration is
\(|s_{\mathrm{end}}-s_{\mathrm{start}}|/v\approx4.0\,\mathrm{s}\), so the
phase was being cut at the instant the ramp completed. Repeating the phase at
\(4\), \(8\) and \(12\,\mathrm{s}\) gives the tip drift over the final fifth of
the phase as \(0.147^\circ\), \(0.002^\circ\) and \(0.000^\circ\)
respectively. The model-estimated normal load overshoots to approximately
\(65\,\mathrm{N}\) at \(t=4.0\,\mathrm{s}\) and relaxes to a steady
\(60\,\mathrm{N}\) by \(t\approx4.6\,\mathrm{s}\), after which tip angle,
estimated load and edge travel are
constant to the printed resolution for the remaining seven seconds.

The \(4\,\mathrm{s}\) configuration therefore sampled the overshoot of a
transient, not an equilibrium, and the quasi-static reading of the set-up
phase did not hold under it. Twelve seconds was used conservatively while the
campaign was recorded, but it is not required by the measured response.
Re-evaluating the existing \(8\)- and \(12\,\mathrm{s}\) traces at
\(t=5.0\,\mathrm{s}\) gives the remaining changes listed in
\cref{tab:results_five_second_check}.

\subsection{Equivalence of the Decoupled and Coupled Formulations}
\label{sec:results_g1}

Block-diagonal gains transformed through the coupled \(6\times6\) formulation
represent the same impedance as the decoupled formulation when the point shift
is zero. Five decoupled runs give a final tip angle of
\(2.458\pm0.111^\circ\) and a steady model-estimated normal load of
\(59.37\pm0.11\,\mathrm{N}\); three block-diagonal coupled runs give
\(2.537\pm0.232^\circ\) and \(59.02\pm0.65\,\mathrm{N}\). The two groups differ
by \(0.079^\circ\), which is \(0.34\) times the larger of the two standard
deviations. Alignment gain and edge travel differ by \(0.31\) and \(0.72\)
standard deviations respectively. No discrepancy exceeding the observed
repeatability was detected between the formulations under the tested
conditions.

\section{Measuring Alignment Against the Physical Plane}
\label{sec:results_alignment}

The alignment quality of interest is the residual angle between the physical
tool axis and the surface normal,
\(\theta_{\mathrm{align,phys}}
=\cos^{-1}\!\big(a_T^\top(-n_{\mathrm{phys}})\big)\). This is not
the logged set-up error \(\mathbf e_R\), which is referenced to the
orientation frozen at geometric clearance and therefore reports how far the
tool \emph{turned} rather than how flat it ended up. The physical tool-axis
angle is evaluated before and after the set-up phase.

One further correction is required before the logged values can be
interpreted, and it inverts the sign of the result if omitted. The logged angle
is evaluated against the \emph{configured} surface
normal, built from the two tilt angles in the parameter set. The configured
and physical normals were deliberately different to provide the initial
angular mismatch. The physical plane was estimated by hand-seating the tool
face and reading back the pose, giving
\(a=-0.72\pm0.31^\circ\) and \(b=14.99\pm1.91^\circ\) against a configured
\(a=0^\circ\), \(b=5^\circ\), a mismatch of \(10.01^\circ\). The tool is
commanded toward the configured normal, so it arrives at contact
\(10.01^\circ\) away from the surface it is actually pressing on.

The consequence is that the printed alignment gain reads backwards: it becomes
\emph{more negative} the closer the tool gets to the real plane, because it
measures distance from an assumption. All alignment results below are
therefore rescored against the measured plane, using the rotation
\(-\mathbf e_R\) --- the logged orientation error is defined as the rotation
carrying the current orientation onto the frozen reference, so the tool's
actual rotation is its negation.

The \emph{absolute}
residual depends on the measured plane, which three hand-seatings pin only to
about \(\pm1.9^\circ\). The \emph{improvement} --- how much closer to the
surface the tool came --- does not: across all three seatings it varies by
less than \(0.1^\circ\), because the tool rotates in essentially the same
plane as the mismatch. Improvements are therefore reported as measured values
and absolute residuals only as a range.

\section{Influence of Compliance-Centre Position}
\label{sec:results_pole}

The position study contains \(57\) runs. A configured-normal sweep comprises
\(24\) runs at eight offsets from \(-120\) to \(+160\,\mathrm{mm}\). Because
the configured normal differs by approximately \(10^\circ\) from the measured
physical-plane normal, this sweep also changes one physical in-plane component
from \(-5.0\) to \(+27.6\,\mathrm{mm}\). It therefore does not isolate the
physical normal coordinate.

The two in-plane coordinates were also varied independently. The first surface
tangent \(t_1\) was evaluated in \(18\) runs at six offsets from \(-71\) to
\(+161\,\mathrm{mm}\) while the normal offset was fixed. The second tangent
\(t_2\) was evaluated in \(12\) runs at four offsets.
\Cref{fig:results_pole_component} shows pronounced non-monotonic structure for
both in-plane coordinates and little independent structure for the physical
normal coordinate.

The response shape is relevant in addition to its fitted maximum. A linear fit
in \(t_1\) alone accounts for \(R^2=0.047\): beyond the fitted maximum the
response reverses. At \(t_1=+161\,\mathrm{mm}\) the improvement is
\(-3.75^\circ\), so a pole placed far beyond that point is worse than
commanding no coupling at all. The turnover is consistent with the quadratic
lever contribution \([r_c]_\times^\top K_p[r_c]_\times\) in
\cref{eq:coupled_tcp_stiffness}, which re-stiffens the rotational block as the
lever becomes large.

The monotonic trend in the configured-normal sweep is consistent with its
unintended tangential drift from \(-5.0\) to \(+27.6\,\mathrm{mm}\), which lies
on the rising part of the fitted \(t_1\) response. The configured-normal sweep
alone therefore cannot be interpreted as an isolated normal-coordinate
effect.

The model was checked against a configuration that was not included in the
fit. Three further runs
placed the pole on the contact edge, at
\((t_1,t_2)=(+5.9,-0.6)\,\mathrm{mm}\), a setting held out of the fit. The
additive quadratic model predicts an improvement of \(+2.44^\circ\) there; the
measured value is \(+2.30\pm0.07^\circ\). The \(0.14^\circ\) discrepancy is
below the \(0.59^\circ\) repeatability-based interpretation threshold. This
provides a held-out consistency check, but not broad validation of the response
outside the sampled cross.

The sign matters as much as the magnitude. At sufficiently negative offsets on
either tangent the coupling does not merely fail to assist: the tool rotates
away from the surface, by \(10.31^\circ\) at \(t_1=-70.9\,\mathrm{mm}\) and
\(6.54^\circ\) at \(t_2=-91.5\,\mathrm{mm}\), ending further from flat than it
arrived. The compliance centre can therefore actively fight the alignment it is
intended to produce. Across the full tested range the improvement spans
\(-10.31^\circ\) to \(+6.96^\circ\), a swing of \(17.3^\circ\) against a
repeatability-based threshold of approximately \(0.4^\circ\).

For the investigated geometry, beneficial alignment is associated with
positive offsets along both in-plane surface-frame coordinates. The physical
first-contact edge was not established independently, so these measurements do
not support a universal rule relating the pole sign to the contacting edge.
The equivalent contact location inferred from the model-estimated external
wrench lies near the middle of the face in \(t_1\), whereas the controller's
\(0.1\,\mathrm{mm}\) tie-breaking rule reports a corner in \(48\) of \(52\)
runs. Distributed surface contact and uncertainty in the selected feature
prevent treating either estimate as an exact physical point contact.

\section{Observed Dynamic Response}
\label{sec:results_damping}

No sustained oscillation was observed in the rotation-vector response with the
automatically calculated damping used in the reported runs. Because damping
was not varied independently, the measurements do not establish a causal
damping effect or an experimentally identified modal damping ratio.

\section{Null-Space Conditioning: Evidence Boundary}
\label{sec:results_nullspace}

The controller implements a two-point smallest-singular-value conditioning
bias. It compares \(\sigma_{\min}(q+\alpha v_7)\) and
\(\sigma_{\min}(q-\alpha v_7)\), selects the better sampled sign, and projects
the resulting torque through the torque-level null-space projector. The SVD
inverse uses \(1/\sigma_i\) only for singular values retained by the configured
relative cutoff.

The available measurements contain one usable mode-0 hold and no controlled
conditioning-torque sweep. They therefore do not establish that the active
bias improves \(\sigma_{\min}\), preserves the Cartesian task within a stated
tolerance, or identifies a preferred \(k_\sigma\), \(\alpha\), or SVD cutoff.
Experimental validation of the null-space strategy remains future work.

\section{Discussion}
\label{sec:discussion_contact}

\subsection{Geometric Interpretation of the Compliance Centre}

The measurements show that the spatial location of the compliance centre is
relevant in addition to the stiffness magnitude. A block-diagonal stiffness
does not encode a remote centre, whereas the point shift creates structured
translation--rotation coupling. Within the investigated configuration, this
coupling is most sensitive to the two in-plane coordinates.

This is consistent with the assembly literature, where remote and variable
compliance centres are useful precisely because they turn contact forces into
correcting motion instead of jamming
\citep{Nevins1989ComputerControlledAssembly,Watson1978RCC,Wang2019VariableComplianceCenter,Hartisch2022FlexureRCC}.
For the investigated geometry, suitable in-plane placements increase alignment
and unsuitable placements rotate the tool away from the desired orientation.

\subsection{Relative Influence of In-Plane and Normal Coordinates}
\label{sec:discussion_pole_height}

Within the investigated configuration and \(277\,\mathrm{mm}\) range,
inclusion of the physical normal coordinate provides little additional
explanatory power: \(R^2\) increases from \(0.962\) to \(0.966\). The two
in-plane coordinates account for the dominant structure and carry a
\(17.3^\circ\) response range.

The tool arrives tilted relative to the physical plane, so contact begins on
one edge. The alignment moment about the pole is
\[
  M = r_c \times f ,
\]
with \(r_c\) the lever from the pole to the contact and \(f\) the equivalent
contact force. Inferred from the model-estimated external wrench across all
\(57\) position-study runs, its direction lies only \(10.1^\circ\) from the
surface normal --- \(56.0\,\mathrm{N}\) normal compared with
\(10.0\,\mathrm{N}\) tangential, a ratio of \(5.6:1\). A component of \(r_c\)
along the surface normal is therefore very nearly parallel to \(f\), and
\(r\parallel f\) contributes nothing to the cross product. Raising or lowering
the compliance centre relative to the tool face slides it along the line of
action of the contact force, which changes no moment. Only displacing it
\emph{within} the surface plane creates a lever the normal contact force can
act on.

The measured asymmetry is stronger than the lever-direction argument alone
predicts. A pure lever account gives an effectiveness ratio of
\(|f_n|/|f_t|\approx5.6\) between in-plane and normal offsets, whereas the
fitted slopes differ by about a factor of \(25\). The difference may also
reflect the stiffness construction: the coupling terms generated by a normal
pole offset act on tangential displacement, which during set-up is only a few
millimetres compared with roughly \(43\,\mathrm{mm}\) of normal press.

For this configuration, the in-plane coordinates should be treated as the
primary placement variables. The additive quadratic model predicts a local
maximum near \(t_1=+62\,\mathrm{mm}\) and \(t_2=+30\,\mathrm{mm}\), but that
combination requires direct experimental confirmation. The measured turnover
is consistent with the quadratic lever term increasing the effective
rotational stiffness at large offsets.

\subsection{Alignment Beyond the Set-Up Phase}

The residuals of \cref{sec:results_pole} are upper bounds on the final
misalignment rather than final values. Continuing into the grinding phase, the
preload established during set-up produces further alignment: between the end
of the set-up phase and the end of the grinding phase the residual falls by a
further \(0.18^\circ\) to \(0.64^\circ\), and it falls most where the set-up
phase achieved least. The grinding phase duration was operator-terminated and
therefore uncontrolled, so this is reported as a direction rather than a
magnitude.

\subsection{Practical Implications for Controller Tuning}

The rotational stiffness should be selected from the required angular
compliance and allowable displacement. Across the tested values, the mean
model-estimated normal load remains within \(0.50\,\mathrm{N}\) while the mean
alignment response changes by \(1.59^\circ\). Compliance-centre tuning should
focus first on the two in-plane coordinates, where both the response magnitude
and its sign vary substantially.

\section{Limitations}
\label{sec:results_limitations}

The physical plane was measured by hand-seating the tool face and
reading back the pose three times, giving \(\pm1.9^\circ\) on the tilt. Part of
that spread is seating technique and part is movement of the workpiece.
Absolute residual angles inherit this uncertainty; the reported improvements
vary by less than \(0.1^\circ\) across all three seatings.

The workpiece fixture is compliant enough to move under load, and the tool
came loose in the gripper twice during the campaign, in both cases detected
and the affected runs re-recorded. Neither was instrumented. Run-to-run
variation from the fixture is contained in the repeatability floor of
\cref{tab:results_noise_floor}; a systematic component would not be, and would
bias absolute values without affecting comparisons between settings, which are
what this chapter claims.

Both in-plane optima are interior to the tested ranges and are located by a
quadratic fit through a cross-shaped sample rather than by a full local grid;
the nearest tested settings are \(+62.9\) and \(+39.4\,\mathrm{mm}\). Which
edge of the tool face makes first contact was inferred from the tool model
rather than deliberately controlled, which leaves the mapping from the
measured relationship onto a general placement rule open.

Finally, the starting tool axis in the rescoring of
\cref{sec:results_alignment} is taken to be the configured normal. The
measured pre-contact alignment lies between \(0.41^\circ\) and \(0.49^\circ\)
across the campaign, so this assumption is good to approximately half a
degree.
