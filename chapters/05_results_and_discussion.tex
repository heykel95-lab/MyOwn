\chapter{Results and Discussion}
\label{ch:results_discussion}

This chapter reports a measured campaign of \(71\) runs, recorded with the
controller tagged \texttt{FULL-\hspace{0pt}Working-\hspace{0pt}Clean}. Every number below is
derived from an archived run rather than from an expectation. The historical
\texttt{Difference-Chasles-Pole} campaign, which motivated the study, is
retained in \cref{sec:coupled_command_experiment} as the prior result that the
new measurements were designed to test --- and, as reported there, do not
reproduce in the form originally claimed.

\section{Measurement Campaign and Provenance}
\label{sec:results_provenance}

Each run applies a parameter overlay to the nominal configuration, starts the
controller, and archives the raw logs together with the effective parameter
set, the git commit and the complete terminal transcript. The parameter
directory is restored afterwards from a shell trap, because the controller
rewrites the auto-computed coupled matrices into its own configuration at the
end of a set-up phase; without the restore each run would inherit its
predecessor's state and a sweep would measure its own history.

Metrics are extracted twice --- once from the CSV and once from the
controller's printed set-up report --- and a disagreement between the two
flags the run rather than averaging it in silently. Flags separate into
\emph{data} faults, where the numbers themselves are untrustworthy, and
\emph{provenance} caveats, where the repository merely held an uncommitted
change when the run was recorded. Only the former are excluded from the
reported means; the latter remain admissible because each run archives the
exact configuration it used.

\section{Gate Experiments}
\label{sec:results_gates}

Three gates were run before any parameter study, on the principle that a
sweep is only interpretable once the quantity being swept is known to be an
equilibrium, the command path is known to be correct, and the noise floor is
known.

\subsection{Equilibrium of the Set-Up Phase}
\label{sec:results_equilibrium}

The set-up phase was originally terminated by a \(4\,\mathrm{s}\) timeout. The
commanded press is a ramp whose duration is
\(|s_{\mathrm{end}}-s_{\mathrm{start}}|/v\approx4.0\,\mathrm{s}\), so the
phase was being cut at the instant the ramp completed. Repeating the phase at
\(4\), \(8\) and \(12\,\mathrm{s}\) gives the tip drift over the final fifth of
the phase as \(0.147^\circ\), \(0.002^\circ\) and \(0.000^\circ\)
respectively. The contact force overshoots to approximately \(65\,\mathrm{N}\)
at \(t=4.0\,\mathrm{s}\) and relaxes to a steady \(60\,\mathrm{N}\) by
\(t\approx4.6\,\mathrm{s}\), after which tip angle, force and edge travel are
constant to the printed resolution for the remaining seven seconds.

The \(4\,\mathrm{s}\) configuration therefore sampled the overshoot of a
transient, not an equilibrium, and the quasi-static reading of the set-up
phase did not hold under it. The timeout was raised to \(12\,\mathrm{s}\) for
the entire campaign, which is margin rather than necessity: equilibrium is
reached by \(t\approx4.6\,\mathrm{s}\).

\subsection{Correctness of the Coupled Command Path}
\label{sec:results_g1}

Feeding block-diagonal gains through the coupled \(6\times6\) path must
reproduce the decoupled wrench exactly; if it does not, every coupled result
is an implementation artefact. Five decoupled runs give a final tip angle of
\(2.458\pm0.111^\circ\) and a steady contact force of
\(59.37\pm0.11\,\mathrm{N}\); three block-diagonal coupled runs give
\(2.537\pm0.232^\circ\) and \(59.02\pm0.65\,\mathrm{N}\). The two groups differ
by \(0.079^\circ\), which is \(0.34\) times the larger of the two standard
deviations. Alignment gain and edge travel differ by \(0.31\) and \(0.72\)
standard deviations respectively. The coupled path is therefore correct, and
the pole effects reported in \cref{sec:results_pole} are properties of the
coupling rather than of its implementation.

\section{Measuring Alignment Against the Physical Plane}
\label{sec:results_alignment}

The alignment quality of interest is the residual angle between the physical
tool axis and the surface normal,
\(\theta_{\mathrm{align}}=\cos^{-1}\!\big(a_T^\top(-n_s)\big)\). This is not
the logged set-up error \(\mathbf e_R\), which is referenced to the
orientation frozen at geometric clearance and therefore reports how far the
tool \emph{turned} rather than how flat it ended up. The controller now logs
\(\theta_{\mathrm{align}}\) directly and prints it before and after the set-up
phase, which removes the instrumentation gap that made earlier versions of
this chapter qualitative.

One further correction is required before the logged values can be
interpreted, and it inverts the sign of the result if omitted. The controller
scores \(\theta_{\mathrm{align}}\) against the \emph{configured} surface
normal, built from the two tilt angles in the parameter set. That
configuration was deliberately left uncalibrated: the physical plane was
measured, with the tool face hand-seated flat and the pose read back, at
\(a=-0.72\pm0.31^\circ\) and \(b=14.99\pm1.91^\circ\) against a configured
\(a=0^\circ\), \(b=5^\circ\), a mismatch of \(10.01^\circ\). The tool is
commanded toward the configured normal, so it arrives at contact
\(10.01^\circ\) away from the surface it is actually pressing on.

The consequence is that the printed alignment gain reads backwards: it becomes
\emph{more negative} the closer the tool gets to the real plane, because it
measures distance from an assumption. All alignment results below are
therefore rescored against the measured plane, using the rotation
\(-\mathbf e_R\) --- the logged orientation error is defined as the rotation
carrying the current orientation onto the frozen reference, so the tool's
actual rotation is its negation.

Two properties of this rescoring should be stated. The \emph{absolute}
residual depends on the measured plane, which three hand-seatings pin only to
about \(\pm1.9^\circ\). The \emph{improvement} --- how much closer to the
surface the tool came --- does not: across all three seatings it varies by
less than \(0.1^\circ\), because the tool rotates in essentially the same
plane as the mismatch. Improvements are therefore reported as measured values
and absolute residuals only as a range.

The response is monotonic and well outside the noise floor: softening the

trade-off assumed in earlier planning: the rotational stiffness does
\emph{not} buy alignment at the cost of contact load. It simply costs
alignment. The selection criterion is consequently not a compromise between
alignment and force but a bound on displacement alone.

\section{Where the Compliance Centre Must Lie}
\label{sec:results_pole}

The pole of the coupled impedance was then swept in two directions: along the
surface normal (\(24\) runs, eight offsets from \(-120\) to
\(+160\,\mathrm{mm}\)) and along the surface tangent (\(12\) runs, four
offsets at a fixed normal offset of approximately \(100\,\mathrm{mm}\)). The
tangential sweep exists because the normal sweep cannot separate the two
components: the configured normal carries a tangential component from the
surface tilt, so commanding a pole along the normal also displaces it
tangentially.

\cref{fig:results_pole_component} plots the alignment improvement of all
\(36\) runs against each pole component in turn.

\begin{figure}[H]
    \centering
    \includegraphics[width=\textwidth]{B_pole_component.pdf}
    \caption{Alignment gained toward the measured plane, for the same runs,
    against the tangential (left) and normal (right) components of the
    commanded pole. The tangential component accounts for the outcome; the
    normal component does not. The twelve tangential-sweep runs share a normal
    offset of about \(100\,\mathrm{mm}\) and span \(-11^\circ\) to
    \(+7^\circ\), which is visible as the vertical spread in the right panel.}
    \label{fig:results_pole_component}
\end{figure}

A linear fit over all \(36\) runs gives \(R^2=0.907\) against the tangential
component, with a slope of \(+0.125^\circ\) per millimetre of pole offset, and
\(R^2=0.005\) against the normal component. The tangential position of the
compliance centre governs the alignment response; the normal position, over a
range of \(277\,\mathrm{mm}\), does not measurably affect it.

This reverses the reading of the normal sweep taken in isolation. Along the
normal alone the improvement rises from \(+1.98^\circ\) at a pole on the
contact edge to \(+3.47^\circ\) at \(+160\,\mathrm{mm}\), which invites the
conclusion that the normal offset is the design variable. It is not: across
that sweep the tangential component drifts from \(-5.0\) to
\(+27.6\,\mathrm{mm}\) as a geometric side effect of the surface tilt, and
that drift accounts for the trend. The tangential sweep, which holds the
normal offset fixed, spans \(-10.31^\circ\) at a tangential offset of
\(-72.7\,\mathrm{mm}\) to \(+6.89^\circ\) at \(+46.8\,\mathrm{mm}\) --- a range
of \(17.2^\circ\) against a noise floor of \(0.4^\circ\), and an order of
magnitude larger than anything the normal sweep produced.

The sign matters as much as the magnitude. At sufficiently negative tangential
offsets the coupling does not merely fail to assist: the tool rotates
\(10^\circ\) \emph{away} from the surface, ending further from flat than it
arrived. The compliance centre can therefore actively fight the alignment it
is intended to produce, and the boundary between assisting and fighting is
crossed within the working range of the tool.

Two qualifications apply. The tangential sweep had not reached an optimum at
its positive extreme, so \(+46.8\,\mathrm{mm}\) is the best measured setting
rather than the best attainable one. And the mapping from this result onto a
placement rule of the form ``the compliance centre belongs opposite the
contact'' requires knowing which edge of the tool face touches first; the
controller's contact selection consistently reports one corner, in \(48\) of
\(52\) runs, but this is a geometric prediction from the tool model and was
not confirmed by direct observation.

\section{Effect of Damping}
\label{sec:results_damping}

The damping coefficients are generated at entry to each phase group. The
controller symmetrizes \(JM_q^{-1}J^\top\), adds \(10^{-9}I_6\), inverts the
result, and applies \(d_i=2\zeta\sqrt{\Lambda_{ii}k_i}\) per task coordinate.
The regularization is a numerical device for the mixed-unit Cartesian matrix,
not the SVD cutoff used by the null-space projector. No oscillation was
observed in the rotation-vector response of any run in the campaign;
\(\zeta\) remains a tuning factor rather than a proof of a coupled-system
modal damping ratio.

That campaign read the offsets as displacements along the surface normal, and
concluded that a pole on the contact edge suppresses the tip, that a pole on
the far side assists it, and that the assist has an optimum near
\(+0.08\,\mathrm{m}\) beyond which the quadratic term
\([r_c]_\times^\top K_p[r_c]_\times\) re-stiffens the rotation.

The measured campaign of \cref{sec:results_pole} does not reproduce that
structure. Swept along the normal with the tangential component held fixed,
the alignment response is flat to within the noise floor
(\(R^2=0.005\)). What the historical runs varied, without isolating it, was
the tangential component, and against that variable the response is strong,
monotonic and free of the reported optimum within the tested range. The
qualitative claim that the pole position decides whether the coupling resists
or assists alignment is confirmed; the specific attribution to the normal
offset, and the optimum at \(+0.08\,\mathrm{m}\), are not.

The consistency check reported for that campaign --- that block-diagonal gains
through the coupled path reproduced the decoupled baseline --- is independently
confirmed here at \(0.34\sigma\) with a measured repeatability floor
(\cref{sec:results_g1}).

\section{Discussion}
\label{sec:discussion_contact}

\subsection{Where the Compliance Appears to Be Located}

The design question for a surface-contact task is not whether the robot is
soft or stiff, but where the compliance appears to be located geometrically.
The measurements support that framing and sharpen it: of the two geometric
degrees of freedom available when placing the compliance centre relative to
the contact, only the tangential one is load-bearing. A purely diagonal
stiffness matrix makes the robot compliant without encoding any particular
centre of compliance, and the resulting alignment is whatever the contact
geometry happens to produce.

This is consistent with the assembly literature, where remote and variable
compliance centres are useful precisely because they turn contact forces into
correcting motion instead of jamming
\citep{Nevins1989ComputerControlledAssembly,Watson1978RCC,Wang2019VariableComplianceCenter,Hartisch2022FlexureRCC}.
The present result adds that the useful placement is a tangential one, and
that the same mechanism run in reverse is capable of driving the tool away
from the surface.

\subsection{Alignment Beyond the Set-Up Phase}

The residuals of \cref{sec:results_pole} are upper bounds on the final
misalignment rather than final values. Continuing into the grinding phase, the
preload established during set-up produces further alignment: between the end
of the set-up phase and the end of the grinding phase the residual falls by a
further \(0.18^\circ\) to \(0.64^\circ\), and it falls most where the set-up
phase achieved least. The grinding phase duration was operator-terminated and
therefore uncontrolled, so this is reported as a direction rather than a
magnitude.

\subsection{Practical Implications for Controller Tuning}

Two of the measured results bear directly on tuning. First, the tangential
rotational stiffness trades alignment against nothing --- the contact force is
unchanged across a tenfold change in stiffness --- so it should be set as soft
as the displacement bound permits. Second, the tangential pole offset is worth
an order of magnitude more alignment than the normal offset and carries a sign
change, so it is the parameter that requires care and the one whose
mis-setting is capable of doing harm.

\section{Limitations}
\label{sec:results_limitations}

The physical plane was measured by hand-seating the tool face and reading back
the pose, three times, giving \(\pm1.9^\circ\) on the tilt. Part of that spread
is seating technique and part is the workpiece itself moving between attempts.
Absolute residual angles inherit this uncertainty; the reported improvements do
not, varying by less than \(0.1^\circ\) across all three seatings.

The workpiece fixture is compliant enough to move under load, and the tool
came loose in the gripper twice during the campaign, in both cases detected
and the affected runs re-recorded. Neither was instrumented. Run-to-run
variation from the fixture is contained in the repeatability floor of
\cref{tab:results_noise_floor}; a systematic component would not be, and would
bias absolute values without affecting comparisons between settings, which are
what this chapter claims.

The tangential pole sweep did not reach an optimum within its tested range,
and its best setting was measured with two repetitions rather than the five
used elsewhere. Which edge of the tool face makes first contact was inferred
from the tool model rather than observed, which leaves the mapping from the
measured relationship onto a general placement rule open.

Finally, the starting tool axis in the rescoring of
\cref{sec:results_alignment} is taken to be the configured normal. The
measured pre-contact alignment lies between \(0.41^\circ\) and \(0.49^\circ\)
across the campaign, so this assumption is good to approximately half a
degree.
