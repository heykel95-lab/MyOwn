\section{Overview of Tested Gain Settings}
\label{sec:results_contact}

This section preserves the \emph{historical planned} five-case surface-contact
study. Its \(10^\circ\) starting misalignment predates the current approach
gate, which requires the tool-axis error to be no greater than
\(0.035\,\mathrm{rad}\) (about \(2^\circ\)) before descent. The values below are
\emph{illustrative expectations} derived from the contact geometry: they show
the motion trends anticipated for each setting.
They are replaced only by provenance-controlled orientation, tool-feature, and
TCP-displacement signals. The figures are \emph{qualitative} sketches of the
corresponding expected motion, not plots of measured data.

Two controller versions must be kept separate in this chapter.  The measured
coupled-pole campaign in
\cref{sec:coupled_command_experiment} was produced with the
historical controller tag \texttt{Difference-Chasles-Pole}
(commit \texttt{491822e}).  The implementation documented in
Chapters~\ref{ch:implementation} and~\ref{ch:methodology} is the later tag
\texttt{FULL-Working-Clean} (commit \texttt{bbe7e85}).  The later controller
uses a different phase supervisor, tool geometry, damping computation, timing,
and a three-component pole offset.  Consequently, the historical measurements
support the pole/adjoint mechanism but are not presented as validation of every
parameter or transition in the current controller.

\section{Orientation Alignment During Contact}
\label{sec:results_alignment}

The decisive variable is the rotational stiffness about the alignment axes.
With high rotational stiffness (Test~1), the controller holds the commanded
orientation and resists rotation during contact, so the contact moment is
largely reacted as joint torque and the tool barely rotates. The residual
misalignment stays close to the initial value,
\(\Delta\theta_{\mathrm{align}}\approx0\), and the
leading edge produces little corrective rotation. As the rotational stiffness
is reduced (Tests~2 and~3), the commanded press and the opposing
environment-on-tool reaction form an action--reaction pair; the reaction at
the offset leading edge increasingly rotates the tool toward the surface, so
\(\Delta\theta_{\mathrm{align}}\) becomes positive and the physical tool-axis
alignment angle decreases.
Adding lateral compliance (Test~4) lets the tool both slide and rotate, which
is expected to give the most complete alignment response during contact. With an over-soft
setting (Test~5), the displacement becomes large and the alignment motion
poorly defined, so this setting is expected to be rejected on the safety and
displacement criteria.

These quantities refer to
\(\theta_{\mathrm{align}}=\cos^{-1}(a_T^\top(-n_s))\), not to the logged
set-up controller error \(\mathbf e_R\). The latter is relative to the
orientation frozen at geometric clearance and can grow during a favorable
alignment motion. The current general CSV therefore needs an added orientation
or tool-axis signal before this physical angle can be evaluated.

\section{Effect of Damping}
\label{sec:results_damping}

The damping coefficients used by the controller are generated at entry to each
phase group. It symmetrizes \(JM_q^{-1}J^\top\), adds \(10^{-9}I_6\), inverts
the result, and applies \(d_i=2\zeta\sqrt{\Lambda_{ii}k_i}\) per task
coordinate. The regularization is a numerical device for the mixed-unit
Cartesian matrix, not the SVD cutoff used by the null-space projector.
Oscillation in the rotation-vector response would indicate insufficient
directional damping for that run; \(\zeta\) is a tuning factor rather than a
proof of a coupled-system modal damping ratio.

\section{Discussion}
\label{sec:discussion_contact}

\subsection{Comparison with Expected Contact-Alignment Behaviour}

The expected trade-off is the same physical trade-off that appears throughout
Cartesian impedance control. Higher stiffness improves pose regulation but
resists motion during contact. Lower stiffness improves compliance but can
allow excessive displacement and may make the alignment motion poorly defined.
For the surface-contact task, the important design question is not simply
whether the robot is soft or stiff. The important question is where the
compliance appears to be located geometrically.

\subsection{Practical Implications for Controller Tuning}

A purely diagonal stiffness matrix can make the robot compliant, but it does
not directly encode a selected local linearized center of compliance. In that case, the
contact wrench may still produce useful alignment because the contact force
acts at the tool edge and because the surface constrains the motion. However,
the result must be evaluated from physical tool-axis alignment and
feature/TCP displacement rather than inferred from the commanded stiffness
matrix alone. This interpretation is consistent with the assembly
literature, where remote or variable compliance centers are useful precisely
because they turn contact forces into correcting motion instead of jamming
\citep{Nevins1989ComputerControlledAssembly,Watson1978RCC,Wang2019VariableComplianceCenter,Hartisch2022FlexureRCC}.

\subsection{Limitations of the Current Qualitative Results}

The numerical values in the planned-study tables remain qualitative
expectations. A local current-schema CSV exists in the controller working tree,
but it has not been archived with a run manifest that binds it to a committed
configuration and it lacks the orientation needed for
\(\theta_{\mathrm{align}}\). It is therefore not silently promoted to final
evidence here. Final validation requires provenance-controlled repetitions,
the physical tool-axis signal, and selected-feature/TCP displacement.

\subsection{Practical Implications of Coupled Gain Design}

The current set-up phase implements this construction directly.  A
block-diagonal pole-frame impedance is shifted to the TCP by the adjoint
congruence of Section~\ref{sec:coupled_pole_stiffness}, producing the
translation--rotation blocks commanded by the controller. The selected
impedance should reduce the relevant surface-alignment angle, avoid excessive
contact loads, remain adequately damped, and respect both the configured Panda
collision/reflex thresholds and the discrete real-time timing budget. These
thresholds are not a software torque clamp.

\section{Decoupled versus Pole-Coupled Impedance}
\label{sec:coupled_command_experiment}

This section reports the historical \texttt{Difference-Chasles-Pole} campaign,
in which the coupled gain design was implemented and tested directly using the
pole-shifted stiffness of \cref{eq:coupled_tcp_stiffness}. During that
controller's post-contact alignment phase it could command either the
decoupled wrench of
\cref{eq:cartesian_force_moment_vector} or the coupled wrench
\(w = K_{\mathrm{TCP}}\,e + D_{\mathrm{TCP}}\,\dot{e}\), with the pole placed at a
chosen offset from the contact edge along the surface-normal direction. All runs
start from the same fixed joint configuration and use the same downward contact
search and the same \(7\,\mathrm{s}\) alignment phase, so that only the command
and the pole differ. They are compared in
\cref{tab:coupled_command_campaign} by the final tip angle (the rotation away
from the orientation held at first contact) and the travel of the contact edge.

As a consistency check, feeding the decoupled gains through the coupled command
path with the pole at the TCP reproduced the decoupled baseline
(\(8.6^\circ\) tip), which confirms that the differences observed for other
poles are a genuine effect of the coupling and not an implementation artefact.

The runs can be read as follows. The decoupled baseline tipped the tool by
\(9.0^\circ\). Placing the pole on the contact edge almost completely suppressed
the tip (\(0.6^\circ\)): the off-diagonal block
\([r_c]_\times^\top K_p\) converts the normal press into an orientation-holding
moment, so the tool stays rigid about the edge and only slides tangentially.
Moving the pole to the opposite side of the TCP reverses the sign of the
coupling, so that it now assists the tip; the tool then reached
\(11.2^\circ\), exceeding the baseline while producing less edge travel.
Increasing the offset
further reduced the tip
again (\(9.7^\circ\)), because the quadratic term
\([r_c]_\times^\top K_p\,[r_c]_\times\) in \cref{eq:coupled_tcp_stiffness} grows
with the square of the lever and re-stiffens the rotation; the assist therefore
has a clear optimum. Increasing the translational push did not increase the
tip. Recomputing the pole from the moving edge each cycle gave essentially the
same equilibrium without an observed oscillation or reflex stop, so a constant
pole pinned at first contact is preferred for its predictability.

These results confirm the central distinction between the two impedance
structures. A
coupled, pole-based wrench is not equivalent to the decoupled wrench unless the
pole lies at the TCP, and the pole position alone decides whether the coupling
resists or assists the alignment response during contact. With an assisting pole on the far
side of the contact edge, the coupled command improves the alignment over the
decoupled baseline: a larger tip and less edge sliding. All single-pole variants
nevertheless settle at an equilibrium short of full flatness, because the
contact moment that drives the rotation decays as the tool tips and the edge
stops digging in. Neither increased push nor live pole tracking removed this
limit in the historical trials. Reaching full flatness would require a
different active reference strategy, such as a time-varying pole or a commanded
rotation, which was not part of that campaign.

The current controller does not reproduce this campaign by merely loading the
same scalar offset. It activates the coupled law only in \texttt{set\_up} and
defines
\[
  p_c=p_{\Pi,\mathrm{clr}}+
  [-0.04,\ 0.08,\ 0]^\top\,\mathrm{m}
\]
in base coordinates, where \(p_{\Pi,\mathrm{clr}}\) is the selected feature
projected onto the plane at the \(20\,\mathrm{mm}\) geometric clearance. The
source names this reference \texttt{first\_contact\_point}, but it is neither a
live edge nor a force-detected contact point.
New measurements with \texttt{FULL-Working-Clean} are therefore required before
the table above can be attributed to the current state machine or parameter
set.
