\chapter{Results and Discussion}
\label{ch:results_discussion}

The measurement set contains \(75\) completed contact runs forming experimental
Cases A--E as defined in \cref{tab:experimental_test_cases}. Every run reached
and completed its set-up phase, and none is excluded from the quantitative
analysis. One run is retained together with a recorded operator observation and
is interpreted in \cref{sec:results_limitations}, because its behaviour
illustrates a limitation of the measurement chain rather than a recording
fault.

Case A establishes the alignment response to the commanded tilt and
provides the baseline against which the remaining cases are read. Cases B and C
vary the rotational and translational stiffness of one axis at a time, Case D
varies the virtual centre-of-compliance lever, and Case E repeats the Case-A
tilt about the axes of the tool face. This case notation is retained
throughout the chapter so that every result can be traced to its experimental
design.

\section{Measurement Campaign and Data Integrity}
\label{sec:results_provenance}

Each run uses one parameter set and records the raw signals together with that
configuration. Metrics are calculated independently from the general data log
and from the controller's set-up summary. A disagreement flags the record for
inspection rather than being averaged silently.

Repeatability is assessed from the three repetitions of each of the \(25\)
settings. The median within-setting standard deviation of the alignment
improvement is \(0.02^\circ\) for settings tilted about \(t_1\) and
\(0.03^\circ\) for those tilted about \(t_2\). Differences smaller than
approximately \(0.1^\circ\) are therefore not interpreted as parameter effects.

The reported values represent a settled response. Over the final second of the
set-up interval the model-estimated load changed by a median of
\(0.10\,\mathrm{N}\) and the tip angle by a median of \(0.00^\circ\), with
worst cases of \(1.10\,\mathrm{N}\) and \(0.60^\circ\) across all \(75\) runs.
The steady model-estimated normal load lies between \(44.5\) and
\(49.4\,\mathrm{N}\) in every setting that retains the baseline lever, so the
preload does not confound comparisons between those settings.

\section{EE-Inferred Alignment Against the Physical Plane}
\label{sec:results_alignment}

The primary response is the change in the angle between the EE-inferred tool
axis and the calibrated physical plane over the set-up interval, with a
positive value denoting improvement. Because the mounted tool can rotate by
approximately \(2^\circ\) about \(y_{EE}\) relative to the end-effector, this
quantity describes the combined robot--gripper--tool response and does not
separate controller compliance from gripper compliance.

The commanded and measured tilts differ systematically. The approach phase
hands over to the descent when the EE-inferred tool axis lies within
\(2^\circ\) of the commanded direction, and the residual at hand-off grows with
the commanded angle. A commanded \(5^\circ\) about \(t_1\) produced a measured
first-contact angle of \(2.45^\circ\), and a commanded \(10^\circ\) about
\(t_2\) produced \(8.45^\circ\). All comparisons below use the measured
first-contact angle rather than the commanded one.

\section{Case A: Commanded Tilt}
\label{sec:results_summary_table}

tilt the set-up press increased the EE-inferred misalignment by
\(0.97^\circ\), from \(1.07^\circ\) to \(2.04^\circ\). This value bounds the
alignment accuracy attainable with the present fixture and calibration: an
improvement smaller than approximately \(1^\circ\) cannot be attributed to the
commanded condition alone.

Two features are consistent across the case. The improvement is larger for

The \(10^\circ\) \(t_2\) setting has a standard deviation of \(0.92^\circ\), an
order of magnitude above every other setting. One of its three repetitions
removed \(0.00^\circ\) while the other two removed \(1.56^\circ\) and
\(1.62^\circ\) under an identical configuration. This repetition is retained
and is interpreted in \cref{sec:results_limitations}.

For a tilt about \(t_1\) the improvement decreases monotonically as

varied separately, this is an axis-specific result rather than a property of a
common paired setting.

\section{Case D: Virtual Centre of Compliance}
\label{sec:results_pole}

Case D varies only the compliance-centre lever
\(r_c=p_{\mathrm{TCP}}-p_c\), with all gains at their baseline values. The
lever is commanded along the surface tangent perpendicular to the tilted one,
so a a tilt about \(t_1\) is paired with \(r_{c,t_2}\) and a a tilt about \(t_2\)
with \(r_{c,t_1}\). The face-centre column places the compliance centre
\(20\,\mathrm{mm}\) along the tool axis from the end-effector origin, off the
tangent plane in which the sweep lies.

The lever placement influenced alignment more than any other parameter
examined. With the lever on the favourable side the residual misalignment fell
from \(6.99^\circ\) to \(0.93^\circ\) for a tilt about \(t_1\) and from
\(8.50^\circ\) to \(2.14^\circ\) for a tilt about \(t_2\), corresponding to
improvements of \(6.05^\circ\) and \(6.36^\circ\). These are approximately ten
and five times the improvements obtained at the same gains with a zero lever.

The effect is strongly sign-dependent, and the favourable sign is opposite for
the two axes. A a tilt about \(t_1\) requires \(r_{c,t_2}=-60\,\mathrm{mm}\),
whereas a a tilt about \(t_2\) requires \(r_{c,t_1}=+60\,\mathrm{mm}\). The
unfavourable sign does not merely reduce the improvement but removes it: the
a tilt about \(t_2\) with \(r_{c,t_1}=-60\,\mathrm{mm}\) improved alignment by
\(0.02\pm0.06^\circ\), which is indistinguishable from no correction.

The favourable placements also reduced the steady model-estimated normal load,
to \(36.5\,\mathrm{N}\) and \(39.2\,\mathrm{N}\) against approximately \(45\)
to \(47\,\mathrm{N}\) at zero lever, although the commanded virtual press was
unchanged.

The face-centre condition gave \(0.59^\circ\) and \(1.35^\circ\), against
\(0.56^\circ\) and \(1.38^\circ\) for the corresponding zero-lever runs. These
differences lie below the interpretation threshold. Within the investigated
configuration and parameter range, displacing the compliance centre along the
surface normal by this amount produced no detectable change, while a tangential
displacement of \(60\,\mathrm{mm}\) changed the response by several degrees.

Both tool-axis tilts lie between the two tangent-axis values, and they
differ from each other by \(0.22^\circ\) against the \(0.42^\circ\) separating
the tangent-axis pair. Resolving each tool-axis command onto the surface
tangents and combining the corresponding Case-A improvements linearly predicts
\(1.25^\circ\) for \(x_{EE}\) and \(1.17^\circ\) for \(y_{EE}\), against the
measured \(1.29^\circ\) and \(1.07^\circ\).

The measured asymmetry is therefore associated with the surface frame rather
than with the geometry of the grinding face. The response to a mixed command is
approximately the linear combination of its tangent components over this range.
This is a model-based interpretation supported by four settings; superposition
is not established outside the tested range.

\section{Null-Space Validation Status}
\label{sec:results_nullspace}

The null-space damping term was active in every reported run, and no sustained
oscillation was observed with the selected damping. The active singular-value
bias was not enabled and no matched conditioning comparison was recorded, so no
experimental claim is made about improved singularity conditioning.

\section{Discussion}
\label{sec:discussion_contact}

\subsection{Relative Influence of the Investigated Parameters}
\label{sec:discussion_pole_height}

Over the tested ranges the compliance-centre lever influenced alignment far
more than either stiffness entry. Varying the rotational stiffness of the
tilted axis by a factor of ten changed the improvement by at most
\(0.44^\circ\), and varying the perpendicular translational stiffness by a
factor of approximately seven changed it by at most \(0.73^\circ\). Moving the
lever by \(60\,\mathrm{mm}\) changed it by up to \(5.5^\circ\) and, with the
unfavourable sign, removed the correction.

For controller tuning this ordering indicates that the placement of the virtual
centre of compliance should be selected before the stiffness entries are
refined, and that its sign must be chosen with respect to the tilted axis.
Within the investigated configuration and parameter range, the normal
compliance-centre coordinate provided little additional explanatory power after
accounting for the in-plane placement.

\subsection{Geometric Interpretation of the Lever}

The sign dependence is consistent with the point-shifted formulation. Shifting
the compliance centre along the tangent perpendicular to the tilted axis
converts part of the commanded normal penetration into a moment about the
tilted axis. The direction of that moment follows the sign of the lever, so
one sign reinforces the rotation that brings the face towards the surface while
the opposite sign opposes it. The reversal between the \(t_1\) and \(t_2\)
tilts follows from the corresponding reversal of the cross product
relating the lever to the tilted axis.

The same mechanism accounts for the reduced normal load at the favourable
placements. A tool that rotates further into conformity relieves part of the
commanded penetration, so the model-estimated load falls although the commanded
press is unchanged.

\subsection{Practical Implications for Controller Tuning}

The measured ordering suggests a tuning sequence. The compliance-centre lever
is selected first, on the tangent perpendicular to the expected tilt and
with the sign appropriate to that axis, because it sets the magnitude of the
available correction. The rotational stiffness of the tilted axis is then kept
as low as the required angular compliance and allowable displacement permit,
since every increase tested reduced alignment. The perpendicular translational
stiffness is adjusted last, and only for tilt about \(t_2\), where it
changed the response measurably.

\section{Limitations}
\label{sec:results_limitations}

The tool can rotate by approximately \(2^\circ\) about \(y_{EE}\) within the
gripper, and the commanded spin places \(y_{EE}\) only \(25^\circ\) from
\(t_2\). For an tilt about \(t_2\) the compliant direction of the mount
therefore nearly coincides with the axis being corrected, so part of the
contact moment can be taken up by the tool rotating relative to the
end-effector. Alignment is inferred from the end-effector pose, so such motion
is not observable and appears as a reduced improvement.

The repetition of the \(10^\circ\) \(t_2\) setting that removed \(0.00^\circ\)
is interpreted as an instance of this behaviour. It is retained because it is a
property of the fixture rather than a recording fault, and because it is the
clearest evidence in the measurement set that the limitation is material. The
consequence is directional: unobserved motion at the mount can only conceal
correction that occurred, so the reported \(t_2\) improvements are lower bounds
and the measured tangent-axis asymmetry is not an artefact of the mount.

The dispersion statistics are consistent with this account. The median
within-setting standard deviation is \(0.02^\circ\) for a tilt about \(t_1\) and
\(0.03^\circ\) for \(t_2\), and the two largest dispersions in the measurement
set both occur at \(t_2\). Dispersion also grows with the magnitude of the
correction on both axes, so the effect is not attributed exclusively to the
tilt axis.

Three further limitations apply. The alignment metric describes the combined
robot--gripper--tool response and cannot separate controller compliance from
gripper compliance; a physically flat tool may coexist with a nonzero inferred
residual. The commanded tilt was positive in every case, so its sign
was not varied and any asymmetry between positive and negative
tilt is unmeasured. The compliance-centre sweep used three tangential
levels on one axis at a time, which locates the favourable sign and establishes
the magnitude of the effect but does not identify an optimum or any interaction
between the two in-plane coordinates.
