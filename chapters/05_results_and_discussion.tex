\chapter{Results and Discussion}
\label{ch:results_discussion}

This chapter presents the measured response of the surface-contact experiments
and the complementary Cartesian pose-hold study. The contact experiments are
not discussed in alphabetical Case A--H order. Instead, the results are
organised according to the experimental questions. The zero-lever response is
established first, followed by the stiffness variations, the tangential
compliance-centre study, the remaining compliance-centre dependencies, and the
generalisation of the lever-direction rule. The final sections combine these
results into a cross-case interpretation.

The principal controller-response quantity is the signed set-up rotation
\mbox{\(\Delta\theta_{\mathrm{set}}\)} about the commanded rotation direction,
as defined in \mbox{\cref{sec:experimental_evaluation}}. The alignment angle
\mbox{\(\theta_{\mathrm{align}}\)}, the TCP-height flatness classification,
displacements, commanded wrench, and model-estimated normal load are used as
supporting quantities.

\section{Data Quality and Comparison Basis}
\label{sec:results_data_quality}
\label{sec:results_repeatability}
\label{sec:results_alignment}
\label{sec:results_quantities}

Of the \mbox{\(171\)} recorded contact runs, \mbox{\(170\)} provide analysable
measurements. One run of a Case-E setting was not recorded completely, so that
setting is evaluated from two repetitions; all other settings use three. No
completed run was excluded.

Repeatability was assessed from the usable repetitions of each setting. The
median within-setting standard deviation of the signed set-up rotation was
approximately \mbox{\(0.04^\circ\)} about \mbox{\(t_1\)} and
\mbox{\(0.14^\circ\)} about \mbox{\(t_2\)}. Differences below approximately
\mbox{\(0.1^\circ\)} are therefore treated as being of the same order as the
observed experimental scatter. This value is a descriptive comparison scale
rather than a statistical significance limit. The scatter is not uniform:
individual Case-E settings reached \mbox{\(0.42^\circ\)} about \mbox{\(t_1\)}
and \mbox{\(2.35^\circ\)} about \mbox{\(t_2\)} near transitions between
response regimes.

The final evaluation interval was close to steady state for most conditions.
During the final second of set-up, the median changes in the model-estimated
normal load and the alignment angle were \mbox{\(0.10\,\mathrm{N}\)} and
\mbox{\(0.00^\circ\)}, respectively. The largest corresponding changes were
\mbox{\(1.10\,\mathrm{N}\)} and \mbox{\(0.60^\circ\)}. These values support a
quasi-static comparison of the final responses, while the complete transient
behaviour remains visible in the recorded time histories.

The alignment angle at the beginning of set-up was not identical to the
commanded orientation offset because the preceding orientation phase ended
with finite tracking error. Matched conditions nevertheless started
reproducibly. A commanded \mbox{\(+10^\circ\)} offset reached
\mbox{\(9.34\pm0.02^\circ\)} about \mbox{\(t_1\)} and
\mbox{\(10.04\pm0.03^\circ\)} about \mbox{\(t_2\)}. At the smaller command,
the corresponding initial values were \mbox{\(4.29^\circ\)} and
\mbox{\(5.19^\circ\)}. The commanded offset therefore identifies the
experimental condition, while the measured initial value is retained in the
figures and tables where it is required.

\section{Surface-Contact Results}
\label{sec:results_cases_a_h}

The contact results are organised by their role in the comparison. Case~D
provides the zero-lever reference. Cases~A and~B quantify stiffness
sensitivity. Case~E then tests the tangential compliance-centre lever over both
surface tangents and both signs of the commanded orientation offset. Cases~F,
G, and H examine the lever-definition frame, the tool-axis centre position,
and the commanded orientation-offset magnitude. Case~C is presented last
because it tests whether the direction rule obtained from the principal
surface tangents extends to intermediate tangent-plane directions.

\label{sec:results_case_d}

therefore produces no moment through a compliance-centre lever, and the

the common Cartesian impedance and null-space settings.

The zero-lever response is strongly axis-dependent. For the positive
\mbox{\(10^\circ\)} command, the end effector rotates
\mbox{\(+7.57^\circ\)} about \mbox{\(t_1\)} but
\mbox{\(-1.63^\circ\)} about \mbox{\(t_2\)}. Reversing the commanded
orientation gives \mbox{\(-10.83^\circ\)} about \mbox{\(t_1\)} and
\mbox{\(-1.25^\circ\)} about \mbox{\(t_2\)}. Substantial alignment-directed
end-effector rotation is therefore already present about \mbox{\(t_1\)},
whereas the zero-lever response about \mbox{\(t_2\)} remains small and does not
follow the desired sign for the positive command.

The TCP-height criterion classifies all five Case-D settings as flat. This is a
geometry-based classification and does not independently measure the physical
grinding-face orientation. The difference between the TCP-height result and
the alignment angle calculated from the end-effector pose is consistent with
relative tool--gripper motion and with the calibration assumptions used by the
alignment metric. The experiment does not independently determine the
magnitude or physical cause of that relative motion.

The zero-orientation-offset condition further shows why the alignment angle is
kept separate from the controller-response metric. The alignment angle changes
from \mbox{\(0.74^\circ\)} at set-up entry to approximately
\mbox{\(2.30^\circ\)} after set-up although no initial orientation offset is
commanded, while the TCP-height criterion still classifies the tool as flat.
The signed set-up rotation is therefore used for the controller comparisons
that follow.

The sign of the commanded orientation determines which side of the TCP assists
the required rotation. About \mbox{\(t_1\)}, the positive command produces
\mbox{\(+7.87^\circ\)} at \mbox{\(+40\,\mathrm{mm}\)} but only
\mbox{\(+0.14^\circ\)} at \mbox{\(-40\,\mathrm{mm}\)}. Reversing the
command exchanges the assisting and opposing sides, with
\mbox{\(-11.30^\circ\)} at \mbox{\(-40\,\mathrm{mm}\)} and
\mbox{\(-0.09^\circ\)} at \mbox{\(+40\,\mathrm{mm}\)}.

About \mbox{\(t_2\)}, the effect of the lever is even clearer relative to the
zero-lever reference. For the positive command, the signed response changes
from \mbox{\(-1.63^\circ\)} at the TCP to
\mbox{\(+4.43^\circ\)} at the assisting \mbox{\(+40\,\mathrm{mm}\)}
position. For the reversed command, the assisting end is exchanged and gives
\mbox{\(-10.51^\circ\)} at \mbox{\(-40\,\mathrm{mm}\)}. The tested
\mbox{\(40\,\mathrm{mm}\)} lever therefore changes the sign of the robot
rotation about \mbox{\(t_2\)}, although it does not remove the complete initial
alignment angle for the positive \mbox{\(10^\circ\)} command.

The two tangents also differ in their sensitivity on the assisting side.
About \mbox{\(t_1\)}, positions from \mbox{\(-10\)} to
\mbox{\(+40\,\mathrm{mm}\)} all give set-up rotations between
\mbox{\(+7.45\)} and \mbox{\(+7.87^\circ\)}, close to the
\mbox{\(+7.57^\circ\)} zero-lever value. About \mbox{\(t_2\)}, the lever is
required to obtain a positive alignment-directed end-effector rotation from
the positive-command zero-lever response.

The transition between assisting and opposing behaviour is not equally smooth
at all positions. The positive \mbox{\(t_1\)} condition changes sharply
between \mbox{\(-10\)} and \mbox{\(-20\,\mathrm{mm}\)}, and the
\mbox{\(-20\,\mathrm{mm}\)} setting has a standard deviation of
\mbox{\(0.42^\circ\)}. The positive \mbox{\(t_2\)} condition at
\mbox{\(-40\,\mathrm{mm}\)} has a standard deviation of
\mbox{\(2.35^\circ\)}. These points are treated separately from the
approximately \mbox{\(0.1^\circ\)} descriptive comparison scale.

The controller-response time histories support the interpretation that the
lever direction changes the commanded moment rather than merely the normal
press magnitude.

\subsubsection{Case~G: Tool-Axis Centre Position}
\label{sec:results_case_g}

\mbox{\(+10^\circ\)} command about \mbox{\(t_1\)}. An axial displacement would
produce no tangential coupling moment if the tool axis were exactly parallel

displacement contains a small tangential component, so a weak dependence is

\mbox{\(-45^\circ\)} and \mbox{\(0.05^\circ\)} at
\mbox{\(+45^\circ\)}, both within the descriptive experimental scatter. The
fixed lever still has a substantial projection along the selected direction
at these diagonals, so the experiment does not show a meaningful performance
separation there.

The \mbox{\(t_2\)} comparison is different. The selected lever gives
\mbox{\(+4.43^\circ\)}, whereas the fixed \mbox{\(t_1\)} lever gives
\mbox{\(-1.61^\circ\)}. The direction rule is therefore supported over the
four tested directions, with the clearest experimental separation occurring
when the selected and fixed lever directions are orthogonal.

\section{Null-Space Pose-Hold Results}
\label{sec:results_nullspace}

The null-space pose-hold study is separate from the contact cases. Matched
trials compare the selectable null-space terms under the same internally
commanded point-force-equivalent disturbance. The commanded disturbance
reached \mbox{\(20.00\,\mathrm{N}\)} in all twelve trials, with peak equivalent
joint-torque norms between \mbox{\(2.17\)} and
\mbox{\(2.45\,\mathrm{N\,m}\)}. The torque-limit scale remained equal to one,
so the waveform was applied without clipping.

Projected null-space damping reduced the cumulative redundant joint excursion
from \mbox{\((7.596\pm0.916)^\circ\)} without null-space torque to
\mbox{\((5.687\pm0.228)^\circ\)} at
\mbox{\(d_{\mathrm{null}}=2\,\mathrm{N\,m\,s/rad}\)}, a reduction of
approximately \mbox{\(25\,\%\)}. The final change in
\mbox{\(\sigma_{\min}\)} became less negative, from
\mbox{\((-2.13\pm0.47)\times10^{-3}\)} to
\mbox{\((-1.26\pm0.10)\times10^{-3}\)}.

Both sigma-only settings returned \mbox{\(\sigma_{\min}\)} approximately to
its initial value by the end of the trial. The final changes were
\mbox{\((1.95\pm0.07)\times10^{-5}\)} at
\mbox{\(k_\sigma=1.5\,\mathrm{N\,m}\)} and
\mbox{\((1.93\pm0.03)\times10^{-5}\)} at
\mbox{\(2\,\mathrm{N\,m}\)}. The lower torque magnitude achieved the same final
recovery with lower cumulative excursion,
\mbox{\((0.283\pm0.007)^\circ\)} instead of
\mbox{\((1.619\pm0.130)^\circ\)}, and a lower mean peak Cartesian position
error, \mbox{\(0.889\pm0.024\,\mathrm{mm}\)} instead of
\mbox{\(0.983\pm0.053\,\mathrm{mm}\)}. The largest position error measured in
the complete pose-hold study was \mbox{\(1.304\,\mathrm{mm}\)}, below the
predefined \mbox{\(2\,\mathrm{mm}\)} acceptance limit.

The geometric Jacobian combines translational and rotational rows without a
common length scale. The reported \mbox{\(\sigma_{\min}\)} is therefore used
only as a relative indicator under the fixed Jacobian convention.

The pose-hold trials isolate the projected damping and sigma-conditioning terms
under the repeatable commanded disturbance. By contrast, the surface-contact
experiments use both contributions together in the combined secondary-torque
mode and therefore do not separate their individual effects during contact.

\section{Cross-Case Discussion}
\label{sec:discussion_contact}

\subsection{Relative Influence of the Tested Parameters}
\label{sec:discussion_parameter_influence}
\label{sec:discussion_pole_height}

The tested parameters do not influence the contact response equally.
Increasing command-axis rotational stiffness from
\mbox{\(5\)} to \mbox{\(50\,\mathrm{N\,m/rad}\)} changes the signed set-up
rotation by \mbox{\(-4.84^\circ\)} about \mbox{\(t_1\)} and
\mbox{\(+1.15^\circ\)} about \mbox{\(t_2\)}. Changing the perpendicular
translational stiffness from \mbox{\(2000\)} to
\mbox{\(300\,\mathrm{N/m}\)} changes the response by only
\mbox{\(+0.02^\circ\)} about \mbox{\(t_1\)} and
\mbox{\(-0.61^\circ\)} about \mbox{\(t_2\)}.

The tangential compliance-centre sweep produces the largest within-series
changes: \mbox{\(7.73^\circ\)} between the outer positions for the positive
\mbox{\(t_1\)} command and \mbox{\(8.94^\circ\)} for the positive
\mbox{\(t_2\)} command. Within the tested configuration and ranges, the
tangential lever therefore has a stronger influence on the set-up rotation
than the tested translational-stiffness variation and a comparable or larger
influence than the rotational-stiffness variation.

This comparison does not imply that gain selection is unimportant. Rotational
stiffness directly determines the resistance to angular motion, and the
cross-axis translational stiffness can modify the response in some directions.
The measurements nevertheless indicate that selecting the compliance-centre
lever is the first geometric decision because it determines whether the normal
press creates a moment that assists or opposes the required rotation.

\subsection{Physical Role of the Compliance-Centre Lever}
\label{sec:discussion_compliance_lever}
\label{sec:discussion_placement}

The sign dependence observed in Case~E follows directly from the
point-shifted impedance. With the press directed approximately along the
negative surface normal,
\mbox{\(f_{\mathrm{press}}\approx-F_n n_s\)}, the coupling moment is

\begin{equation}
    m_c=-r_c\times f_{\mathrm{press}}.
\end{equation}

For the two principal surface tangents, the selected tangential lever is

\begin{equation}
r_{c,t}=-\rho_c t_2
\quad\text{about } t_1,
\qquad
r_{c,t}=+\rho_c t_1
\quad\text{about } t_2,
\qquad \rho_c>0,
\label{eq:discussion_general_lever_selection}
\end{equation}

which gives, under the same normal-force approximation,

\begin{equation}
m_c\approx-F_n\rho_c t_1
\quad\text{about } t_1,
\qquad
m_c\approx-F_n\rho_c t_2
\quad\text{about } t_2.
\label{eq:discussion_general_lever_moment}
\end{equation}

The lever must therefore lie along the tangent perpendicular to the commanded
rotation direction, with a sign chosen so that the induced moment acts in the
alignment direction. Reversing the commanded orientation offset reverses the
required lever sign. Case~E supports this rule on both principal tangents and
at both signs of the commanded offset. Case~C supports the extension to the
four tested tangent-plane directions, while the diagonal comparisons remain
within the experimental scatter because the fixed lever retains a substantial
component in the selected direction.

The weak Case-G sensitivity is also consistent with the cross-product
mechanism. A lever component parallel to the normal press produces no moment.
For a tool inclined by approximately \mbox{\(10^\circ\)}, an axial
\mbox{\(40\,\mathrm{mm}\)} displacement has only
\mbox{\(40\sin 10^\circ\approx6.9\,\mathrm{mm}\)} of tangential projection.
The direct tangential Case-E lever is therefore much more effective over the
tested range.

\subsection{Axis-Dependent Response and Measurement Interpretation}
\label{sec:discussion_contact_alone}

The zero-lever Case-D response shows that the two principal surface tangents
cannot be treated as dynamically equivalent. The end effector already rotates
substantially about \mbox{\(t_1\)} without a compliance-centre lever, whereas
the corresponding \mbox{\(t_2\)} response is small. This difference explains
why the assisting \mbox{\(40\,\mathrm{mm}\)} lever adds only
\mbox{\(0.30^\circ\)} to the positive \mbox{\(t_1\)} response but changes the
positive \mbox{\(t_2\)} response from \mbox{\(-1.63\)} to
\mbox{\(+4.43^\circ\)}.

The asymmetry is consistent with the rectangular contact geometry and the
direction-dependent compliance of the tool mounting. The grinding face has
different half-dimensions across the two surface tangents, and unloaded
clearance was observed near the mounting direction associated with
\mbox{\(t_2\)}. The experiments do not isolate the individual contributions of
contact geometry and tool-mount motion.

Across all contact conditions, the TCP-height criterion classified
\mbox{\(50\)} of the \mbox{\(57\)} parameter settings as flat. The seven
settings classified as tilted are listed in
\mbox{\cref{tab:results_not_flat}}.

The flatness classification and the alignment angle answer different
questions. TCP height is based on the calibrated plane and grinding-face
geometry, whereas the alignment angle is calculated from the measured
end-effector orientation and the calibrated grinding-face direction. A
difference between them is therefore consistent with relative tool--gripper
motion or with the assumptions of the geometric reconstruction, but it is not
an independent measurement of physical grinding-face rotation.

The alignment angle can also carry a bias from the calibrated grinding-face
direction. Because the same calibration is used for all runs, this bias is
common to the evaluation chain, but it does not necessarily appear as an
identical additive angular offset in every orientation. The primary signed
set-up rotation does not use the calibrated grinding-face direction; it is
obtained from the measured end-effector motion and then resolved in the
surface frame. This makes it the more direct quantity for comparing the
controller-induced rotation between settings.

\subsection{Implications for Controller Parameter Selection}
\label{sec:discussion_lever_frame}
\label{sec:discussion_parameter_selection}

Within a similar contact geometry, the measurements suggest selecting the
compliance-centre lever before refining the stiffness values. The tangential
lever direction is chosen perpendicular to the commanded rotation direction,
and its sign is selected so that the press-induced moment assists alignment.
The lever-definition frame must also be specified: Case~F shows that a
tool-fixed and a surface-fixed lever with the same nominal coordinates can
produce very different responses once the end effector rotates.

The lever magnitude is then bounded by both the desired coupling and the robot
torque limits. The reported tangential sweep ends at
\mbox{\(40\,\mathrm{mm}\)}. This value adds little to the already large
positive \mbox{\(t_1\)} zero-lever rotation but is required to obtain a
positive alignment-directed \mbox{\(t_2\)} rotation in the tested
configuration. It does not establish a best-performing or sufficient
\mbox{\(t_2\)} magnitude. A wider, safety-bounded sweep would be required for
that conclusion.

An additional exploratory tool-axis test outside the reported Case-G range
used a \mbox{\(120\,\mathrm{mm}\)} displacement. In that test, the commanded
sixth-joint torque reached \mbox{\(21.4\,\mathrm{N\,m}\)} against its
\mbox{\(12\,\mathrm{N\,m}\)} limit and the run aborted. Across the reported
sweep, the same joint remained near \mbox{\(6\,\mathrm{N\,m}\)} and reached
\mbox{\(15.1\,\mathrm{N\,m}\)} only at the final tested position. The centre
position must therefore be selected together with the joint-torque limits.

After the lever has been selected, the rotational stiffness about the
commanded axis can be refined. Increasing it reduced the available alignment
motion over the tested range. The perpendicular translational stiffness had a
smaller effect and can be treated as a secondary tuning variable for this
geometry. This ordering is a practical interpretation of the present
measurements, not a universal optimisation rule.

\subsection{Scope of Interpretation}
\label{sec:results_limitations}

The conclusions of this chapter apply to the tested Panda configuration,
rectangular grinding tool, plane surface, dry-contact condition, commanded
press trajectory, and sampled parameter ranges. The numerical lever distances
are therefore geometry-specific. The transferable result is the relationship
between the signed commanded rotation direction and the tangential lever
direction and sign, supported over the tested directions.

The physical grinding-face orientation was not measured independently under
load. The alignment angle relies on the calibrated tool-to-end-effector
relation, while the TCP-height classification relies on the calibrated face
geometry. These quantities are therefore retained as supporting indicators
rather than substituted for the measured end-effector set-up rotation.

The surface-contact experiments used projected null-space damping and
singular-value conditioning together in the combined secondary-torque mode.
Their individual effects during contact are not isolated. The separate
pose-hold study provides that comparison under the commanded disturbance.
Finally, successful operation through the nominal \mbox{\(1\,\mathrm{kHz}\)}
Franka Control Interface demonstrates the implemented operating rate but does
not constitute a worst-case execution-time or jitter analysis.
