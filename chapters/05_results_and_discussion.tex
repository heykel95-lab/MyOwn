\chapter{Results and Discussion}
\label{ch:results_discussion}
\ReviewMark{purple}{The chapter was revised in full and every paragraph is
settled. No further editing is to be applied to it, and any remaining concern
is raised as a comment rather than as a change.}

\Added{This chapter presents the measured response of the surface-contact tests
and the separate Cartesian pose-hold study. The eight contact cases introduced in
\mbox{\cref{tab:experimental_test_cases}} are evaluated using the alignment change
relative to the calibrated surface, together with the measured rotation,
displacement, and model-estimated external load. Case~A provides the reference
for the stiffness and compliance-centre comparisons in Cases~B--H. The results
are interpreted as answers to the three linked research questions rather than
as separate case-specific findings: Cases~A--C establish the baseline and
stiffness response; Cases~D, F, and G establish the compliance-centre direction,
sign, and magnitude behaviour; and Cases~E and H test the coordinate-frame
interpretation and the tilt-dependent selection rule.}

\section{Data Quality and Evaluation Basis}
\label{sec:results_data_quality}
\label{sec:results_repeatability}
\label{sec:results_alignment}

\Revised{All contact runs completed the set-up phase and were retained for analysis.
Each parameter setting was repeated three times, and the tables report the mean
and standard deviation of the corresponding repetitions. One repetition in the
\mbox{\(10^\circ\)} \mbox{\(t_2\)} condition differed substantially from the other two. It
was retained so that the reported mean and dispersion include the complete
measured response. Its possible relation to the tool mounting is discussed in
\mbox{\cref{sec:results_limitations}}.}

\Revised{The primary result is the change during set-up in the angle between the
calibrated surface normal and the grinding-face direction calculated from the
measured end-effector orientation. A positive value denotes a reduction of this
angle and therefore an improvement in alignment. Because the tool could rotate
by approximately \mbox{\(\pm2^\circ\)} relative to the gripper about
\mbox{\(y_{\mathrm{EE}}\)}, the quantity represents the combined response of the
robot, gripper, and tool. The physical rotation of the grinding face was not
measured independently.}

\Added{Repeatability was assessed from the three repetitions of each of the
\mbox{\(58\)} contact settings. The median within-setting standard deviation of the
alignment improvement was \mbox{\(0.03^\circ\)} for tests about \mbox{\(t_1\)} and
\mbox{\(0.05^\circ\)} for tests about \mbox{\(t_2\)}. Differences smaller than approximately
\mbox{\(0.1^\circ\)} are therefore treated as being of the same order as the observed
experimental scatter. This value is used as a descriptive comparison scale
rather than as a statistical significance limit.}

\Revised{The reported values were calculated from settled intervals. During the final
second of set-up, the median change in the model-estimated normal load was
\mbox{\(0.10\,\mathrm{N}\)}, and the median change in the alignment angle was
\mbox{\(0.00^\circ\)}. The largest corresponding changes were
\mbox{\(1.10\,\mathrm{N}\)} and \mbox{\(0.60^\circ\)}. For settings that retained the zero
compliance-centre lever, the steady model-estimated normal load remained
between \mbox{\(44.5\)} and \mbox{\(49.4\,\mathrm{N}\)}. The contact comparisons were
therefore made under similar estimated preload levels, except where the lever
position itself altered the contact response.}

\Revised{The alignment angle at the beginning of set-up differed from the commanded
angular offset. This difference increased with the command and reflects the
orientation-transition tolerance, the calibrated geometry, and the compliance
of the tool mounting. For example, a \mbox{\(5^\circ\)} command about \mbox{\(t_1\)}
produced an initial alignment angle of \mbox{\(2.45^\circ\)}, whereas a
\mbox{\(10^\circ\)} command about \mbox{\(t_2\)} produced \mbox{\(8.45^\circ\)}. The measured
angle at the beginning of set-up is therefore used as the initial value in all
subsequent comparisons.}

\section{Case A: Effect of the Commanded Tilt}
\label{sec:results_case_a}
\label{sec:results_summary_table}

alignment angle increased from \mbox{\(1.07^\circ\)} to \mbox{\(2.04^\circ\)}, which gives
an alignment improvement of \mbox{\(-0.97^\circ\)}. Set-up therefore introduced a
systematic orientation change in this condition. The zero-tilt value was kept
as a separate reference and was not subtracted from the tilted results in
\mbox{\cref{tab:results_case_a}}.}

\Revised{At both commanded magnitudes, the measured improvement was larger for a tilt
about \mbox{\(t_2\)} than for the corresponding tilt about \mbox{\(t_1\)}. At

\Revised{The fraction of the measured initial angle removed during set-up decreased as

approximately \mbox{\(38\,\%\)} for the \mbox{\(5^\circ\)} command to approximately
\mbox{\(9\,\%\)} for the \mbox{\(10^\circ\)} command. About \mbox{\(t_2\)}, the corresponding
values were approximately \mbox{\(35\,\%\)} and \mbox{\(13\,\%\)}. Within the tested range,
the alignment improvement therefore did not increase proportionally with the
initial angular error.}

\Revised{The \mbox{\(10^\circ\)} \mbox{\(t_2\)} condition showed substantially greater variability
than the remaining settings. One repetition produced \mbox{\(0.00^\circ\)} of
alignment improvement, whereas the other two produced \mbox{\(1.56^\circ\)} and
\mbox{\(1.62^\circ\)}. Retaining all three repetitions gives the reported standard
deviation of \mbox{\(0.92^\circ\)}.}

paired rotational-stiffness value.}

in-plane lever therefore determined whether the induced moment assisted or
opposed the measured alignment motion.}

\Revised{The model-estimated normal load was also lower for the assisting placements.
The steady values were \mbox{\(36.5\,\mathrm{N}\)} and \mbox{\(39.2\,\mathrm{N}\)},
compared with approximately \mbox{\(45\)} to \mbox{\(47\,\mathrm{N}\)} for the zero-lever
settings. The commanded press remained unchanged.}

\Revised{Placing the centre of compliance at the grinding-face centre produced
\mbox{\(0.59^\circ\)} of improvement for the \mbox{\(t_1\)} condition and
\mbox{\(1.35^\circ\)} for the \mbox{\(t_2\)} condition. These values differ from the
corresponding zero-lever results by \mbox{\(0.03^\circ\)} and \mbox{\(-0.03^\circ\)}, which
is below the descriptive comparison scale. The geometric interpretation of the
in-plane and tool-axis lever components is discussed in
\mbox{\cref{sec:discussion_compliance_lever}}.}

\section{Discussion}
\label{sec:discussion_contact}

\subsection{Relative Influence of the Varied Parameters}
\label{sec:discussion_parameter_influence}
\label{sec:discussion_pole_height}

\Revised{The measured effect sizes differed substantially across the varied parameter
families. For \mbox{\(t_1\)}, increasing the tilted-axis rotational stiffness from
\mbox{\(5\)} to \mbox{\(50\,\mathrm{N\,m/rad}\)} reduced the alignment improvement by
\mbox{\(0.15^\circ\)}. For \mbox{\(t_2\)}, the highest tested rotational stiffness reduced
the response by \mbox{\(0.44^\circ\)} relative to the
\mbox{\(15\,\mathrm{N\,m/rad}\)} condition. Reducing the perpendicular translational
stiffness from \mbox{\(2000\)} to \mbox{\(300\,\mathrm{N/m}\)} changed the response by
\mbox{\(-0.06^\circ\)} about \mbox{\(t_1\)} and by \mbox{\(+0.73^\circ\)} about \mbox{\(t_2\)}.}

\Revised{The compliance-centre lever produced the largest measured changes. Relative to
the zero-lever condition, the assisting \mbox{\(60\,\mathrm{mm}\)} lever increased
the alignment improvement by \mbox{\(5.49^\circ\)} about \mbox{\(t_1\)} and
\mbox{\(4.98^\circ\)} about \mbox{\(t_2\)}. The opposing lever direction reduced the
improvement to \mbox{\(0.41^\circ\)} and \mbox{\(0.02^\circ\)}, respectively. Within the
tested configuration and parameter range, the compliance-centre position and
its sign therefore had a substantially stronger influence than the individual
stiffness variations.}

\Revised{The measurements support considering the compliance-centre lever before
refining the stiffness entries. The rotational stiffness controls the angular
resistance of the impedance, and the perpendicular translational stiffness can
modify the response for particular directions. The lever, however, directly
determines whether the normal press produces a moment that assists or opposes
the required rotation.}

\subsection{Role of the Compliance-Centre Lever}
\label{sec:discussion_compliance_lever}
\label{sec:discussion_placement}

\Revised{The sign dependence observed in Case~D follows from the point-shifted
impedance. With the press directed approximately along the negative surface
normal, \mbox{\(f\approx-Fn_s\)}, the coupling moment is}

\begin{equation}
    m_c=-r_c\times f.
\end{equation}

\Added{For an arbitrary signed tilt axis \mbox{\(u_{\mathrm{tilt}}\)} in the
tangent plane, choose the tangential lever according to}

\begin{equation}
r_{c,t}
=
-\rho\,n_s\times u_{\mathrm{tilt}},
\qquad \rho>0.
\label{eq:discussion_general_lever_selection}
\end{equation}

\Added{With \mbox{\(f\approx-Fn_s\)}, this selection gives}

\begin{equation}
m_c
\approx
-F\rho\,u_{\mathrm{tilt}}.
\label{eq:discussion_general_lever_moment}
\end{equation}

\Added{The induced moment is therefore opposite to the signed angular error.
The lever lies along the corresponding normal-error direction
\mbox{\(d_{\mathrm{tilt}}=u_{\mathrm{tilt}}\times n_s\)}, not along the
rotation axis itself. Reversing the required corrective rotation changes
\mbox{\(u_{\mathrm{tilt}}\)} to \mbox{\(-u_{\mathrm{tilt}}\)} and reverses the
selected lever. Cases~D and F tested the tangent-axis instances of this vector
rule, while Case~H tested four directions in the tangent plane.}

\Revised{A lever component along the tangent perpendicular to the tilted axis therefore
creates a moment about that axis. Reversing the lever reverses the moment. One
sign assists the rotation that brings the grinding face towards the surface,
whereas the opposite sign opposes it. Exchanging the roles of \mbox{\(t_1\)} and
\mbox{\(t_2\)} changes the cross-product direction, which is consistent with the
assisting lever signs being opposite for the two tilted axes.}

\Revised{The component of \mbox{\(r_c\)} parallel to the press direction contributes no moment
because its cross product with \mbox{\(f\)} is zero. This explains why the
compliance-centre position at the grinding-face centre produced a response
close to the zero-lever setting. At a \mbox{\(10^\circ\)} tilt, the
\mbox{\(20\,\mathrm{mm}\)} displacement along the grinding-face direction has a
tangential projection of only approximately \mbox{\(3.5\,\mathrm{mm}\)}. This is
about one seventeenth of the \mbox{\(60\,\mathrm{mm}\)} tangential lever used in the
main comparison.}

\Added{Case~H nevertheless measured a \mbox{\(0.9^\circ\)} range when the same
tangential lever was moved along the surface normal. The ideal zero-moment
statement assumes a purely normal force and isolates the force moment from the
remaining coupled motion. In the experiment, tangential force components,
finite translation and rotation, the evolving contact geometry, and compliance
within the tool mounting could each make the normal coordinate affect the
response. The point-shifted stiffness transformation can also change the
effective rotational resistance when a normal offset is introduced. The
measurements do not separate these mechanisms, so they establish a small
normal-coordinate effect without identifying its cause.}

\Revised{For an axial displacement \mbox{\(d\)} and a tilt angle \mbox{\(\theta\)}, the effective
tangential component is \mbox{\(d\sin\theta\)}. At \mbox{\(10^\circ\)}, an axial
displacement of approximately \mbox{\(345\,\mathrm{mm}\)} would be required to
produce a tangential component of \mbox{\(60\,\mathrm{mm}\)}. The tested axial
placement therefore generated substantially less coupling moment than the
direct tangential placement.}

\Revised{The lower model-estimated normal loads at the assisting lever positions are
consistent with the same mechanism. A larger alignment motion allows part of
the commanded displacement to be accommodated through rotation, reducing the
remaining translational compression. This explanation remains model-based
because the external load was estimated by the robot model and the tool
rotation relative to the gripper was not measured independently.}

\Added{The lever is a commanded setting and does not change sign on its own
during a run. Case~F reversed the commanded tilt and measured the response
directly. The assisting direction reversed with it on both surface tangents. A
lever selected from the sign of the measured initial tilt therefore assists the
alignment, while a lever fixed in advance assists one tilt sign and opposes the
other.}

\subsection{Implications for Controller Parameter Selection}
\label{sec:discussion_parameter_selection}

\Revised{The measured ordering suggests a practical sequence for selecting controller
parameters within a similar contact geometry. The compliance-centre lever is
considered first because it determines the direction and approximate magnitude
of the coupling moment. Its tangential direction should be perpendicular to
the expected tilt axis, and its sign should be selected so that the induced
moment assists alignment.}

\Revised{After the lever has been selected, the rotational stiffness about the tilted
axis can be refined. Within the tested range, increasing this stiffness reduced
the available alignment motion. The perpendicular translational stiffness can
then be adjusted where the measurements show a directional benefit. In the
present tests, reducing it increased the response about \mbox{\(t_2\)}, whereas no
clear effect above the descriptive comparison scale was observed about
\mbox{\(t_1\)}.}

\Revised{This sequence is a starting point rather than a complete tuning rule.
It is based on one robot configuration, one tool mounting, a finite set of
commanded tilt directions and magnitudes, and a limited parameter range.
Cases~D, F, and H establish the direction and sign rule at the tested
conditions. Case~G varies the lever magnitude systematically at two positive
tilt magnitudes, while F2 adds one longer lever to each reversed condition.
The best tested magnitude remained condition-dependent, so no single value
applies across the measured directions and tilt magnitudes.}

\section{Limitations}
\label{sec:results_limitations}

\subsection{Experimental Range}

\Added{The experimental conclusions are limited to the tested parameter matrix.
Both tilt signs were commanded at \mbox{\(10^\circ\)}, and one intermediate
magnitude of \mbox{\(5^\circ\)} was tested in Case~G. The rotational and
translational stiffnesses were varied at three levels, and only one combined
low-translational-stiffness and high-rotational-stiffness condition was tested
for each axis. The compliance-centre comparison used four lever magnitudes along
one surface tangent at a time in the magnitude sweep. Case~H additionally
sampled four combined in-plane tilt and lever directions at one common
magnitude. These settings establish the direction and sign of the assisting
lever and the dependence of its magnitude on the commanded tilt. They do not
establish a complete stiffness interaction. Only four in-plane directions were
tested, and a continuous two-dimensional mapping of lever direction and
magnitude was not performed.}

\Added{The measured lever magnitudes apply to the investigated contact geometry.
They follow from the tool-face dimensions, the commanded press, and the
calibrated surface, none of which was varied. The transferable result is the
rule that relates lever direction and sign to the measured tilt. For both
surface tangents, the positive-tilt tests showed a larger best tested magnitude
at \mbox{\(10^\circ\)} than at \mbox{\(5^\circ\)}. The individual values in
millimetres are not transferable to another tool or press.}

\Revised{The results were obtained with one surface, one rectangular tool, one gripper
configuration, and one commanded press trajectory. Changes in friction,
contact geometry, tool dimensions, payload, or robot configuration may change
the measured alignment response and the assisting lever magnitude.}

\subsection{Measurement and Tool-Mount Uncertainty}

\Revised{The alignment metric combines the measured end-effector orientation with the
calibrated grinding-face direction and the calibrated surface normal. Its
absolute value therefore inherits uncertainty from the surface calibration,
the grinding-face calibration, and the relative motion between the tool and
the gripper. The approximately \mbox{\(\pm2^\circ\)} rotational freedom about
\mbox{\(y_{\mathrm{EE}}\)} was of the same order as several measured alignment
changes.}

\Revised{For the tested mounting orientation, \mbox{\(y_{\mathrm{EE}}\)} lay approximately
\mbox{\(25^\circ\)} from \mbox{\(t_2\)}. Relative tool motion may therefore have contributed
more strongly to the \mbox{\(t_2\)} conditions than to the \mbox{\(t_1\)} conditions. The
\mbox{\(10^\circ\)} \mbox{\(t_2\)} repetition that produced no measured alignment
improvement is consistent with this possibility, although the tool mounting
cannot be identified as its sole cause. Retaining the repetition ensures that
the reported mean and standard deviation include the observed variability.}

\Added{The end-effector-derived alignment angle does not capture relative
rotation between the tool and gripper. Because that relative rotation was not
measured independently, its direction and contribution cannot be recovered.
The physical grinding-face rotation may therefore differ from the reported
value. The larger within-setting scatter about \mbox{\(t_2\)} is consistent with
mounting motion, but it does not establish that mechanism. Scatter also grew
with the size of the correction on both tangents, and the largest spread about
\mbox{\(t_1\)} was measured in the \mbox{\(6.05^\circ\)} compliance-centre condition.}

\Revised{The normal load used in the comparison is the model-estimated external load
reported by libfranka. It is a supporting quantity rather than an independent
force measurement. The measured relationship between alignment and estimated
load should therefore be interpreted as a relationship between two signals
derived from the robot state and model.}

\subsection{Scope of the Null-Space and Timing Evaluation}

\Revised{The surface-contact tests used projected null-space damping alone. The
singular-value-conditioning term was inactive, so the alignment results
describe the controller with one null-space contribution rather than both. The
pose-hold study isolates the two terms only in free space under an internally
commanded point-force equivalent. It does not establish their response to
physical link contact, and the conditioning term was not exercised during
surface contact at all.}

\Revised{The controller operated through the nominal \mbox{\(1\,\mathrm{kHz}\)} Franka
Control Interface. The experiments evaluated the resulting motion and contact
response, but they did not measure the worst-case execution time of the
real-time callback. The results therefore establish experimental behaviour at
the nominal control rate rather than a complete timing guarantee.}
