\chapter{Results and Discussion}
\label{ch:results_discussion}

This chapter reports one consolidated evaluation. The archive contains \(92\)
run records, of which \(89\) are usable for the quantities for which they were
recorded. Every numerical result below is derived from an archived run rather
than from an expectation. Three hold records are excluded from quantitative
comparison because the general log is missing or the Cartesian task moved
beyond the registered invariance limit. The exact source commit and effective
parameter set remain attached to every run.

The campaign answers four questions in order. Does the set-up phase reach an
equilibrium, so that a number taken from it means anything? Is the coupled
command path arithmetically correct, so that differences between poles are
real? How large is a difference that is merely noise? And then the substantive
one: \emph{where should the centre of compliance be placed}? The first three
are cheap and everything downstream depends on them, so they are answered
first, in \cref{sec:results_gates}.

\section{Measurement Campaign and Provenance}
\label{sec:results_provenance}

Each run applies a parameter overlay to the nominal configuration, starts the
controller, and archives the raw logs together with the effective parameter
set, the git commit and the complete terminal transcript. The parameter
directory is restored afterwards from a shell trap, because the controller
rewrites the auto-computed coupled matrices into its own configuration at the
end of a set-up phase; without the restore each run would inherit its
predecessor's state and a sweep would measure its own history.

Metrics are extracted twice --- once from the CSV and once from the
controller's printed set-up report --- and a disagreement between the two
flags the run rather than averaging it in silently. Flags separate into
\emph{data} faults, where the numbers themselves are untrustworthy, and
\emph{provenance} caveats, where the repository merely held an uncommitted
change when the run was recorded. Only the former are excluded from the
reported means; the latter remain admissible because each run archives the
exact configuration it used.

\section{Gate Experiments}
\label{sec:results_gates}

Three gates were run before any parameter study. The principle is that a sweep
is worth nothing until three things are established: that the quantity being
swept has settled, so it is a property of the configuration rather than of when
one stopped looking; that the code path producing it computes what it claims
to; and that one knows how large a difference has to be before it is an effect
rather than scatter. Each gate below took under half an hour and each changed
what the rest of the campaign could claim.

\subsection{Equilibrium of the Set-Up Phase}
\label{sec:results_equilibrium}

The set-up phase was originally terminated by a \(4\,\mathrm{s}\) timeout. The
commanded press is a ramp whose duration is
\(|s_{\mathrm{end}}-s_{\mathrm{start}}|/v\approx4.0\,\mathrm{s}\), so the
phase was being cut at the instant the ramp completed. Repeating the phase at
\(4\), \(8\) and \(12\,\mathrm{s}\) gives the tip drift over the final fifth of
the phase as \(0.147^\circ\), \(0.002^\circ\) and \(0.000^\circ\)
respectively. The contact force overshoots to approximately \(65\,\mathrm{N}\)
at \(t=4.0\,\mathrm{s}\) and relaxes to a steady \(60\,\mathrm{N}\) by
\(t\approx4.6\,\mathrm{s}\), after which tip angle, force and edge travel are
constant to the printed resolution for the remaining seven seconds.

The \(4\,\mathrm{s}\) configuration therefore sampled the overshoot of a
transient, not an equilibrium, and the quasi-static reading of the set-up
phase did not hold under it. Twelve seconds was used conservatively while the
campaign was recorded, but it is not required by the measured response.
Re-evaluating the existing \(8\)- and \(12\,\mathrm{s}\) traces at
\(t=5.0\,\mathrm{s}\) gives the remaining changes listed in
\cref{tab:results_five_second_check}.

\(5\,\mathrm{s}\), while slower push experiments are allowed to finish their
ramp.

\subsection{Correctness of the Coupled Command Path}
\label{sec:results_g1}

Feeding block-diagonal gains through the coupled \(6\times6\) path must
reproduce the decoupled wrench exactly; if it does not, every coupled result
is an implementation artefact. Five decoupled runs give a final tip angle of
\(2.458\pm0.111^\circ\) and a steady contact force of
\(59.37\pm0.11\,\mathrm{N}\); three block-diagonal coupled runs give
\(2.537\pm0.232^\circ\) and \(59.02\pm0.65\,\mathrm{N}\). The two groups differ
by \(0.079^\circ\), which is \(0.34\) times the larger of the two standard
deviations. Alignment gain and edge travel differ by \(0.31\) and \(0.72\)
standard deviations respectively. The coupled path is therefore correct, and
the pole effects reported in \cref{sec:results_pole} are properties of the
coupling rather than of its implementation.

\section{Measuring Alignment Against the Physical Plane}
\label{sec:results_alignment}

The alignment quality of interest is the residual angle between the physical
tool axis and the surface normal,
\(\theta_{\mathrm{align}}=\cos^{-1}\!\big(a_T^\top(-n_s)\big)\). This is not
the logged set-up error \(\mathbf e_R\), which is referenced to the
orientation frozen at geometric clearance and therefore reports how far the
tool \emph{turned} rather than how flat it ended up. The controller now logs
\(\theta_{\mathrm{align}}\) directly and prints it before and after the set-up
phase, which removes the instrumentation gap that made earlier versions of
this chapter qualitative.

One further correction is required before the logged values can be
interpreted, and it inverts the sign of the result if omitted. The controller
scores \(\theta_{\mathrm{align}}\) against the \emph{configured} surface
normal, built from the two tilt angles in the parameter set. That
configuration was deliberately left uncalibrated: the physical plane was
measured, with the tool face hand-seated flat and the pose read back, at
\(a=-0.72\pm0.31^\circ\) and \(b=14.99\pm1.91^\circ\) against a configured
\(a=0^\circ\), \(b=5^\circ\), a mismatch of \(10.01^\circ\). The tool is
commanded toward the configured normal, so it arrives at contact
\(10.01^\circ\) away from the surface it is actually pressing on.

The consequence is that the printed alignment gain reads backwards: it becomes
\emph{more negative} the closer the tool gets to the real plane, because it
measures distance from an assumption. All alignment results below are
therefore rescored against the measured plane, using the rotation
\(-\mathbf e_R\) --- the logged orientation error is defined as the rotation
carrying the current orientation onto the frozen reference, so the tool's
actual rotation is its negation.

Two properties of this rescoring should be stated. The \emph{absolute}
residual depends on the measured plane, which three hand-seatings pin only to
about \(\pm1.9^\circ\). The \emph{improvement} --- how much closer to the
surface the tool came --- does not: across all three seatings it varies by
less than \(0.1^\circ\), because the tool rotates in essentially the same
plane as the mismatch. Improvements are therefore reported as measured values
and absolute residuals only as a range.

\subsection{Required Separation of Plane Geometry and Commanded Angle}
\label{subsec:results_plane_command_separation}

The analysis exposes a geometric limitation in the active implementation. One
configured normal is used for plane height, descent, projection, the surface
task frame and the desired tool axis. The intended experiment instead requires
the physical plane to be correct while the tool is deliberately commanded at
a different angle.

The confirmation controller should define the physical plane from three
measured, non-collinear contact points:
\begin{align}
  u &= p_2-p_1, &
  v &= p_3-p_1, \\
  n_s &= \frac{u\times v}{\lVert u\times v\rVert}, &
  p_s &= \frac{p_1+p_2+p_3}{3}.
  \label{eq:results_three_point_plane}
\end{align}
The normal sign is selected toward free space, and near-collinear or closely
spaced points are rejected. The measured positions must be physical probe
points, not uncorrected TCP positions:
\[
  p_i=p_{\mathrm{EE},i}
      +R_{\mathrm{EE},i}c_{\mathrm{probe}}^{EE}.
\]

The calibrated pair \((p_s,n_s)\) then determines height, clearance,
projection, the surface frame and the physical alignment metric. The command
normal remains independent,
\begin{equation}
  n_{\mathrm{command}}
  =
  R(t_2,\delta_2)R(t_1,\delta_1)n_s,
  \label{eq:results_command_normal_offset}
\end{equation}
so a nonzero \((\delta_1,\delta_2)\) creates the intended initial mismatch
without moving the geometric plane. This is a methodological correction for
the confirmation campaign, not a claim that the archived runs used a
three-point calibration.

\section{Where the Compliance Centre Must Lie}
\label{sec:results_pole}

This is the substantive question, and answering it took three sweeps rather
than one, because the first produced a conclusion the second overturned.

The pole was first swept along the \textbf{surface normal}: \(24\) runs at
eight offsets from \(-120\) to \(+160\,\mathrm{mm}\). Alignment improved
steadily along it, from \(+1.98^\circ\) with the pole on the contact edge to
\(+3.47^\circ\) at \(+160\,\mathrm{mm}\). Read alone, that sweep says the
distance along the normal is the design variable.

It is not, and the reason is geometric. The sweep followed the
\emph{configured} normal, which differs by approximately \(10^\circ\) from
the measured physical-plane normal. Relative to the physical plane used to
score alignment, that direction therefore contains a tangential component.
Across the sweep the corresponding tangential offset drifted from
\(-5.0\) to \(+27.6\,\mathrm{mm}\). The physical normal offset and one
tangential offset were varied together, so the sweep alone cannot identify
which component produced the response.

Separating them required holding one fixed and moving the other. The pole was
therefore swept along the first surface tangent \(t_1\) (\(18\) runs, six
offsets from \(-71\) to \(+161\,\mathrm{mm}\)) at a fixed normal offset, and
then along the second tangent \(t_2\) (\(12\) runs, four offsets), an axis
which had been held at approximately zero in everything recorded until that
point. \cref{fig:results_pole_component} plots all \(57\) runs against each
component in turn, and the answer is visible before any fitting: the two
in-plane panels show structure and the normal panel does not.

accounts for \(R^2=0.962\) of the outcome across \(57\) runs. The offset along
the surface normal adds \(0.004\) to that figure, over a range of
\(277\,\mathrm{mm}\), and is not a design variable.

The shape matters as much as the location. A linear fit in \(t_1\) alone
accounts for \(R^2=0.047\): past the optimum the response does not merely
flatten, it reverses. At \(t_1=+161\,\mathrm{mm}\) the improvement is
\(-3.75^\circ\), so a pole placed far beyond the optimum is worse than
commanding no coupling at all. The turnover is consistent with the quadratic
lever contribution \([r_c]_\times^\top K_p[r_c]_\times\) in
\cref{eq:coupled_tcp_stiffness}, which re-stiffens the rotational block as the
lever becomes large.

The fitted shape also explains why the normal sweep looked so convincing on its
own. Its unintended tangential drift, from \(-5.0\) to \(+27.6\,\mathrm{mm}\),
happens to lie entirely on the rising flank of the \(t_1\) optimum. Sampled
over that stretch the response is close to linear and monotonically improving,
which is exactly what a sweep of the design variable would look like.

The model was checked against runs it was not fitted on. Three further runs
placed the pole on the contact edge, at
\((t_1,t_2)=(+5.9,-0.6)\,\mathrm{mm}\), a setting held out of the fit. The
quadratic surface predicts an improvement of \(+2.44^\circ\) there; the
measured value is \(+2.30\pm0.07^\circ\). The \(0.14^\circ\) discrepancy is
well inside the \(0.59^\circ\) noise floor, so the surface generalises rather
than merely interpolating its own training points.

The sign matters as much as the magnitude. At sufficiently negative offsets on
either tangent the coupling does not merely fail to assist: the tool rotates
away from the surface, by \(10.31^\circ\) at \(t_1=-70.9\,\mathrm{mm}\) and
\(6.54^\circ\) at \(t_2=-91.5\,\mathrm{mm}\), ending further from flat than it
arrived. The compliance centre can therefore actively fight the alignment it is
intended to produce. Across the full tested range the improvement spans
\(-10.31^\circ\) to \(+6.96^\circ\), a swing of \(17.3^\circ\) against a noise
floor of \(0.4^\circ\).

Both tangential sweeps place the useful pole on the positive side of their
axis. Taken with the reported contacting edge --- which lies at \(-y\) in the
end-effector frame, and therefore at \(+y\) in the base frame, since the
end-effector \(y\) axis maps to base \(-y\) --- this places the compliance
centre on the \emph{same} side as the contacting edge rather than opposite it.
That is the reverse of the placement rule the experiment was designed to test,
and the reversal is consistent across two independent axes.

One qualification is not yet settled. The contacting edge was reported as a
long \(120\,\mathrm{mm}\) side, but the long sides of the face lie at
\(x=\pm20\,\mathrm{mm}\), not at \(\pm y\). Either the contact is on a short
\(40\,\mathrm{mm}\) end at \(-y\), or it is on a long side at \(-x\). The
measured relationships are unaffected --- positive offsets win on both axes
either way --- but the phrasing of a general placement rule depends on which
edge it is. Independently, the contact point recovered from the measured wrench
lies near the middle of the face in \(t_1\) rather than at the corner the
controller's tie-breaking feature selection reports in \(48\) of \(52\) runs,
which suggests that selection reflects its \(0.1\,\mathrm{mm}\) tolerance
rather than the load distribution.

\section{Effect of Damping}
\label{sec:results_damping}

The damping coefficients are generated at entry to each phase group. The
controller symmetrizes \(JM_q^{-1}J^\top\), adds \(10^{-9}I_6\), inverts the
result, and applies \(d_i=2\zeta\sqrt{\Lambda_{ii}k_i}\) per task coordinate.
The regularization is a numerical device for the mixed-unit Cartesian matrix,
not the SVD cutoff used by the null-space projector. No oscillation was
observed in the rotation-vector response of any run in the campaign;
\(\zeta\) remains a tuning factor rather than a proof of a coupled-system
modal damping ratio.

\section{Null-Space Conditioning: Evidence Boundary}
\label{sec:results_nullspace}

The controller implements a two-point smallest-singular-value conditioning
bias. It compares \(\sigma_{\min}(q+\alpha v_7)\) and
\(\sigma_{\min}(q-\alpha v_7)\), selects the better sampled sign, and projects
the resulting torque through the torque-level null-space projector. The SVD
inverse uses \(1/\sigma_i\) only for singular values retained by the configured
relative cutoff.

The logging required to evaluate the method is present:
\(\sigma_{\min}\), both probes, the selected direction, projected velocity,
sigma torque, Cartesian task drift and
\(\lVert Jn_{\mathrm{best}}\rVert\). The experiment set is not complete. The
archive contains one usable mode-0 hold and no usable conditioning-torque
sweep. It is therefore not defensible to claim that the active bias improves
\(\sigma_{\min}\), preserves the Cartesian task within tolerance, or establishes
a preferred \(k_\sigma\), \(\alpha\) or SVD cutoff.

A valid sigma-enabled result must show more than a positive final
\(\Delta\sigma_{\min}\):
\[
  \Delta\sigma_{\min}>0,\qquad
  \overline{v_{\mathrm{best}}}>0,\qquad
  \Delta e_p<1\,\mathrm{mm},\qquad
  \lVert Jn_{\mathrm{best}}\rVert\ \text{near numerical zero}.
\]
Torque and per-joint motion must also be reported. A positive probe with
negligible projected joint velocity means that the command did not overcome
the present friction and task effects; it is not evidence of successful
conditioning. The null-space contribution of this thesis is consequently the
implemented and instrumented method, while its performance evaluation remains
open.

\section{Discussion}
\label{sec:discussion_contact}

\subsection{Where the Compliance Appears to Be Located}

The design question for a surface-contact task is not whether the robot is
soft or stiff, but where the compliance appears to be located geometrically.
The measurements support that framing and sharpen it: of the two geometric
degrees of freedom available when placing the compliance centre relative to
the contact, only the two in-plane ones are load-bearing. A purely diagonal
stiffness matrix makes the robot compliant without encoding any particular
centre of compliance, and the resulting alignment is whatever the contact
geometry happens to produce.

This is consistent with the assembly literature, where remote and variable
compliance centres are useful precisely because they turn contact forces into
correcting motion instead of jamming
\citep{Nevins1989ComputerControlledAssembly,Watson1978RCC,Wang2019VariableComplianceCenter,Hartisch2022FlexureRCC}.
The present result adds that the useful placement is a tangential one, and
that the same mechanism run in reverse is capable of driving the tool away
from the surface.

\subsection{In the Plane of the Tool, or Above It?}
\label{sec:discussion_pole_height}

A natural question about the centre of compliance is whether it should lie in
the plane of the tool face or be displaced above or below it. The measurements
answer it directly: \textbf{the height does not matter, and the in-plane
position does.} Over a range of \(277\,\mathrm{mm}\) along the surface normal
the alignment outcome is essentially unchanged (\(R^2=0.010\) alone,
\(-0.004^\circ/\mathrm{mm}\) inside the joint model), while the two in-plane
components account for \(R^2=0.962\) and carry a \(17.3^\circ\) swing.

The mechanism is the one the question itself suggests. The tool arrives tilted
relative to the physical plane, so contact begins on one edge, and the
alignment moment about the pole is
\[
  M = r_c \times f ,
\]
with \(r_c\) the lever from the pole to the contact and \(f\) the contact
force. Measured across all \(57\) pole runs, that force sits only
\(10.1^\circ\) off the surface normal --- \(56.0\,\mathrm{N}\) normal against
\(10.0\,\mathrm{N}\) tangential, a ratio of \(5.6:1\). A component of \(r_c\)
along the surface normal is therefore very nearly parallel to \(f\), and
\(r\parallel f\) contributes nothing to the cross product. Raising or lowering
the compliance centre relative to the tool face slides it along the line of
action of the contact force, which changes no moment. Only displacing it
\emph{within} the surface plane creates a lever the normal contact force can
act on.

The measured asymmetry is in fact stronger than that argument alone predicts. A
pure lever account gives an effectiveness ratio of \(|f_n|/|f_t|\approx5.6\)
between in-plane and normal offsets, whereas the fitted slopes differ by about
a factor of \(25\). The remainder comes from the stiffness side of the
construction: the coupling terms that a normal pole offset creates act on
\emph{tangential} displacement, which during set-up is small
(a few millimetres against roughly \(43\,\mathrm{mm}\) of normal press) and
largely reacted by friction rather than converted into rotation.

Two consequences follow for design. The compliance centre should be specified
by its position in the surface plane, and its height treated as a free
parameter --- one fewer quantity to tune, and one that need not be measured
accurately. And within the plane the placement is not a monotone preference but
an optimum, at \(t_1\approx+62\,\mathrm{mm}\) and \(t_2\approx+30\,\mathrm{mm}\)
for this tool and surface, on the same side as the contacting edge; too little
offset leaves the coupling unable to generate the alignment moment, and too
much re-stiffens the rotation through the quadratic lever term until the tool
is driven away from the surface.

\subsection{Alignment Beyond the Set-Up Phase}

The residuals of \cref{sec:results_pole} are upper bounds on the final
misalignment rather than final values. Continuing into the grinding phase, the
preload established during set-up produces further alignment: between the end
of the set-up phase and the end of the grinding phase the residual falls by a
further \(0.18^\circ\) to \(0.64^\circ\), and it falls most where the set-up
phase achieved least. The grinding phase duration was operator-terminated and
therefore uncontrolled, so this is reported as a direction rather than a
magnitude.

\subsection{Practical Implications for Controller Tuning}

Two of the measured results bear directly on tuning. First, the tangential
rotational stiffness trades alignment against nothing --- the contact force is
unchanged across a tenfold change in stiffness --- so it should be set as soft
as the displacement bound permits. Second, the tangential pole offset is worth
an order of magnitude more alignment than the normal offset and carries a sign
change, so it is the parameter that requires care and the one whose
mis-setting is capable of doing harm.

\subsection{Required Confirmation Experiments}
\label{subsec:results_confirmation_experiments}

The strongest measurements should be confirmed after the three-point plane and
independent command normal of
\cref{subsec:results_plane_command_separation} are active. The minimum
high-value campaign is:
\begin{enumerate}
  \item repeat the same three-point calibration five times and validate it
        with at least two held-out surface points;
  \item validate the nominal timing at \(4.5\), \(5.0\), \(6.0\) and
        \(8.0\,\mathrm{s}\), using five repetitions at \(5.0\,\mathrm{s}\);
  \item sweep signed command offsets of \(0\), \(\pm5^\circ\) and
        \(\pm10^\circ\) about both surface tangents;
  \item measure a \(3\times3\) grid around the fitted pole optimum,
        \(t_1=[45,60,75]\,\mathrm{mm}\) and
        \(t_2=[15,30,45]\,\mathrm{mm}\), with three repetitions per point;
  \item compare decoupled impedance, a pole on the selected contact edge and
        the locally optimal pole at matched initial angular offsets; and
  \item repeat the selected setting at separated plane locations and with the
        first-contact edge deliberately reversed.
\end{enumerate}

The first four establish the calibrated geometry, operational timing and local
optimum. The final two test whether the measured sign and benefit generalize
beyond one contact location.

\section{Limitations}
\label{sec:results_limitations}

The archived physical plane was measured by hand-seating the tool face and
reading back the pose three times, giving \(\pm1.9^\circ\) on the tilt. Part of
that spread is seating technique and part is movement of the workpiece.
Absolute residual angles inherit this uncertainty; the reported improvements
vary by less than \(0.1^\circ\) across all three seatings. A three-point
calibration with held-out point residuals is required before absolute
alignment and clearance are treated as calibrated geometric measurements.

The workpiece fixture is compliant enough to move under load, and the tool
came loose in the gripper twice during the campaign, in both cases detected
and the affected runs re-recorded. Neither was instrumented. Run-to-run
variation from the fixture is contained in the repeatability floor of
\cref{tab:results_noise_floor}; a systematic component would not be, and would
bias absolute values without affecting comparisons between settings, which are
what this chapter claims.

Both in-plane optima are interior to the tested ranges and are located by a
quadratic fit through a cross-shaped sample rather than by a full local grid;
the nearest tested settings are \(+62.9\) and \(+39.4\,\mathrm{mm}\). Which
edge of the tool face makes first contact was inferred from the tool model
rather than deliberately controlled, which leaves the mapping from the
measured relationship onto a general placement rule open.

Finally, the starting tool axis in the rescoring of
\cref{sec:results_alignment} is taken to be the configured normal. The
measured pre-contact alignment lies between \(0.41^\circ\) and \(0.49^\circ\)
across the campaign, so this assumption is good to approximately half a
degree.
