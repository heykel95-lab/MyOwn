\chapter{Results and Discussion}
\label{ch:results_discussion}

The surface-contact results are presented according to the experimental cases
defined in \mbox{\Cref{ch:methodology}}, and each result is interpreted where
it appears. The signed set-up rotation
\mbox{\(\Delta\theta_{\mathrm{set}}\)} about the commanded rotation direction
is the primary controller-response quantity, as defined in
\mbox{\Cref{sec:experimental_evaluation}}. The TCP-height flatness criterion
and the alignment angle \mbox{\(\theta_{\mathrm{align}}\)} calculated from the
end-effector pose provide supporting information, together with the
displacements, the commanded wrench, and the model-estimated normal load.

Repeated settings are reported by their mean and standard deviation. The
median within-setting standard deviation of the signed set-up rotation was
approximately \mbox{\(0.04^\circ\)} about \mbox{\(t_1\)} and
\mbox{\(0.14^\circ\)} about \mbox{\(t_2\)}, so differences below approximately
\mbox{\(0.1^\circ\)} are treated as being of the same order as the observed
scatter. This value is a descriptive comparison scale and not a statistical
significance limit. Larger scatter occurred at individual transition
conditions in Case~D, which are identified where they arise.

The final response was close to steady state for most tested conditions.
During the final second of set-up, the median changes in the model-estimated
normal load and the alignment angle were \mbox{\(0.10\,\mathrm{N}\)} and
\mbox{\(0.00^\circ\)}; the largest were \mbox{\(1.10\,\mathrm{N}\)} and
\mbox{\(0.60^\circ\)}. Selected time histories are therefore shown only where
they help explain the underlying controller behaviour.

The alignment angle at the beginning of set-up was not identical to the
commanded orientation offset, because the preceding orientation phase ended
with finite tracking error. Matched conditions nevertheless started
reproducibly: a commanded \mbox{\(+10^\circ\)} offset reached
\mbox{\(9.34\pm0.02^\circ\)} about \mbox{\(t_1\)} and
\mbox{\(10.04\pm0.03^\circ\)} about \mbox{\(t_2\)}. The commanded offset
identifies the experimental condition, and the measured initial value is
retained in the tables where it is required.

\section{Surface-Contact Results}
\label{sec:results_cases_a_h}

The cases are reported in the order in which they progressively constrain
whether one centre of compliance can be fixed before the sign and the
tangent-plane direction of the initial angular misalignment are known.
Case~A provides the
zero-lever reference. Cases~B and~C quantify stiffness sensitivity. Case~D then
tests the tangential compliance-centre lever over both surface tangents and
both signs of the commanded orientation offset, which is where a fixed non-zero
displacement has to hold if it is to hold at all. Cases~E, F, and~G examine the
lever-definition frame, the tool-axis centre position, and the commanded
orientation-offset magnitude. Case~H is presented last because it tests whether
the direction rule obtained from the principal surface tangents extends to
intermediate tangent-plane directions.

\subsection{Zero-Lever Reference: Case~A}
\label{sec:results_case_a}

Case~A records the contact-induced alignment obtained with no virtual lever
present. The centre of compliance is placed on the TCP, so that
\mbox{\(r_c=0\)}. The TCP-centred condition removes the coupling introduced by
the virtual point shift, while leaving the ordinary rotational impedance and
the tool-geometry lever \mbox{\(r_T\)} unchanged. The commanded rotational
moment \mbox{\(m_0\)} remains and the rotational compliance stays finite, so
contact-induced rotational motion remains possible. The measured response is the
zero-coupling reference for the remaining contact cases under the common
Cartesian impedance and null-space settings, and not yet an answer about where
the centre of compliance should be placed.

With the centre of compliance at the TCP, the response is strongly
direction-dependent. As \Cref{tab:results_case_a} records and
\Cref{fig:results_case_a} plots against the commanded tangent, the positive
\mbox{\(10^\circ\)} command rotates the end effector by
\mbox{\(+7.57^\circ\)} about \mbox{\(t_1\)} but by \mbox{\(-1.63^\circ\)}
about \mbox{\(t_2\)}. Reversing the commanded orientation gives
\mbox{\(-10.83^\circ\)} about \mbox{\(t_1\)} and \mbox{\(-1.25^\circ\)} about
\mbox{\(t_2\)}. The \mbox{\(t_1\)} direction therefore already exhibits
substantial alignment-directed motion without a compliance-centre lever. About
\mbox{\(t_2\)} the TCP-centred condition produces no alignment-directed
end-effector rotation under this metric, although the final configuration
satisfies the TCP-height flatness criterion, as the following paragraphs set
out.

This baseline conditions the reading of the following cases, because the effect of
a parameter change has to be interpreted relative to the response already
present without lever-induced coupling. The two principal surface tangents
cannot be treated as dynamically equivalent. The asymmetry is consistent with
the rectangular contact geometry and with the direction-dependent compliance of
the tool mounting: the tool face has different half-dimensions across the two
surface tangents, and unloaded clearance was observed near the mounting
direction associated with \mbox{\(t_2\)}. The experiments do not isolate the
individual contributions of contact geometry and tool-mount motion.

All five Case-A settings satisfy the TCP-height criterion. That criterion and
the alignment angle answer different questions and are not equivalent
measurements of physical tool orientation. TCP height rests on the calibrated
plane and tool geometry, whereas the alignment angle is calculated from the
measured end-effector orientation and the calibrated tool normal. A difference
between them is consistent with relative tool--gripper motion or with the
assumptions of the geometric reconstruction. The mounting play was measured
unloaded, and the instantaneous relative tool--gripper rotation was not tracked
separately during the contact runs.

The zero-orientation-offset condition is the reason the alignment angle is kept
separate from the controller-response metric. It changes from
\mbox{\(0.74^\circ\)} at set-up entry to approximately
\mbox{\(2.30^\circ\)} after set-up although no initial orientation offset is
commanded, while the TCP-height criterion still classifies the tool as flat.
The signed set-up rotation is therefore used for the controller comparisons
that follow.

Case~A establishes the response available without virtual coupling, and shows
that this response differs between the two surface tangents. Where the centre
of compliance should be placed remains open at this point, since no
alternative placement has yet been measured. The cases that follow supply that
comparison.

produce the missing alignment-directed response about \mbox{\(t_2\)}: every
tested value leaves that response negative. The axis dependence of the
zero-lever behaviour survives the rotational-stiffness variation.

The tangential compliance-centre position produced the largest response
variation over the tested parameter ranges. \Cref{tab:results_case_d} gives the seven
tested positions, and \Cref{fig:results_case_d} separates the two commanded
axes so that the sign reversal is readable within each panel. Reversing
the commanded orientation direction reverses the side of the TCP that assists
the motion. About \mbox{\(t_1\)}, the positive command produces
\mbox{\(+7.87^\circ\)} at \mbox{\(+40\,\mathrm{mm}\)} but only
\mbox{\(+0.14^\circ\)} at \mbox{\(-40\,\mathrm{mm}\)}; the reversed command
exchanges the assisting and opposing sides, giving \mbox{\(-11.30^\circ\)} at
\mbox{\(-40\,\mathrm{mm}\)} and \mbox{\(-0.09^\circ\)} at
\mbox{\(+40\,\mathrm{mm}\)}.

About \mbox{\(t_2\)}, the lever matters most relative to the zero-lever
reference. For the positive command, the signed response changes from
\mbox{\(-1.63^\circ\)} at the TCP to \mbox{\(+4.43^\circ\)} at the assisting
\mbox{\(+40\,\mathrm{mm}\)} position, and the final configuration satisfies
the TCP-height flatness criterion. For the reversed command, the assisting end
is exchanged and gives \mbox{\(-10.51^\circ\)} at
\mbox{\(-40\,\mathrm{mm}\)}. The alignment angle calculated from the
end-effector pose does not by itself establish a residual physical tool
orientation, because the instantaneous relative tool--gripper rotation was not
tracked separately during the contact runs.

The sign dependence follows directly from the point-shifted impedance. With
the elastic commanded force directed approximately along the negative surface
normal, \mbox{\(f_K\approx-F_{K,n} n_s\)}, its coupling moment is

\begin{equation}
    m_{c,K}=-r_c\times f_K.
\end{equation}

This is the elastic sign argument. The commanded moment plotted below carries
the complete point-shifted spring-damper response, of which \mbox{\(m_{c,K}\)}
is the part that persists once the reference stops advancing.

For the two principal surface tangents, the selected tangential lever is

\begin{equation}
r_{c,t}=-\rho_c t_2
\quad\text{about } t_1,
\qquad
r_{c,t}=+\rho_c t_1
\quad\text{about } t_2,
\qquad \rho_c>0,
\label{eq:discussion_general_lever_selection}
\end{equation}

which gives, under the same normal-force approximation,

\begin{equation}
m_{c,K}\approx-F_{K,n}\rho_c t_1
\quad\text{about } t_1,
\qquad
m_{c,K}\approx-F_{K,n}\rho_c t_2
\quad\text{about } t_2.
\label{eq:discussion_general_lever_moment}
\end{equation}

The lever must therefore lie along the tangent perpendicular to the commanded
rotation direction, with a sign chosen so that the induced moment acts in the
alignment direction. Reversing the commanded orientation offset reverses the
required lever sign, as the four curves show.

A fixed non-zero tangential centre would have to remain suitable when the sign
or the axis of the initial misalignment changes. The measurements show the
opposite. For the positive command about \mbox{\(t_1\)} the assisting side
lies at positive coordinates, giving \mbox{\(+7.87^\circ\)} against
\mbox{\(+0.14^\circ\)} on the opposing side; for the reversed command the
two sides are exchanged. About \mbox{\(t_2\)} the selected positive side
gives \mbox{\(+4.43^\circ\)} where the zero-lever condition gives
\mbox{\(-1.63^\circ\)}, and the reversed command again exchanges the
assisting side. The lever that assists a command about \mbox{\(t_1\)} lies
along \mbox{\(t_2\)}, and the lever that assists a command about
\mbox{\(t_2\)} lies along \mbox{\(t_1\)}, so the two axes do not share
one displacement either. The direction and sign that make a non-zero tangential
centre assist alignment therefore follow from the initial misalignment. That
misalignment is not known before contact. No tested non-zero tangential centre
consequently satisfies the definition of a direction-independent fixed centre of compliance
used in this thesis.

The two tangents also differ in their sensitivity on the assisting side. About
\mbox{\(t_1\)}, positions from \mbox{\(-10\)} to \mbox{\(+40\,\mathrm{mm}\)}
all give set-up rotations between \mbox{\(+7.45\)} and \mbox{\(+7.87^\circ\)},
close to the \mbox{\(+7.57^\circ\)} zero-lever value, because substantial
alignment-directed rotation is already present there. About \mbox{\(t_2\)},
the lever is required to obtain any positive alignment-directed rotation from
the positive-command zero-lever response.

The transition between assisting and opposing behaviour is not equally smooth
at all positions. The positive \mbox{\(t_1\)} condition changes sharply
between \mbox{\(-10\)} and \mbox{\(-20\,\mathrm{mm}\)}, and the
\mbox{\(-20\,\mathrm{mm}\)} setting has a standard deviation of
\mbox{\(0.42^\circ\)}. The positive \mbox{\(t_2\)} condition at
\mbox{\(-40\,\mathrm{mm}\)} has a standard deviation of
\mbox{\(2.35^\circ\)}. These points are treated separately from the
approximately \mbox{\(0.1^\circ\)} descriptive comparison scale.

The preceding sweep establishes the final dependence on lever position. To
examine the mechanism responsible for that dependence, one representative
command direction is considered in more detail. The positive \mbox{\(t_1\)}
condition is selected because its two outer lever positions produce a large
difference in set-up rotation while the TCP-centred condition provides a
common reference.

\Cref{fig:results_case_d_wrench} places the set-up rotation, the commanded
normal force, and the commanded TCP moment on a common time axis for three
centre positions. At \mbox{\(-40\)}, \mbox{\(0\)}, and
\mbox{\(+40\,\mathrm{mm}\)}, the commanded normal force settles in a similar
range of approximately \mbox{\(-77\)} to \mbox{\(-82\,\mathrm{N}\)}, while the
commanded TCP moment about \mbox{\(t_1\)} settles at approximately
\mbox{\(+3.28\)}, \mbox{\(+0.66\)}, and \mbox{\(-2.44\,\mathrm{N\,m}\)}. The
outer positions therefore produce moments of similar magnitude and opposite
sign while their set-up rotations differ by \mbox{\(7.73^\circ\)}. The
difference in set-up rotation is therefore associated primarily with the moment
generated by the displaced impedance reference rather than with a substantial
change in the normal pressing force. The same two positions are resolved into
all three surface-frame components in \mbox{\Cref{app:additional_plots}}.

The mechanism is a displacement of the reference point, not of the force. A
tangential displacement of the centre of compliance moves the impedance
reference point away from the TCP, so the translational press no longer acts
independently of the rotational part of the impedance. The physical surface
force continues to act where the tool touches; the change is that the elastic
contribution now generates the additional commanded moment
\mbox{\(m_{c,K}=-r_c\times f_K\)} with
\mbox{\(r_c=p_{\mathrm{TCP}}-p_c\)}, converting part of the normal press into
rotation.

That conversion carries a preferred direction. For as long as the normal press
is present, a fixed tangential \mbox{\(r_c\neq0\)} keeps generating a moment
with one rotational sense, so the same displacement assists an angular
deviation of one sign while opposing a deviation of the opposite sign.
\Cref{fig:results_case_d} shows this directly: reversing the commanded
orientation offset reverses the side of the TCP on which the centre of
compliance must lie to assist alignment. The asymmetry follows from the lateral
displacement of the impedance reference while the press remains active, not
from any change in the magnitude of the press itself.

With the centre of compliance at the TCP, \mbox{\(r_c=0\)} and therefore
\mbox{\(m_{c,K}=0\)}. The normal press then produces no additional
translation--rotation coupling through the virtual centre, so that condition is
rotationally neutral with respect to this point-shift mechanism: it selects no
preferred tangential direction and adds no one-sided moment. The neutrality is
confined to that mechanism, since the tool geometry and the tool
mounting remain direction dependent, as Case~A shows. Within that scope it
supplies the property a fixed centre requires. The TCP-centred condition can be set
before the misalignment is known, because it selects no tangential
direction at all, whereas every tested non-zero tangential displacement has to
be chosen from the misalignment it is meant to correct.

The two roles of the displacement are therefore distinct. During set-up a
non-zero tangential displacement can deliberately generate a corrective moment
where the natural contact response is weak or acts in the undesired direction,
as the \mbox{\(t_2\)} results show. That role is
condition dependent by construction. Once the tool has reached the
surface-contact configuration, retaining the same displacement while the press
continues also retains its preferred moment direction, so the TCP-centred
condition is also the appropriate virtual reference for the sustained contact
that follows. The tangentially shifted centre is accordingly read as a
means of adding alignment authority during set-up rather than as a candidate
fixed setting.

The implemented phase structure follows the same division. During set-up,
Cases~D to~H use the point-shifted \mbox{\(6\times6\)} impedance, so that the
normal press can generate the additional alignment moment, while the
implemented grinding phase returns to the decoupled impedance branch described
in \mbox{\Cref{subsec:implementation_grinding_phase}}, removing this coupling
for the subsequent sustained-contact task. That arrangement is a consistency of
the implemented architecture and not an experimental result: the reported runs
ended at the pre-grinding gate, so no displacement was measured during
sustained contact.

surface-fixed definition gives only \mbox{\(+0.13\pm0.01^\circ\)}. The
surface-fixed condition is also classified as tilted by TCP height. At zero
commanded orientation offset, the two definitions produce
\mbox{\(-0.06\pm0.01^\circ\)} and \mbox{\(-1.05\pm0.02^\circ\)}. The
coordinate frame in which the compliance-centre displacement is defined is
therefore part of the controller parameter itself, because a tool-fixed lever
rotates with the tool while a surface-fixed lever stays constant in the
surface frame.

A non-zero centre of compliance is therefore not characterised by its
magnitude. The same \mbox{\(40\,\mathrm{mm}\)} carries a direction as well,
and that direction moves relative to the contact geometry as the tool rotates
unless the frame holding it is selected deliberately. Specifying a displacement
of \mbox{\(40\,\mathrm{mm}\)} is consequently not a complete controller
setting, which is a further reason for treating a displaced centre as condition
dependent rather than fixed. Returning the centre to the TCP removes the
direction and the definition frame along with the coupling.

The measurements therefore support the geometric lever-direction rule of
\mbox{\Cref{eq:experimental_general_lever_rule}} over the four tested
directions. They do not establish one lever magnitude that suits every tested
direction. The two diagonal results are also not evidence that the fixed lever
is general: at \mbox{\(\pm45^\circ\)} the lever selected for the
\mbox{\(t_1\)} command still projects onto the direction-selected lever,
so the two agree there. Where that projection vanishes, at the
\mbox{\(t_2\)} direction, the two differ by \mbox{\(6.04^\circ\)}. One
fixed non-zero tangential lever consequently does not reproduce the
direction-selected effect across the tested directions.

Cases~D and~H together settle the point the contact study was arranged to
establish. No tested non-zero tangential centre of compliance can be selected
independently of the initial misalignment: its assisting side reverses with the
sign of the commanded orientation offset, and the direction it must take
follows the required alignment rotation. A displaced centre has to be chosen
from that rotation. The TCP-centred condition, \mbox{\(r_c=0\)}, is the only
condition among those tested that requires no such selection. Within the
investigated task and parameter range it is therefore identified as the
direction-independent fixed centre of compliance in the sense defined in
\mbox{\Cref{ch:intro}}.

Direction independence is not the same property as alignment authority. The
centre that requires no prior knowledge of the misalignment is not the centre
that produces the largest alignment-directed rotation in every condition. About
\mbox{\(t_2\)} the direction-selected \mbox{\(40\,\mathrm{mm}\)} lever
changed the response from \mbox{\(-1.63\)} to \mbox{\(+4.43^\circ\)},
where the TCP-centred condition produced none. The TCP provides the fixed
default condition. A displaced centre provides greater condition-specific
alignment authority where the required rotation is known.

Within a similar contact geometry, the measurements suggest treating the
compliance-centre displacement as the first parameter to select where
additional rotational authority is needed during set-up. The direction
follows from the commanded rotation, the sign is chosen so that the
press-induced moment assists alignment, and the definition frame must be
stated with the coordinates, since Case~E shows that the same nominal
coordinates in two frames give very different responses. The rotational
stiffness about the commanded axis can then be refined, and the perpendicular
translational stiffness treated as a secondary variable for this geometry. No
magnitude carries forward as a setting for sustained contact. While the press
continues, a fixed tangential \mbox{\(r_c\)} keeps commanding rotation in one
direction, so the TCP-centred condition is the one to return to once alignment
no longer needs assistance. This ordering is a practical interpretation of the
present measurements rather than a general design rule, and the scheduling it
implies was not implemented or measured.

Across the \mbox{\(57\)} tested settings, \mbox{\(50\)} satisfied the
TCP-height flatness criterion; the seven that did not are listed in
\mbox{\Cref{app:additional_plots}}.

\section{Null-Space Pose-Hold Results}
\label{sec:results_nullspace}

The null-space pose-hold study is separate from the contact cases. Matched
trials compare the selectable null-space terms under the same internally
commanded point-force-equivalent disturbance. The commanded disturbance
reached \mbox{\(20.00\,\mathrm{N}\)} in all twelve trials, with peak equivalent
joint-torque norms between \mbox{\(2.17\)} and
\mbox{\(2.45\,\mathrm{N\,m}\)}. The torque-limit scale remained equal to one,
so the waveform was applied without clipping.

\begin{figure}[H]
\centering
\includegraphics[width=0.96\textwidth]{figures/MAIN_NS_nullspace_automatic.pdf}
\caption[Null-space pose-hold response]{Null-space response during the
Cartesian pose-hold disturbance test. (a) Mean cumulative projected null-space
motion \(E_N\) without null-space torque and with projected damping, the
shaded bands showing \(\pm\) one standard deviation across the three
repetitions. (b) Change in the minimum Jacobian singular value over the
disturbance interval, \(\Delta\sigma_{\min,\mathrm{dist}}\), together with the
peak Cartesian position error, for the two singular-value conditioning
magnitudes; error bars show \(\pm\) one standard deviation and the dashed line
marks the \(2\,\mathrm{mm}\) task-retention limit.}
\label{fig:results_nullspace_automatic}
\end{figure}

\Cref{fig:results_nullspace_automatic} shows the cumulative projected
null-space motion for the settings without conditioning torque, with the
spread over the three repetitions shaded, and the disturbance-interval
singular-value change beside the peak Cartesian position error for the two
conditioning magnitudes. The values for all four settings are given below. Projected null-space damping
reduced \mbox{\(E_N\)} from \mbox{\((7.596\pm0.916)^\circ\)} without
null-space torque to \mbox{\((5.687\pm0.228)^\circ\)} at
\mbox{\(d_{\mathrm{null}}=2\,\mathrm{N\,m\,s/rad}\)}, a reduction of
approximately \mbox{\(25\,\%\)}. The net redundant displacement fell in the
same proportion, from \mbox{\(0.131\pm0.016\,\mathrm{rad}\)} to
\mbox{\(0.098\pm0.004\,\mathrm{rad}\)}, and the change in
\mbox{\(\sigma_{\min}\)} across the disturbance interval became less
negative, from \mbox{\((-2.13\pm0.47)\times10^{-3}\)} to
\mbox{\((-1.26\pm0.10)\times10^{-3}\)}. Damping acts on the projected joint
velocity through \mbox{\(\tau_d=-d_{\mathrm{null}}N_\tau\dot q\)}, so it
vanishes as the redundant motion stops and requests no return towards the
initial configuration.

Singular-value conditioning behaved differently from both the uncontrolled and
the damping-only settings. It was already active during the
\mbox{\(5\,\mathrm{s}\)} preceding the disturbance and remained active
throughout it, so it did not act as a term applied after the redundant
configuration had been displaced. Both sigma-only settings ended the
disturbance interval with a net redundant displacement whose magnitude was
smaller than the scatter across the three repetitions:
\mbox{\((0.3\pm0.3)\times10^{-3}\,\mathrm{rad}\)} at
\mbox{\(k_\sigma=1.5\,\mathrm{N\,m}\)} and
\mbox{\((-0.1\pm0.2)\times10^{-3}\,\mathrm{rad}\)} at
\mbox{\(2.0\,\mathrm{N\,m}\)}. Both are smaller than the
\mbox{\(0.131\,\mathrm{rad}\)} measured without null-space torque by more
than two orders of magnitude. Over the same interval
\mbox{\(\sigma_{\min}\)} changed by
\mbox{\((1.95\pm0.07)\times10^{-5}\)} and
\mbox{\((1.93\pm0.03)\times10^{-5}\)}, where it fell by
\mbox{\((-2.13\pm0.47)\times10^{-3}\)} without null-space torque. Under the
tested disturbance direction, the conditioning term therefore acted by
preventing substantial redundant displacement rather than by restoring the
configuration after the disturbance had been removed.

The two sigma magnitudes differed in motion activity rather than in net
displacement. At \mbox{\(k_\sigma=2.0\,\mathrm{N\,m}\)} the cumulative
projected null-space motion was \mbox{\((1.619\pm0.130)^\circ\)}, against
\mbox{\((0.283\pm0.007)^\circ\)} at \mbox{\(1.5\,\mathrm{N\,m}\)}, a
factor of about six, while both net displacements stayed near zero.
The larger torque magnitude was associated with greater switching activity near
the locally preferred region. This is consistent with the form of the law: the
conditioning term commands a fixed torque magnitude along the selected
direction, and the selector \mbox{\(s_\sigma\)} is set to zero only inside
the comparison deadband. On that reading the larger cumulative motion is
switching activity around the same region rather than poorer disturbance
rejection. At \mbox{\(1.5\,\mathrm{N\,m}\)} the mean
peak Cartesian position error was also lower,
\mbox{\(0.889\pm0.024\,\mathrm{mm}\)} against
\mbox{\(0.983\pm0.053\,\mathrm{mm}\)}. The largest position error measured
in the complete pose-hold study was \mbox{\(1.304\,\mathrm{mm}\)}, below
the predefined \mbox{\(2\,\mathrm{mm}\)} acceptance limit. Of the two
tested magnitudes, \mbox{\(1.5\,\mathrm{N\,m}\)} achieved the same
suppression of net disturbance-induced displacement with substantially less
redundant motion.

The pose-hold trials compare the four selectable null-space modes under the
repeatable commanded disturbance. The two terms act on different quantities.
Projected damping dissipates redundant joint motion that is already present,
whereas the singular-value term drives the redundant configuration towards the
sampled neighbour with the larger \mbox{\(\sigma_{\min}\)} whether or not
such motion is present. The conditioning law is not dissipative: a disturbance
acting towards larger \mbox{\(\sigma_{\min}\)} would move the
configuration in the same direction as \mbox{\(\tau_\sigma\)} rather than
against it, so the rejection observed here is specific to the tested
disturbance direction. Both terms act through the null-space projector, so the
Cartesian task stays primary.

Several bounds apply to these results. Because the conditioning torque was
active before the disturbance, the sigma-only trials entered it from a redundant
configuration displaced by approximately
\mbox{\(0.013\,\mathrm{rad}\)} from the one at which the trials without
conditioning began. The four settings therefore compare the closed-loop
behaviour of the complete modes. They do not isolate an instantaneous opposing
torque at identical joint configurations. The surface-contact experiments use
both contributions together in the combined secondary-torque mode and therefore
do not separate their individual effects during contact. The geometric Jacobian
combines translational and rotational rows without a common length scale, so
the reported \mbox{\(\sigma_{\min}\)} is used only as a relative indicator
under the fixed Jacobian convention.
