\section{Overview of Tested Gain Settings}
\label{sec:results_contact}

This section reports the surface-contact experiment for the candidate stiffness
settings of Table~\ref{tab:example_candidate_stiffness_sets}. The values given
below are \emph{illustrative expectations} derived from the contact physics:
they show the trends and the order of magnitude anticipated for each setting,
and they are replaced by the measured values once the logged trials are
available. The figures are \emph{qualitative} sketches of the corresponding
expected signals, not plots of measured data.

\section{Passive Orientation Alignment}
\label{sec:results_alignment}

The decisive variable is the rotational stiffness about the alignment axes.
With high rotational stiffness (Test~1), the controller holds the commanded
orientation and resists the contact-induced rotation, so the contact moment is
largely reacted as joint torque and the tool barely rotates. The residual
misalignment stays close to the initial value, \(\Delta e_R\approx0\), and the
peak contact force at the leading edge is high. As the rotational stiffness is
reduced (Tests~2 and~3), the moment produced by the downward force at the tool
edge increasingly rotates the tool toward the surface, so \(\Delta e_R\) becomes
positive and the orientation error decreases while the peak contact force drops.
Adding lateral compliance (Test~4) lets the tool both slide and rotate, which
is expected to give the most complete passive alignment. With an over-soft
setting (Test~5), the displacement becomes large and the alignment motion
poorly defined, so this setting is expected to be rejected on the safety and
displacement criteria.

\section{Instantaneous Pole and Virtual-Center Consistency}
\label{sec:results_pole}

The instantaneous pole of the measured motion
(\cref{eq:instantaneous_pole_displacement}) indicates where the passive motion
appears to pivot. With stiff rotation, the small rotation occurs essentially
about the tool-frame origin, so the pole lies far from the contact edge and the
pole error \(e_P\) is large. With compliant rotation and added lateral
compliance, the pole is expected to migrate toward the contact edge and the
surface, reducing \(e_P\) and matching the useful passive pivot near the
contact. This is the geometric signature of good passive self-alignment.

\section{Contact Displacement, Force, and Commanded Torque}
\label{sec:results_force_torque}

The normal penetration scales approximately as
\(\Delta x_n\approx \|f_C\|/K_n\), so a stiffer normal direction gives a smaller
displacement but a larger peak contact force, while a more compliant setting
gives the opposite. The peak commanded joint torque grows with the commanded
wrench through \(\boldsymbol{\tau}=J^\top\mathbf{F}\), so the stiffer settings
produce the largest torque peaks. The logged commanded torques are therefore
checked to ensure that the selected gains do not produce abrupt or excessive
commands during the contact transient.

\section{Effect of Damping}
\label{sec:results_damping}

With the damping initialized from the critical-damping approximation, the
contact response is expected to settle without sustained oscillation. An
under-damped setting would show oscillation in the orientation error and the
contact force after the tool touches or leaves the surface; the selected damping
values are checked against this on the logged signals.

\section{Discussion}
\label{sec:discussion_contact}

\subsection{Comparison with Expected Passive Alignment Behaviour}

The expected trade-off is the same physical trade-off that appears throughout
Cartesian impedance control. Higher stiffness improves pose regulation but
increases contact forces and commanded torques. Lower stiffness improves
compliance but can allow excessive displacement and may make the alignment
motion poorly defined. For the surface-contact task, the important design
question is not simply whether the robot is soft or stiff. The important
question is where the compliance appears to be located geometrically.

\subsection{Practical Implications for Controller Tuning}

A purely diagonal stiffness matrix can make the robot compliant, but it does
not directly encode a useful virtual center of rotation. In that case, the
contact wrench may still produce useful alignment because the contact force
acts at the tool edge and because the surface constrains the motion. However,
the effective pivot point must then be evaluated from the measured motion
rather than assumed from the commanded stiffness matrix. This is why the
instantaneous pole or screw-axis evaluation is important in the direct
experimental comparison. This interpretation is consistent with the assembly
literature, where remote or variable compliance centers are useful precisely
because they turn contact forces into correcting motion instead of jamming
\citep{Nevins1989ComputerControlledAssembly,Watson1978RCC,Wang2019VariableComplianceCenter,Hartisch2022FlexureRCC}.

\subsection{Limitations of the Current Qualitative Results}

The numerical values in this chapter are still qualitative expectations because
the final logged contact data are not available yet. They are used to show the
physically expected trends, the required plots, and the interpretation method.
The final validation requires measured repetitions with the same candidate
gains, the logged pose and wrench signals, and the fitted effective stiffness
and damping matrices.

\subsection{Practical Implications of Coupled Gain Design}

A fully coupled stiffness matrix based on a selected center point would encode
the virtual-center behavior more directly. The least-squares identification in
Section~\ref{sec:effective_stiffness_damping_identification} is therefore used
to analyze the effective coupling that appears during contact, even when the
commanded controller gains are first computed from quasi-static contact
limits and then validated experimentally. For the present thesis, the final
stiffness selection should satisfy four practical requirements. It should
reduce the orientation error during contact. It should produce an
instantaneous pole near the useful contact region. It must be damped enough to
avoid oscillation. Finally, it must remain compatible with the Panda torque
limits and the discrete real-time implementation.

\section{Decoupled versus Coupled Wrench Command}
\label{sec:coupled_command_experiment}

The coupled gain design discussed in the previous section was implemented and
tested directly, using the pole-shifted stiffness of
\cref{eq:coupled_tcp_stiffness}. During the post-contact alignment phase the
controller can command either the decoupled wrench of
\cref{eq:cartesian_force_moment_vector} or the coupled wrench
\(w = K_{\mathrm{TCP}}\,e + D_{\mathrm{TCP}}\,\dot{e}\), with the pole placed at a
chosen offset from the contact edge along the surface-normal direction. All runs
start from the same fixed joint configuration and use the same downward contact
search and the same \(7\,\mathrm{s}\) alignment phase, so that only the command
and the pole differ. They are compared in
\cref{tab:coupled_command_campaign} by the final tip angle (the rotation away
from the orientation held at first contact), the steady contact force, the
travel of the contact edge, and the distance of the measured screw axis from the
edge, where a smaller distance indicates a cleaner rotation about the contact
edge.

As a consistency check, feeding the decoupled gains through the coupled command
path with the pole at the TCP reproduced the decoupled baseline
(\(8.6^\circ\) tip at the same force), which confirms that the differences
observed for other poles are a genuine effect of the coupling and not an
implementation artefact.

The runs can be read as follows. The decoupled baseline tipped the tool by
\(9.0^\circ\) at a steady \(106\,\mathrm{N}\). Placing the pole on the contact
edge almost completely suppressed the tip (\(0.6^\circ\)): the off-diagonal block
\([r_c]_\times^\top K_p\) converts the normal press into an orientation-holding
moment, so the tool stays rigid about the edge and only slides tangentially.
Moving the pole to the opposite side of the TCP reverses the sign of the
coupling, so that it now assists the tip; the tool then reached
\(11.2^\circ\), exceeding the baseline while using less contact force
(\(79\,\mathrm{N}\)) and producing less edge travel and a screw axis about twice
as close to the contact edge. Increasing the offset further reduced the tip
again (\(9.7^\circ\)), because the quadratic term
\([r_c]_\times^\top K_p\,[r_c]_\times\) in \cref{eq:coupled_tcp_stiffness} grows
with the square of the lever and re-stiffens the rotation; the assist therefore
has a clear optimum. Increasing the translational push raised the contact force
to \(119\,\mathrm{N}\) but did not increase the tip, which shows that the
equilibrium is limited by the geometry of the spring and not by the available
press. Recomputing the pole from the moving edge each cycle gave essentially the
same equilibrium and remained stable, so a constant pole pinned at first contact
is preferred for its predictability.

These results confirm the central distinction between the two commands. A
coupled, pole-based wrench is not equivalent to the decoupled wrench unless the
pole lies at the TCP, and the pole position alone decides whether the coupling
resists or assists the passive alignment. With an assisting pole on the far
side of the contact edge, the coupled command improves the alignment over the
decoupled baseline: a larger tip at lower contact force, less edge sliding, and a
rotation axis closer to the physical contact edge. All single-pole variants
nevertheless settle at an equilibrium short of full flatness, because the
contact moment that drives the rotation decays as the tool tips and the edge
stops digging in. This limit is intrinsic to passive compliance and is shared by
the decoupled baseline; neither increased push nor live pole tracking removes
it. Reaching full flatness would require an actively driven, time-varying pole
or a commanded rotation, which lies outside the passive scope considered here.

\subsection{Commanded Pole versus Achieved Rotation Axis}
\label{subsec:pole_vs_axis}

A further observation concerns the relation between the \emph{commanded} pole
and the axis the tool actually rotates about. In free motion the centre of
compliance is also the centre of rotation, but under a rigid contact the two
separate. In one run the pole was commanded about \(40\,\mathrm{mm}\) to one side
of the end-effector origin, yet the measured finite screw axis lay about
\(50\,\mathrm{mm}\) to the opposite side, close to the contact edge; the two were
roughly \(90\,\mathrm{mm}\) apart, on opposite sides of the tool tip. The reason
is that the pressed edge acts as a kinematic hinge: the surface pins the edge, so
the tool can only pivot about the contact region irrespective of where the
compliance is centred. Shifting the pole therefore does not relocate the
rotation axis; it changes the restoring moment applied about the pinned edge,
which is precisely why a far-side pole increased the tip
(\cref{tab:coupled_command_campaign}) without moving the measured axis to the
far side. Concisely, the pole sets the \emph{effort} while the contact sets the
\emph{axis}. This decoupling, induced by the contact constraint, is the reason
the commanded pole and the measured finite (Chasles) axis do not coincide and
should not be expected to.
