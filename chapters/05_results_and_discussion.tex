\chapter{Results and Discussion}
\label{ch:results_discussion}
\Revised{This chapter presents the measured response of the surface-contact tests
and complementary Cartesian pose-hold trials. The eight contact cases introduced in
\mbox{\cref{tab:experimental_test_cases}} are compared using the signed set-up
rotation about the commanded axis. TCP-height flatness, the EE-inferred
alignment angle, displacement, and model-estimated external load are reported
as separate supporting quantities. The presentation follows the experimental
logic from the zero-lever baseline through lever and stiffness variations to
the secondary null-space study.}

\section{Data Quality and Evaluation Basis}
\label{sec:results_data_quality}
\label{sec:results_repeatability}
\label{sec:results_alignment}

\Revised{The archive contains \mbox{\(171\)} contact-run directories, of which
\mbox{\(170\)} contain analysable measurements. The incomplete directory
\mbox{\texttt{P2\_t1\_pos\_m040/r02}} contains neither a CSV log nor a set-up
report. The corresponding Case-E value is therefore based on two repetitions;
all other settings use three. No completed run was excluded.}

\Revised{The primary controller-response metric is the signed component of
\mbox{\(\Delta\theta_{\mathrm{set}}^S\)} about the commanded rotation axis.
It is calculated between the clearance transition and the end of set-up from
the measured end-effector poses. A positive value follows the intended
alignment direction. The same interval and definition are used for every
contact case.}

\Revised{Repeatability was assessed from the usable repetitions of each contact
setting. The median within-setting standard deviation of the signed set-up
rotation was approximately \mbox{\(0.04^\circ\)} about \mbox{\(t_1\)} and
\mbox{\(0.14^\circ\)} about \mbox{\(t_2\)}. Differences smaller than approximately
\mbox{\(0.1^\circ\)} are therefore treated as being of the same order as the
observed experimental scatter. This value is used as a descriptive comparison
scale rather than as a statistical significance limit.}

\Revised{The scatter is not uniform across the conditions. It is largest where
the contact is near the transition between correcting and not correcting, and
reaches \mbox{\(0.42^\circ\)} about \mbox{\(t_1\)} and
\mbox{\(2.35^\circ\)} about \mbox{\(t_2\)} at single positions of Case~E.
Those settings are named where they are reported, and the comparison scale
does not apply to them.}

\Revised{The reported values were calculated from settled intervals. During the final
second of set-up, the median change in the model-estimated normal load was
\mbox{\(0.10\,\mathrm{N}\)}, and the median change in the alignment angle
\mbox{\(\theta_{\mathrm{align}}\)} was
\mbox{\(0.00^\circ\)}. The largest corresponding changes were
\mbox{\(1.10\,\mathrm{N}\)} and \mbox{\(0.60^\circ\)}. For settings that kept the zero
compliance-centre lever \mbox{\(r_c\)}, the steady model-estimated normal load
\mbox{\(F_n\)} remained between \mbox{\(76\)} and
\mbox{\(85\,\mathrm{N}\)}. The contact comparisons were
therefore made under similar estimated preload levels, except where the lever
position itself altered the contact response.}

\Revised{The alignment angle \mbox{\(\theta_{\mathrm{align}}\)} at the beginning
of set-up differed from the commanded angular offset. The orientation phase
used \mbox{\(180\,\mathrm{N\,m/rad}\)} about each surface tangent and ended
after the minimum duration once the tool-axis and spin errors satisfied their
configured tolerances. Finite tracking error and the distinction between the
commanded rotation and the EE-inferred alignment angle therefore remain in the
initial values.}

\Revised{The difference is neither uniform nor always a deficit. A commanded
\mbox{\(+10^\circ\)} about \mbox{\(t_1\)} arrived as
\mbox{\(9.34\pm0.02^\circ\)} and the reversed one as
\mbox{\(9.37\pm0.07^\circ\)}, both short of the command, whereas a commanded
\mbox{\(+10^\circ\)} about \mbox{\(t_2\)} arrived as
\mbox{\(10.04\pm0.03^\circ\)} and overshot it. At half the command the same
pattern holds, \mbox{\(4.29^\circ\)} about \mbox{\(t_1\)} against
\mbox{\(5.19^\circ\)} about \mbox{\(t_2\)}. What the two tangents share is the
scatter, at most \mbox{\(0.07^\circ\)} within a group, so every condition of a
comparison starts from the same attitude even where that attitude is not the
commanded one.}

\Revised{Figure legends therefore identify each series by the measured
EE-inferred orientation at the beginning of set-up. The commanded offset names
the condition because it is the controlled setting, but it is not substituted
for the measured initial value.}

set-up phase for two compliance-centre positions. The deviation holds near its
initial value while the press builds, falls as the tool rolls onto the surface,
and settles on the residual the condition reaches. Each curve is one trial.}

\subsection{The Three Reported Quantities}
\label{sec:results_quantities}

\Revised{Three quantities describe each run, and they answer different
questions. Treating them as interchangeable would confuse gripper attitude,
robot rotation, and the geometry-based flatness classification.}

\Revised{The \emph{angular deviation} \mbox{\(\theta_{\mathrm{align}}\)} is the
angle between the tool normal \mbox{\(n_T\)} and the calibrated surface normal
\mbox{\(n_s\)}. It has an absolute zero, but \mbox{\(n_T\)} is not measured
directly: it is reconstructed from the
end-effector orientation and a calibrated direction in that frame,
\mbox{\(n_T=R_{\mathrm{EE}}n_{\mathrm{EE}}\)}. The pads that clamp the tool to
the gripper carry play, so the tool can turn relative to the frame the
reconstruction rests on. What this quantity therefore reports is the attitude
of the gripper.}

\Revised{The \emph{set-up rotation} \mbox{\(\Delta\theta_{\mathrm{set}}\)} is
the rotation since the clearance transition at the start of set-up, resolved
on the surface axes. The finite rotation comes from the joint angles and
forward kinematics; the calibrated surface frame enters only when its
components are resolved about \mbox{\(t_1,t_2,n_s\)}. The tool-axis calibration and
relative tool motion within the gripper do not enter it. It has no absolute
flat-tool zero, so it says how far the robot turned and not where the tool
ended up. This is the quantity that describes what the controller did.}

\Revised{The \emph{TCP height above the plane} provides a geometry-based
flatness classification. A
tool lying flat puts its grinding face on the surface, so the TCP stands one
face offset above it; a tool resting on one edge stands higher, by the half
dimension it is tilted over times the sine of the tilt. The face is
\mbox{\(60\,\mathrm{mm}\)} half-length across \mbox{\(t_1\)} and
\mbox{\(20\,\mathrm{mm}\)} half-width across \mbox{\(t_2\)}, so a
\mbox{\(10^\circ\)} tilt lifts the TCP by \mbox{\(10.4\)} and
\mbox{\(3.5\,\mathrm{mm}\)} respectively. Only the TCP position and the plane
enter it. The classification therefore does not depend on the reconstructed
tool direction, but it assumes the calibrated face offset and edge-contact
geometry.}

\Revised{Measured that way, the tool was classified as flat in \mbox{\(50\)} of the
\mbox{\(57\)} reported settings. The height sat within
\mbox{\(2\,\mathrm{mm}\)} of the flat-tool value in every one of them and
\mbox{\(3.5\)} to \mbox{\(9.3\,\mathrm{mm}\)} above it in the seven that did not,
with nothing in between, so the classification does not rest on where a
threshold is placed.}

\Revised{The pattern is the whole of the difference between the two tangents.
About \mbox{\(t_1\)} the TCP-height metric classifies the tool as tilted whenever
the rotational stiffness is raised, the compliance centre is placed on the
opposing side, or the offset is named in the surface frame. About
\mbox{\(t_2\)} every tested setting is classified as flat, including those
whose deviation grew by several degrees.}

\Revised{The discrepancy implies relative motion between the tool and gripper
during contact. A commanded offset about \mbox{\(t_2\)} acts near the axis for
which unloaded mount clearance was observed, so mount compliance is one
plausible contributor. The arithmetic closes: for every condition the
EE-inferred deviation after set-up equals the value at set-up entry plus the
corresponding set-up rotation, to within \mbox{\(0.1^\circ\)}. This agreement is
internal to the end-effector-based quantities and is not a direct measurement
of tool rotation.}

\Revised{Under the flatness classification, the implied relative orientation
is the deviation that remains. About \mbox{\(t_1\)} it is \mbox{\(1.1\)} to \mbox{\(1.8^\circ\)} in
the conditions that end flat, which matches the clearance the mount was
expected to carry. About \mbox{\(t_2\)} it reaches \mbox{\(8.5\)} to
\mbox{\(11.9^\circ\)} in the conditions where the robot does not turn. A
unloaded clearance alone does not explain that range. The mount was not
characterised under load, so neither the exact relative motion nor its cause is
established independently.}

\Revised{What follows therefore reports the set-up rotation as the response of
the controller, the flatness as the geometry-based physical classification,
and the deviation as the EE-inferred gripper attitude. The first quantity is
used for every parameter comparison; the other two are kept distinct even
when they indicate the same trend.}


\section{Baseline Contact Response (Case D)}
\label{sec:results_case_d}

\Revised{Case~D places the centre of compliance on the TCP, so the commanded
press makes no moment through a lever and what acts is the contact and the
finite rotational stiffness. The commanded offset is
\mbox{\(10^\circ\)}, applied about each surface tangent and in both signs, and
nothing else differs between those four conditions. A fifth commands no offset
at all.}

\Revised{That fifth condition says what the deviation reads when nothing is
asked of the tool. The gripper arrives at \mbox{\(0.74^\circ\)} and the

\mbox{\(1.56\pm0.01^\circ\)} while the TCP-height criterion classifies the

nothing, which is the clearest single statement of what it measures.}

\Revised{The set-up rotation reverses with the sign of the commanded offset.

\mbox{\(+4.43^\circ\)} for the positive command and
\mbox{\(-10.51^\circ\)} for the negative command. The signed commanded
rotation direction therefore determines the assisting lever direction.}

\Revised{The two tangents differ in what the wrong direction costs, and the
flatness separates them. About \mbox{\(t_1\)} the opposing direction leaves
the tool tilted: at \mbox{\(-20\)} and \mbox{\(-40\,\mathrm{mm}\)} the
TCP stands \mbox{\(5.3\)} and \mbox{\(6.0\,\mathrm{mm}\)} above the
flat-tool height, and at \mbox{\(+20\)} and \mbox{\(+40\,\mathrm{mm}\)}
of the reversed offset \mbox{\(5.2\)} and \mbox{\(9.3\,\mathrm{mm}\)}.
These are the only conditions of the case in which the tool does not reach the
surface. About \mbox{\(t_2\)} it ends flat at every position, so what the
opposing direction costs there is not the flatness but the share of the
rotation the robot performs.}

\Revised{The sweep about \mbox{\(t_2\)}
runs from \mbox{\(-4.51\)} at \mbox{\(-40\,\mathrm{mm}\)} through
\mbox{\(-1.63\)} at the TCP to \mbox{\(+4.43^\circ\)} at
\mbox{\(+40\,\mathrm{mm}\)}. The assisting lever is therefore necessary to
produce helpful robot rotation about \mbox{\(t_2\)}, but the tested
\mbox{\(40\,\mathrm{mm}\)} length does not remove the full initial
EE-inferred deviation of approximately \mbox{\(10^\circ\)}. The grinding face
is nevertheless classified as flat from TCP height, implying relative motion
within the tool--gripper assembly that was not measured directly.}

\Revised{What the offset achieves also differs between the tangents. About
\mbox{\(t_1\)} the assisting side is level: from \mbox{\(-10\)} to
\mbox{\(+40\,\mathrm{mm}\)} the rotation stays between \mbox{\(+7.45\)}
and \mbox{\(+7.87^\circ\)}, against \mbox{\(+7.57^\circ\)} with the
centre on the TCP. Displacing the centre adds nothing there that the contact
was not already delivering through the robot. About \mbox{\(t_2\)} it is what
makes the robot move at all, carrying the rotation from \mbox{\(-1.63\)} to
\mbox{\(+4.43^\circ\)} at the positive offset and from \mbox{\(-1.25\)}
to \mbox{\(-10.51^\circ\)} at the negative one.}

\Revised{The transition between the two regimes is not gradual. About
\mbox{\(t_1\)} at a positive offset the change collapses from
\mbox{\(+5.69\)} to \mbox{\(+0.70^\circ\)} between \mbox{\(-10\)} and
\mbox{\(-20\,\mathrm{mm}\)}, and the three repetitions at
\mbox{\(-20\,\mathrm{mm}\)} scatter by \mbox{\(0.42^\circ\)} against a median
within-setting scatter of \mbox{\(0.05^\circ\)} elsewhere. The
\mbox{\(-40\,\mathrm{mm}\)} position of the positive \mbox{\(t_2\)} condition
scatters by \mbox{\(2.35^\circ\)} for the same reason. Near the transition the
contact resolves one way or the other from nominally identical starts.}

\section{Null-Space Pose-Hold Results}
\label{sec:results_nullspace}

\Revised{Matched Cartesian pose-hold trials evaluated the null-space terms under a
repeatable internally commanded disturbance. The point-force equivalent reached
\mbox{\(20.00\,\mathrm{N}\)} in all twelve trials. Its peak equivalent joint-torque
norm lay between \mbox{\(2.17\)} and \mbox{\(2.45\,\mathrm{N\,m}\)}. The torque-limit scale
remained equal to one, so the requested waveform was applied without clipping.
All trials completed and were included in the analysis.}

\Revised{Projected null-space damping reduced the cumulative redundant joint excursion
from \mbox{\((7.596\pm0.916)^\circ\)} without null-space torque
\mbox{\(\tau_{\mathrm{null}}\)} to
\mbox{\((5.687\pm0.228)^\circ\)} at
\mbox{\(2\,\mathrm{N\,m\,s/rad}\)}. This corresponds to a reduction of approximately
\mbox{\(25\,\%\)} relative to the zero-torque condition. The final change in the
minimum singular value \mbox{\(\sigma_{\min}\)} also became less negative, from
\mbox{\((-2.13\pm0.47)\times10^{-3}\)} to
\mbox{\((-1.26\pm0.10)\times10^{-3}\)}.}

\Revised{In both sigma-only settings, the minimum singular value
\mbox{\(\sigma_{\min}\)} was approximately at
its initial level by the end of the trial. Its final change was
\mbox{\((1.95\pm0.07)\times10^{-5}\)} at
\mbox{\(k_\sigma=1.5\,\mathrm{N\,m}\)} and
\mbox{\((1.93\pm0.03)\times10^{-5}\)} at \mbox{\(2\,\mathrm{N\,m}\)}. Both commands
therefore achieved the same final singular-value recovery. The
\mbox{\(1.5\,\mathrm{N\,m}\)} setting did so with lower cumulative excursion,
\mbox{\((0.283\pm0.007)^\circ\)} rather than \mbox{\((1.619\pm0.130)^\circ\)}, and a
lower mean peak Cartesian position error \mbox{\(e_p\)},
\mbox{\(0.889\pm0.024\,\mathrm{mm}\)} rather
than \mbox{\(0.983\pm0.053\,\mathrm{mm}\)}. The largest position error \mbox{\(e_p\)} measured in
the complete pose-hold study was \mbox{\(1.304\,\mathrm{mm}\)}, below the predefined
\mbox{\(2\,\mathrm{mm}\)} acceptance limit.}

\Revised{The geometric Jacobian combines translational and rotational rows
without a common length scale. The reported
\mbox{\(\sigma_{\min}\)} is therefore a relative indicator under this fixed
Jacobian convention, not a unit-invariant manipulability measure.}

\section{Discussion}
\label{sec:discussion_contact}

\subsection{Relative Influence of the Varied Parameters}
\label{sec:discussion_parameter_influence}
\label{sec:discussion_pole_height}

\Revised{The measured effect sizes differed substantially across the varied
parameter families. Increasing the command-axis rotational stiffness from
\mbox{\(5\)} to \mbox{\(50\,\mathrm{N\,m/rad}\)} reduced the signed set-up
rotation from \mbox{\(+7.57\)} to \mbox{\(+2.73^\circ\)} about \mbox{\(t_1\)}.
About \mbox{\(t_2\)}, it changed from \mbox{\(-1.63\)} to
\mbox{\(-0.48^\circ\)}. Reducing the perpendicular translational stiffness
from \mbox{\(2000\)} to \mbox{\(300\,\mathrm{N/m}\)} changed the rotation by
only \mbox{\(0.02^\circ\)} about \mbox{\(t_1\)} and \mbox{\(-0.61^\circ\)}
about \mbox{\(t_2\)}.}

\Revised{The tangential compliance-centre position produced the largest
within-series changes. For the positive \mbox{\(t_1\)} command, the two outer
positions differ by \mbox{\(7.73^\circ\)}; for the positive \mbox{\(t_2\)}
command, they differ by \mbox{\(8.94^\circ\)}. These spans exceed the changes
caused by either stiffness family over its tested range.}

\Revised{Both spans were measured under the same press and at the same
commanded offset, so the comparison is controlled. Within the tested
configuration and parameter range the compliance-centre position therefore had
the stronger influence.}

\Revised{The measurements support considering the compliance-centre lever
\mbox{\(r_c\)} before
refining the stiffness entries. The rotational stiffness controls the angular
resistance of the impedance, and the perpendicular translational stiffness can
modify the response for particular directions. The lever, however, directly
determines whether the normal press produces a moment that assists or opposes
the required rotation.}

\subsection{Role of the Compliance-Centre Lever}
\label{sec:discussion_compliance_lever}
\label{sec:discussion_placement}

\Revised{The sign dependence observed in Case~E follows from the point-shifted
impedance. With the press directed approximately along the negative surface
normal \mbox{\(n_s\)}, \mbox{\(f_{\mathrm{press}}\approx-F_n n_s\)}, the coupling moment is}

\begin{equation}
    m_c=-r_c\times f_{\mathrm{press}}.
\end{equation}

\Revised{The tangential lever \mbox{\(r_{c,t}\)} follows the rule derived in
\mbox{\cref{eq:general_compliance_lever_selection}}, perpendicular to the
commanded rotation axis within the tangent plane:}

\begin{equation}
r_{c,t}=-\rho_c t_2
\quad\text{about } t_1,
\qquad
r_{c,t}=+\rho_c t_1
\quad\text{about } t_2,
\qquad \rho_c>0.
\label{eq:discussion_general_lever_selection}
\end{equation}

\Revised{With \mbox{\(f_{\mathrm{press}}\approx-F_n n_s\)}, this selection gives}

\begin{equation}
m_c\approx-F_n\rho_c t_1
\quad\text{about } t_1,
\qquad
m_c\approx-F_n\rho_c t_2
\quad\text{about } t_2.
\label{eq:discussion_general_lever_moment}
\end{equation}

\Revised{The induced moment is therefore opposite to the commanded
rotation in both cases. The lever lies along
the tangent perpendicular to the commanded rotation axis. Reversing
the orientation offset reverses the commanded rotation axis and the selected
lever with it. Case~E
tested this vector rule on both surface tangents and at both signs of the
commanded offset, and Case~C four directions in the tangent plane.}

\Revised{A lever component along the tangent perpendicular to the commanded
rotation axis therefore
creates a moment about that axis. Reversing the lever reverses the moment. One
sign assists the rotation that brings the grinding face towards the surface,
whereas the opposite sign opposes it. Exchanging the roles of \mbox{\(t_1\)} and
\mbox{\(t_2\)} changes the cross-product direction, which is consistent with the
assisting lever signs being opposite for the two tilted axes.}

\Revised{The component of \mbox{\(r_c\)} parallel to the press direction contributes no moment
because its cross product with \mbox{\(f_{\mathrm{press}}\)} is zero. A centre displaced along
the tool axis is close to that case, because the tool stands nearly normal to
the surface. At a \mbox{\(10^\circ\)} physical inclination, a
\mbox{\(40\,\mathrm{mm}\)} axial displacement has a tangential projection of
approximately \mbox{\(6.9\,\mathrm{mm}\)}, far below the direct
\mbox{\(40\,\mathrm{mm}\)} tangential lever.}

\Revised{Case~G accordingly changes the signed set-up rotation by only
\mbox{\(0.34^\circ\)} across the full \mbox{\(-40\)} to
\mbox{\(+40\,\mathrm{mm}\)} tool-axis range. The variation can arise from
tangential force components, finite rotation, evolving contact geometry, or
mount compliance. The experiment does not isolate these contributions.}

\Revised{For an axial displacement \mbox{\(d\)} and a physical inclination
\mbox{\(\theta\)}, the effective tangential component is
\mbox{\(d\sin\theta\)}. At \mbox{\(10^\circ\)}, approximately
\mbox{\(230\,\mathrm{mm}\)} of axial displacement would be needed to match a
\mbox{\(40\,\mathrm{mm}\)} tangential component. This first-order comparison
explains why the tested axial placement had a much smaller effect.}

\Revised{The lower model-estimated normal loads \mbox{\(F_n\)} at the assisting lever positions are
consistent with the same mechanism. A larger alignment motion allows part of
the commanded displacement to be accommodated through rotation, reducing the
remaining translational compression. This explanation remains model-based
because the external load was estimated by the robot model. Relative
tool--gripper rotation remains part of the alignment uncertainty.}

\Revised{The controller regulated pose through Cartesian impedance rather than
controlling the contact force directly. Changing the compliance-centre lever
\mbox{\(r_c\)}
therefore also changed the resulting contact load, so the measured improvement
characterises the complete coupled contact response rather than an isolated
moment effect.}

\Revised{The lever is a commanded setting and does not change sign on its own
during a run. Case~E measured the consequence of its sign directly, and at
both signs of the commanded offset. About \mbox{\(t_1\)} the response moved
between \mbox{\(+7.87\)} and \mbox{\(+0.14^\circ\)} as the centre was
carried from one side of the TCP to the other, and reversing the commanded
offset exchanged the two ends. About \mbox{\(t_2\)} the same exchange spans
\mbox{\(+4.43\)} to \mbox{\(-4.51^\circ\)}. The signed commanded rotation
direction therefore defines the assisting offset direction. For every reported
run, that direction was selected before set-up from the commanded rotation
direction.}

\subsection{What the Contact Contributes Without a Lever}
\label{sec:discussion_contact_alone}

\Revised{Case~D separates the contact from the coupling. With the centre of
compliance at the TCP the commanded press produces no moment, and what remains
is the response of the contact and the finite rotational stiffness. That
response is large about \mbox{\(t_1\)}: the robot turns
\mbox{\(+7.57^\circ\)} for the positive commanded offset and
\mbox{\(-10.83^\circ\)} for the negative offset.}

\Revised{About \mbox{\(t_2\)}, the robot turns only
\mbox{\(-1.63^\circ\)} and \mbox{\(-1.25^\circ\)}. The contact face has
different half-dimensions across the two tangents, and the observed unloaded
mount clearance lies near the \mbox{\(t_2\)} axis. The asymmetry is therefore
consistent with the combined effects of contact geometry, mount compliance,
and frame definition; the experiment does not isolate one cause.}

\Revised{This changes what the compliance centre has to achieve on each axis.
About \mbox{\(t_1\)}, a helpful robot rotation is already present and the
assisting \mbox{\(40\,\mathrm{mm}\)} lever adds only \mbox{\(0.30^\circ\)},
from \mbox{\(+7.57\)} to \mbox{\(+7.87^\circ\)}. About \mbox{\(t_2\)}, the
same lever changes the response from \mbox{\(-1.63\)} to
\mbox{\(+4.43^\circ\)}. A tangential lever is therefore necessary for helpful
robot rotation about \mbox{\(t_2\)}, but the tested length is insufficient to
remove the full initial EE-inferred deviation.}

\subsection{Frame and Magnitude of the Lever}
\label{sec:discussion_lever_frame}

\Revised{Case~F separates the frame the offset is named in from the offset
itself. At \mbox{\(10^\circ\)} about \mbox{\(t_1\)} the two definitions differ by
\mbox{\(7.74^\circ\)} in set-up rotation: \mbox{\(+0.13^\circ\)} for the
surface-frame lever and \mbox{\(+7.87^\circ\)} for the tool-frame lever.
The frame in which the lever is fixed is therefore part of the controller
setting and cannot be omitted from its definition.}

\Revised{The reported campaign bounds the tangential lever at
\mbox{\(40\,\mathrm{mm}\)}. This length adds little to the already helpful
\mbox{\(t_1\)} response, but it is necessary to obtain helpful robot rotation
about \mbox{\(t_2\)}. It still corrects less than half of the approximately
\mbox{\(10^\circ\)} initial EE-inferred deviation for the positive command.
The optimum or sufficient \mbox{\(t_2\)} lever length therefore lies outside
the tested conclusion and must be established with a wider, safety-bounded
sweep.}

\subsection{Implications for Controller Parameter Selection}
\label{sec:discussion_parameter_selection}

\Revised{The selection below is quasi-static. It rests on the balance between
the moment the press produces through the lever and the restoring stiffness
that opposes the rotation, both evaluated at the settled end of set-up, and it
says nothing about the transient between contact and that state. Two things
justify treating the problem this way here. The reported values were read from
settled intervals, over which the load and the angle no longer changed. And the
range over which a setting may be varied is itself bounded statically: at a
tool-axis displacement of \mbox{\(120\,\mathrm{mm}\)} the commanded torque of
the sixth joint reached \mbox{\(21.4\,\mathrm{N\,m}\)} against its limit of
\mbox{\(12\,\mathrm{N\,m}\)} and the run aborted, while across the reported
sweep that joint stayed near \mbox{\(6\,\mathrm{N\,m}\)} and rose to
\mbox{\(15.1\,\mathrm{N\,m}\)} only at the last position. A selection method
for this arrangement therefore has to respect a wrist torque before it
optimises a response.}

\Revised{The measured ordering suggests a practical sequence for selecting controller
parameters within a similar contact geometry. The compliance-centre lever
\mbox{\(r_c\)} is
considered first because it determines the direction and approximate magnitude
of the coupling moment. Its tangential direction should be perpendicular to
the commanded rotation axis, and its sign should be selected so that the induced
moment assists alignment.}

\Revised{After the lever has been selected, the rotational stiffness about the commanded
rotation axis can be refined. Within the tested range, increasing this stiffness reduced
the available alignment motion. The perpendicular translational stiffness can
then be adjusted where the measurements show a directional benefit. In the
present tests, reducing it increased the response about \mbox{\(t_2\)}, while the
\mbox{\(t_1\)} variation remained within the descriptive comparison scale.}

\Revised{This measured sequence provides an initial tuning procedure for the
investigated contact geometry and parameter range.
Cases~C and~E establish the direction and sign rule at the tested conditions,
and Cases~E and~G quantify sensitivity along the tangential and tool-axis
coordinates. On the tangent, the helpful side depends on the sign of the
commanded offset. Along the tool axis, the response varies by only
\mbox{\(0.34^\circ\)} over the tested range and does not justify a unique
preferred position.}

\section{Limitations}
\label{sec:results_limitations}

\subsection{Experimental Range}

\Revised{The parameter matrix includes commanded offsets of \mbox{\(5^\circ\)}
and \mbox{\(10^\circ\)} about both surface tangents. The rotational and
translational stiffnesses were varied at three levels. The both-varied comparison
used one both-varied pair: the low cross-axis translational stiffness and high
command-axis rotational stiffness were applied together for each
axis. The compliance-centre comparison sampled seven positions along the
assisting tangent at both signs of the commanded offset, and six along the
tool axis. Case~C additionally
sampled four in-plane commanded rotation and lever directions at one common
magnitude. These settings establish the direction and sign of the assisting
displacement and the dependence of the response on the centre position. The both-varied
stiffness result applies to the selected pair, and the direction result covers
the four sampled in-plane directions.}

\Revised{The measured centre positions are geometry-specific design values
defined by the tool-face dimensions, commanded press, and calibrated surface.
The transferable controller results are the rule relating lever direction and
sign to the signed commanded rotation direction, and the finding that the
tool-axis coordinate has only a weak effect over the tested range. The
distances are then selected for the contact geometry.}

\Revised{A fixed Panda configuration, rectangular tool, plane surface, and commanded
press trajectory provided a common contact geometry across all comparisons.
This controlled basis isolates the effects of the tested controller parameters
and defines the reference response of the complete robot--gripper--tool system.}

\subsection{Measurement and Tool-Mount Uncertainty}

\Revised{The alignment metric combines the measured end-effector orientation with the
calibrated grinding-face direction and surface normal \mbox{\(n_s\)}. It
therefore infers tool orientation under the assumption that the calibrated
tool-to-end-effector relation remains fixed. Approximately
\mbox{\(\pm2^\circ\)} of unloaded clearance was observed about
\mbox{\(y_{\mathrm{EE}}\)}, but this observation is not a calibrated uncertainty
bound under contact load.}

\Revised{For the tested mounting orientation, the projected
\mbox{\(y_{\mathrm{EE}}\)} line was aligned with \mbox{\(t_2\)} by the
commanded \mbox{\(90^\circ\)} normal-axis spin. Relative tool motion may
therefore have contributed more strongly to the \mbox{\(t_2\)} conditions. The
TCP-height and EE-inferred results imply larger relative motion in some runs,
but its magnitude and physical cause were not measured independently.}

\Revised{Within-setting scatter was not uniform and increased near transitions
between response regimes. The setting-specific standard deviations are
therefore reported where they materially affect a comparison rather than being
replaced by one global uncertainty value.}

\Revised{Within-setting scatter is not the only scale that bounds a comparison.
The grinding-face direction in end-effector coordinates was obtained by
seating the face on the surface at several normal-axis orientations and solving
for their invariant direction. The archived controller settings preserve the
result used in every reported run, but the mount was not independently
characterised under load. The absolute zero of the EE-inferred alignment metric
therefore carries calibration and mounting uncertainty that is not represented
by the repeated-run standard deviation.}

\Revised{This bounds the absolute residuals and not the comparisons. A constant
error in the measured face direction shifts every condition by the same amount
and cancels in a difference between conditions. The rotation since first
contact, resolved along the surface axes, is measured from joint angles alone
and carries neither the face direction nor the surface normal; it agreed with
the change in the alignment metric to \mbox{\(0.05^\circ\)} on every trial
compared. The reported changes are therefore not limited by this uncertainty,
whereas a statement that the tool ended flat within a degree would be.
\mbox{\Cref{fig:results_metric_agreement}} places the two quantities side by side: the
left panel overlays them for one trial, and the right panel puts the magnitude
of the reported change against the rotation measured from joint angles for
every archived trial.}

\Revised{Many conditions about \mbox{\(t_1\)} ended near a residual of
\mbox{\(3.3^\circ\)}, which invites reading that value as a limit of the
arrangement. Other conditions passed it: the reversed offset about
\mbox{\(t_1\)} with the centre on the TCP left \mbox{\(2.23\pm0.24^\circ\)},
and the assisting position about \mbox{\(t_2\)} left
\mbox{\(1.95\pm0.31^\circ\)} at the full commanded offset and
\mbox{\(1.80\pm0.06^\circ\)} at half of it. The residual is a property of the
settings rather than of the mount.}

\Revised{The model-estimated force change from the clearance transition provided the
supporting normal-load quantity. It was evaluated with the alignment metric
across matched runs. Both quantities follow a common robot-state and model
basis, preserving a consistent comparison across the contact campaign.}

\subsection{Null-Space Configuration and Real-Time Operation}

\Revised{The surface-contact results establish controller performance with
projected damping and singular-value conditioning active together in
null-space mode~3. Matched pose-hold trials isolate the two contributions under
an internally commanded point-force equivalent.}

\Revised{The communication check described in
\mbox{\cref{subsec:experimental_software_computer}} passed the \mbox{\(0.90\)}
average command-success criterion before the experiments. The controller then
completed every reported contact and pose-hold trial through the nominal
\mbox{\(1\,\mathrm{kHz}\)} Franka Control Interface. The reported campaign therefore
ran to completion at the nominal control rate.}
