\subsection{Reference Frames}
\ReviewMark{yellow}{The controller-specific frame choices are useful, although
the three long bullet entries mix essential implementation conventions with
textbook description.}

Three right-handed coordinate frames are used in the controller
\cite{Siciliano2009, Craig2005}. Joint indexing is introduced using the
conventional serial-chain notation of Denavit and Hartenberg
\cite{Denavit1955, Craig2005}. The implemented controller does not evaluate a
separate Denavit--Hartenberg model: the end-effector pose and the geometric
Jacobian are obtained directly from the libfranka model interface.

\begin{itemize}
  \item Base frame $\{0\}$: fixed to the robot mounting
        surface. Its origin is the robot base reference point used by
        the kinematic model. For the upright Panda configuration used in
        this thesis, the $z_0$-axis points normal to the mounting surface
        and is aligned with the first joint axis, while the $x_0$- and
        $y_0$-axes lie in the mounting plane and complete a right-handed
        coordinate system according to the manufacturer convention.
        All positions, orientations, and forces are ultimately expressed in
        this frame unless stated otherwise.
  \item Joint frames $\{1\},\{2\},\ldots,\{n\}$: rigidly
        attached to the successive links of the kinematic chain. Joint \(i\)
        rotates about \(z_{i-1}\), and \(p_{i-1}\) is a point on that axis;
        this indexing is used by the Jacobian below. These frames encode the
        fixed geometry between consecutive joint axes.
  \item End-effector (\abbr{EE}) frame $\{\mathrm{EE}\}$: attached to the tool
        centre point. Its origin is the controlled point at the tool tip
        or flange, depending on the selected tool definition. The axes
        describe the tool orientation: $z_{\mathrm{EE}}$ is taken as the tool approach
        direction, while $x_{\mathrm{EE}}$ and $y_{\mathrm{EE}}$ span the tool plane and complete
        a right-handed frame. The position and orientation of $\{\mathrm{EE}\}$
        relative to $\{0\}$ constitute the controlled position and orientation of the robot.
        In the implementation, this frame is the libfranka end-effector/tool
        frame selected through the robot model interface.
\end{itemize}

\subsection{Rigid Transformations}
\ReviewMark{yellow}{The direction-cosine and composition conventions support
the later derivations, but much of this subsection restates standard material
that could be shortened around the equations.}

A position is a vector \(p\in\mathbb{R}^3\), and an orientation is a proper
rotation \(R\in SO(3)\), for which \(R^\top R=I\), \(\det R=1\) and
\(R^{-1}=R^\top\). For \(R={}^bR_a\), let \(e_i^b\) and \(e_j^a\) denote axes of
the reference and rotated frames. Its direction-cosine entries are
\begin{equation}
R_{ij}=(e_i^b)^\top e_j^a .
\label{eq:direction_cosine_entry}
\end{equation}
Thus column \(j\), rather than row \(j\), contains the \(j\)-th axis of frame
\(\{a\}\) expressed in frame \(\{b\}\) \cite{Craig2005}. The elementary
right-handed rotations used later for the surface tilt are
\begin{equation}
\begin{aligned}
R_x(\alpha)&=
\begin{bmatrix}1&0&0\\0&\cos\alpha&-\sin\alpha\\0&\sin\alpha&\cos\alpha\end{bmatrix},&
R_y(\beta)&=
\begin{bmatrix}\cos\beta&0&\sin\beta\\0&1&0\\-\sin\beta&0&\cos\beta\end{bmatrix},\\
R_z(\gamma)&=
\begin{bmatrix}\cos\gamma&-\sin\gamma&0\\\sin\gamma&\cos\gamma&0\\0&0&1\end{bmatrix}.
\end{aligned}
\label{eq:elementary_rotations}
\end{equation}

Rotation and translation are collected in the homogeneous transformation
\cite{Craig2005,Siciliano2009}
\begin{equation}
{}^bT_a=
\begin{bmatrix}
{}^bR_a & {}^bp_a\\
0^\top & 1
\end{bmatrix}.
\label{eq:homogeneous_transformation}
\end{equation}
It maps a point by \({}^bp={}^bR_a\,{}^ap+{}^bp_a\) and composes as
\({}^cT_a={}^cT_b{}^bT_a\).

\subsection{End-Effector Pose}
\label{sec:position_orientation}
\ReviewMark{green}{This subsection is concise and states exactly which pose is
controlled, which frame it uses, and how the implementation obtains it.}

The controlled pose consists of the end-effector position
\begin{equation}
p_{\mathrm{EE}}
=
\begin{bmatrix}
p_{x,\mathrm{EE}} & p_{y,\mathrm{EE}} & p_{z,\mathrm{EE}}
\end{bmatrix}^{\top}
\in\mathbb{R}^3
\label{eq:end_effector_position}
\end{equation}
and orientation \(R_{\mathrm{EE}}\in SO(3)\), both expressed in the base frame
and collected into \(T_{\mathrm{EE}}\) by
\cref{eq:homogeneous_transformation}. The implementation reads this
transformation directly from the reported end-effector pose; no Euler-angle
conversion is used.

\subsection{Position and Orientation Error}
\label{subsec:position_orientation_error}
\ReviewMark{green}{The sign and frame of both error components are derived in
enough detail to make the implementation checkable.}

Let the desired end-effector pose be
\begin{equation}
T_d
=
\begin{bmatrix}R_d&p_d\\0^\top&1\end{bmatrix},
\label{eq:desired_transformation}
\end{equation}
with \(T_{\mathrm{EE}}\) the current pose of
\cref{eq:homogeneous_transformation}.

The controller obtains the pose error from the relative transformation because
the difference of two homogeneous transformations is not itself a rigid-body
transformation:
\begin{equation}
T_{\mathrm{err}}
=
T_d^{-1}T_{\mathrm{EE}}
=
\begin{bmatrix}
R_d^\top R_{\mathrm{EE}} &
R_d^\top(p_{\mathrm{EE}}-p_d)\\
0^\top&1
\end{bmatrix},
\label{eq:expanded_transformation_error}
\end{equation}
using the inverse of a homogeneous transformation
\cite{Craig2005,Siciliano2009}. When the two poses coincide,
\(T_{\mathrm{err}}=T_d^{-1}T_d=I_4\), so the identity matrix represents zero
position and orientation error.

The translational block of \cref{eq:expanded_transformation_error} is expressed
in the desired frame. The impedance law instead uses the restoring error in the
base frame,
\begin{equation}
e_p=p_d-p_{\mathrm{EE}}\in\mathbb{R}^3 ,
\label{eq:position_control_error}
\end{equation}
which corresponds to \(-R_d\,\Delta p_d\) for the displacement
\(\Delta p_d=R_d^\top(p_{\mathrm{EE}}-p_d)\) read from that block.

\paragraph{Axis--angle form of the orientation error.}
The rotational spring requires the rotation that carries the current
orientation to the desired one,
\begin{equation}
\Delta R=R_{\mathrm{EE}}^\top R_d .
\label{eq:relative_rotation_error}
\end{equation}
If the current orientation already equals the desired one then
\(\Delta R=I\), so the identity again represents zero error.

For use in the impedance controller this rotation must be represented as a
three-dimensional vector. A rotation matrix \(\Delta R\) can be described by a
rotation angle \(\phi\) about a unit rotation axis \(u\in\mathbb{R}^3\)
\cite{Siciliano2009,Murray1994}. The pair \((\phi,u)\) is the screw axis of the
relative rotation, and \(u\) carries only its direction.

The angle follows from the trace, the sum of the diagonal elements. For a
three-dimensional rotation,
\begin{equation}
\operatorname{tr}(\Delta R)=1+2\cos\phi ,
\label{eq:trace_angle_relation}
\end{equation}
so that
\begin{equation}
\phi
=
\arccos\!\left(\frac{\operatorname{tr}(\Delta R)-1}{2}\right),
\qquad \phi\in[0,\pi].
\label{eq:angle_from_trace}
\end{equation}

The axis follows from the skew-symmetric part of \(\Delta R\). Rodrigues'
rotation formula expresses a rotation as
\cite{Siciliano2009,Murray1994}
\begin{equation}
\Delta R
=
I+\sin\phi\,[u]_\times+(1-\cos\phi)[u]_\times^2 ,
\label{eq:rodrigues_formula}
\end{equation}
where \([u]_\times\) is the skew-symmetric matrix of \(u\),
\begin{equation}
[u]_\times
=
\begin{bmatrix}
0 & -u_z & u_y\\
u_z & 0 & -u_x\\
-u_y & u_x & 0
\end{bmatrix},
\qquad
[u]_\times^\top=-[u]_\times .
\label{eq:skew_symmetric_axis_matrix}
\end{equation}
Only the term \(\sin\phi\,[u]_\times\) carries the axis. Transposing
\cref{eq:rodrigues_formula} flips the sign of that term while leaving the
identity and the symmetric term \((1-\cos\phi)[u]_\times^2\) unchanged, so
subtracting the transpose isolates it:
\begin{equation}
\tfrac{1}{2}\left(\Delta R-\Delta R^\top\right)
=
\sin\phi\,[u]_\times .
\label{eq:skew_part_rotation_matrix}
\end{equation}
Reading the entries of \cref{eq:skew_part_rotation_matrix} and dividing by
\(\sin\phi\) gives the axis
\begin{equation}
u
=
\frac{1}{2\sin\phi}
\begin{bmatrix}
\Delta R_{32}-\Delta R_{23}\\
\Delta R_{13}-\Delta R_{31}\\
\Delta R_{21}-\Delta R_{12}
\end{bmatrix}.
\label{eq:rotation_axis_from_matrix}
\end{equation}

Scaling the axis by the angle gives the orientation error in the current
end-effector frame, and rotating it with \(R_{\mathrm{EE}}\) expresses it in
the base frame:
\begin{equation}
e_{R,\mathrm{EE}}=\phi u,
\qquad
e_R=R_{\mathrm{EE}}\,e_{R,\mathrm{EE}}\in\mathbb{R}^3 .
\label{eq:rotation_error_base_frame}
\end{equation}
The direction of \(e_R\) is the axis of the required rotational correction and
its magnitude is the orientation error in radians. If the orientations agree
then \(\Delta R=I\), \(\phi=0\), and \(e_R=0\).

The six-dimensional controller error is therefore
\begin{equation}
e=
\begin{bmatrix}e_p\\e_R\end{bmatrix}\in\mathbb{R}^6 .
\label{eq:cartesian_error_vector}
\end{equation}

\section{Kinematics of a 7-DOF Manipulator}
\ReviewMark{red}{This section retains the strongest textbook-restatement
pattern in the thesis. The kinematic conventions are needed, but the generic
explanation around them is repetitive and weakly tied to the implemented Panda.}

Having defined the end-effector pose as the controlled variable, the next step
is to describe how it is obtained from the joint angles.

\subsection{Forward Kinematics}
\ReviewMark{red}{The subsection follows a canonical textbook sequence and
repeats definitions that an examiner already knows. Retain the convention and
indexing needed later, but reduce the generic exposition.}

Forward kinematics maps the joint configuration to the resulting end-effector
position and orientation. For a serial manipulator the end-effector
transformation is the ordered product of the homogeneous transformations
between neighbouring link frames,
\begin{equation}
{}^0T_n(q)
=
{}^0T_1(q_1)\,
{}^1T_2(q_2)\cdots
{}^{n-1}T_n(q_n),
\label{eq:forward_kinematics_chain}
\end{equation}
where the joint configuration vector collects the joint angles,
\begin{equation}
q =
\begin{bmatrix}
q_1 & q_2 & \cdots & q_n
\end{bmatrix}^\top
\in \mathbb{R}^n .
\label{eq:joint_configuration_vector}
\end{equation}
In the Denavit--Hartenberg convention each \(q_i\) is the rotation of joint
\(i\) about its own joint axis, measured in radians.

Each factor \({}^{i-1}T_i(q_i)\) describes the relative pose of two
neighbouring link frames. In the standard Denavit--Hartenberg convention
\cite{Denavit1955,Craig2005,Siciliano2009} it is the product of four elementary
homogeneous transformations,
\begin{equation}
{}^{i-1}T_i(q_i)
=
R_z(q_i)\,T_z(d_i)\,T_x(a_i)\,R_x(\alpha_i),
\label{eq:dh_elementary_transformations}
\end{equation}
in which \(R_z\) and \(R_x\) are homogeneous rotations and \(T_z\) and \(T_x\)
homogeneous translations. All four are \(4\times4\), so their product maps
frame \(\{i\}\) into frame \(\{i-1\}\). With column vectors the factors act
from right to left: \(R_x(\alpha_i)\) rotates about \(x\) by the link twist,
\(T_x(a_i)\) translates along \(x\) by the link length, \(T_z(d_i)\) translates
along \(z\) by the offset, and \(R_z(q_i)\) applies the joint rotation.

Written out, the product is
\begin{equation}
{}^{i-1}T_i(q_i)
=
\begin{bmatrix}
\cos q_i & -\sin q_i\cos\alpha_i & \sin q_i\sin\alpha_i & a_i\cos q_i\\
\sin q_i & \cos q_i\cos\alpha_i & -\cos q_i\sin\alpha_i & a_i\sin q_i\\
0 & \sin\alpha_i & \cos\alpha_i & d_i\\
0 & 0 & 0 & 1
\end{bmatrix}.
\label{eq:dh_transformation_matrix}
\end{equation}
The parameters \(d_i\), \(a_i\) and \(\alpha_i\) describe the fixed geometry
between neighbouring link frames: \(d_i\) is a translation along \(z\),
\(a_i\) a translation along \(x\), and the link twist \(\alpha_i\) the angle
between two neighbouring joint axes, that is, how far \(z_i\) is tilted with
respect to \(z_{i-1}\).

The complete product gives the end-effector pose in the base frame,
\begin{equation}
{}^0T_n(q)
=
T_{\mathrm{EE}}(q)
=
\begin{bmatrix}
R_{\mathrm{EE}}(q) & p_{\mathrm{EE}}(q)\\
0^\top & 1
\end{bmatrix},
\label{eq:forward_kinematics_result}
\end{equation}
whose upper-left \(3\times3\) block is the end-effector orientation and whose
upper-right column is the end-effector position. The mapping is nonlinear,
since the link transformations contain trigonometric functions of the joint
angles, so a change in one joint angle generally produces a nonlinear change in
the end-effector pose.

The convention is stated here because it fixes the joint indexing used below.
The implemented controller does not evaluate this chain at run time: libfranka
supplies both the end-effector pose and the model Jacobian directly.

\subsection{Differential Kinematics}
\ReviewMark{red}{The pose-to-velocity transition is presented as a generic
definition rather than a thesis-specific requirement; most of the prose can be
condensed around \(\dot x=J\dot q\).}

Forward kinematics gives the pose as a function of \(q\). Control also requires
the velocity relation. Writing the Cartesian end-effector state as
\begin{equation}
x = f(q),
\label{eq:cartesian_state_forward_kinematics}
\end{equation}
and differentiating with respect to time gives
\begin{equation}
\dot{x}
=
J(q)\dot{q}.
\label{eq:differential_kinematics}
\end{equation}
Here \(\dot{x}\in\mathbb{R}^6\) is the end-effector twist, consisting of the
linear and angular velocities,
\begin{equation}
\dot{x}
=
\begin{bmatrix}
\dot{p}_{\mathrm{EE}}\\
\omega_{\mathrm{EE}}
\end{bmatrix},
\label{eq:cartesian_velocity_vector}
\end{equation}
and \(\dot q\in\mathbb{R}^n\) collects the joint velocities,
\begin{equation}
\dot{q}
=
\begin{bmatrix}
\dot{q}_1 & \dot{q}_2 & \cdots & \dot{q}_n
\end{bmatrix}^\top,
\label{eq:joint_velocity_vector}
\end{equation}
each \(\dot q_i\) being the angular velocity of joint \(i\) in radians per
second. The matrix \(J(q)\in\mathbb{R}^{6\times n}\) is the geometric Jacobian
\cite{Siciliano2009,Craig2005}. Because it depends on \(q\), the same joint
velocity produces different end-effector velocities in different
configurations.

\subsection{Geometric Jacobian}
\ReviewMark{yellow}{The column formula is relevant to later point-force and
null-space arguments, but its physical explanation repeats standard robotics
material at greater length than the thesis needs.}

The Jacobian describes the local relation between joint velocities and the
end-effector twist. For a robot with \(n\) joints it is written column-wise,
\begin{equation}
\begin{bmatrix}\dot p_{\mathrm{EE}}\\\omega_{\mathrm{EE}}\end{bmatrix}
=
J(q)\dot q,
\qquad
J(q)=\begin{bmatrix}J_1&J_2&\cdots&J_n\end{bmatrix}
\in\mathbb{R}^{6\times n},
\label{eq:jacobian_velocity_relation}
\end{equation}
where each column \(J_i\) is the contribution of joint \(i\). The Panda has
only revolute joints, so with \(z_{i-1}\) the unit vector along the axis of
joint \(i\) and \(p_{i-1}\) a point on that axis, both expressed in the base
frame, the column is
\begin{equation}
J_i=
\begin{bmatrix}
z_{i-1}\times\left(p_{\mathrm{EE}}-p_{i-1}\right)\\
z_{i-1}
\end{bmatrix}
\in\mathbb{R}^6 .
\label{eq:jacobian_column}
\end{equation}
This standard form follows from the instantaneous velocity of a point under a
revolute motion \cite{Siciliano2009,Craig2005}. The vector
\(p_{\mathrm{EE}}-p_{i-1}\) is the lever arm from the joint axis to the
end-effector, so the cross product
\(z_{i-1}\times(p_{\mathrm{EE}}-p_{i-1})\) is the linear velocity that rotation
of joint \(i\) produces at the end-effector, while the lower block \(z_{i-1}\)
is the corresponding angular velocity. Each column therefore stacks a linear
contribution above an angular one.

Because \(z_{i-1}\), \(p_{i-1}\) and \(p_{\mathrm{EE}}\) all depend on the
current joint configuration, the Jacobian is configuration dependent, and the
influence of each joint on the end-effector motion changes as the robot moves.

\section{Cartesian Impedance Control}
\ReviewMark{yellow}{The control law is essential, but the section repeats the
error and spring--damper interpretation before reaching the implementation-
specific wrench and compensation conventions.}
%% â”€â”€â”€â”€â”€â”€â”€â”€â”€â”€â”€â”€â”€â”€â”€â”€â”€â”€â”€â”€â”€â”€â”€â”€â”€â”€â”€â”€â”€â”€â”€â”€â”€â”€â”€â”€â”€â”€â”€â”€â”€â”€â”€â”€â”€â”€â”€â”€â”€â”€â”€â”€â”€â”€â”€â”€â”€â”€â”€â”€â”€

The Jacobian is used in the reverse direction as well, to map a Cartesian wrench
to joint torques \cite{Khatib1987}.
Before this mapping is introduced, the desired Cartesian interaction behaviour
is defined using impedance control.

Impedance control prescribes a dynamic relation between end-effector motion and
the wrench acting on it \cite{Hogan1985,Ott2008,Mayr2024CartesianImpedance}.
Here that relation is a Cartesian spring-damper: a pose deviation produces a
restoring wrench through \(K\), and a velocity produces an opposing one
through \(D\). Those two matrices carry the design freedom, since the same law
produces very different contact behaviour depending on their entries
\cite{Pollayil2023ImpedancePlanning}.

The Cartesian error vector was defined as

\begin{equation}
e
=
\begin{bmatrix}
e_p\\
e_R
\end{bmatrix}
\in \mathbb{R}^6,
\label{eq:cartesian_error_impedance}
\end{equation}

where \(e_p\) and \(e_R\) are the translational and rotational errors used in
the Cartesian impedance law.

\subsection{Impedance Wrench}
\ReviewMark{yellow}{The equations and frame commitments are correct and useful;
the term-by-term explanation after each equation is repetitive and can be made
more direct.}

The impedance command is evaluated in the robot base frame, so the matrices
appearing in the commanded wrench are the base-frame stiffness and damping
matrices \(K_{p,0}\), \(D_{p,0}\), \(K_{R,0}\), and \(D_{R,0}\). They are not
assumed to be diagonal in the base frame. If the task frame changes, for example
because the surface normal changes, these base-frame matrices change as well,
and so do the commanded Cartesian force, moment, and joint torque, even when the
local diagonal gain values are kept the same.

The positional impedance force is defined as the translational part of a
Cartesian spring-damper law \cite{Hogan1985,Mayr2024CartesianImpedance},

\begin{equation}
f
=
K_{p,0} e_p
+
D_{p,0}\left(\dot{p}_d-\dot{p}_{\mathrm{EE}}\right)
\in \mathbb{R}^3.
\label{eq:positional_impedance_force}
\end{equation}

Here, \(K_{p,0} \in \mathbb{R}^{3 \times 3}\) is the positional stiffness
matrix expressed in the base frame, \(D_{p,0} \in \mathbb{R}^{3 \times 3}\) is
the positional damping matrix expressed in the base frame, and
\(\dot{p}_{\mathrm{EE}}\in\mathbb{R}^3\) and
\(\dot{p}_d\in\mathbb{R}^3\) are the measured and desired Cartesian linear
velocities expressed in the same frame.
The stiffness term \(K_{p,0} e_p\) generates a restoring force
proportional to the position error, while the damping term
\(D_{p,0}(\dot{p}_d-\dot{p}_{\mathrm{EE}})\) damps the translational
velocity error. For a fixed setpoint, \(\dot{p}_d=\mathbf{0}\), so this term
reduces to \(-D_{p,0}\dot{p}_{\mathrm{EE}}\).

The rotational impedance moment is defined as

\begin{equation}
m
=
K_{R,0} e_R
-
D_{R,0} \omega_{\mathrm{EE}}
\in \mathbb{R}^3.
\label{eq:rotational_impedance_moment}
\end{equation}

Here, \(K_{R,0} \in \mathbb{R}^{3 \times 3}\) is the rotational stiffness
matrix expressed in the base frame, \(D_{R,0} \in \mathbb{R}^{3 \times 3}\) is
the rotational damping matrix expressed in the base frame, and
\(\omega_{\mathrm{EE}}\in\mathbb{R}^3\) is the angular velocity of the
end-effector expressed in the base frame. The stiffness term
\(K_{R,0}e_R\) generates a restoring moment proportional to the
orientation error, while the damping term \(D_{R,0}\omega_{\mathrm{EE}}\)
opposes the angular velocity of the end-effector. The rotational damping
therefore acts on the measured angular velocity itself rather than on an
angular velocity error, because no angular reference velocity is commanded.

The positional force and rotational moment are combined into the Cartesian
wrench. In this thesis, a wrench means a six-dimensional vector containing a
Cartesian force and a Cartesian moment:

\begin{equation}
F
=
\begin{bmatrix}
f\\
m
\end{bmatrix}
=
\begin{bmatrix}
K_{p,0} e_p + D_{p,0}\left(\dot{p}_d-\dot{p}_{\mathrm{EE}}\right)\\
K_{R,0} e_R - D_{R,0} \omega_{\mathrm{EE}}
\end{bmatrix}
\in \mathbb{R}^6.
\label{eq:cartesian_force_moment_vector}
\end{equation}

The wrench ordering is force followed by moment. Every quantity in
\cref{eq:cartesian_force_moment_vector} is expressed in the base frame, and the
resulting wrench is mapped to joint torques through \(J^\top\).

\subsection{Joint-Torque Control Law}
\label{sec:control_law}
\ReviewMark{green}{The virtual-work mapping is concise, cited, and connected
directly to the complete torque command used later.}
%% â”€â”€â”€â”€â”€â”€â”€â”€â”€â”€â”€â”€â”€â”€â”€â”€â”€â”€â”€â”€â”€â”€â”€â”€â”€â”€â”€â”€â”€â”€â”€â”€â”€â”€â”€â”€â”€â”€â”€â”€â”€â”€â”€â”€â”€â”€â”€â”€â”€â”€â”€â”€â”€â”€â”€â”€â”€â”€â”€â”€â”€

By the principle of virtual work, the wrench maps to joint torques
\cite{Khatib1987} through the transpose of the Jacobian,

\begin{equation}
\tau
=
J^\top(q)F,
\label{eq:joint_torque_mapping}
\end{equation}

with \(\tau\in\mathbb{R}^n\) in \(\mathrm{N\,m}\) and
\(J^\top(q)\in\mathbb{R}^{n\times6}\). Substituting
\cref{eq:cartesian_force_moment_vector} gives the complete Cartesian impedance
control law:

\begin{equation}
\tau
=
J^\top(q)
\begin{bmatrix}
K_{p,0} e_p + D_{p,0}\left(\dot{p}_d-\dot{p}_{\mathrm{EE}}\right)\\
K_{R,0} e_R - D_{R,0} \omega_{\mathrm{EE}}
\end{bmatrix}.
\label{eq:cartesian_impedance_control_law}
\end{equation}

This equation is the computational core of the controller. Its gain matrices are
the already transformed base-frame matrices, so for a surface task, changing the
surface frame changes them before the wrench is evaluated, and the joint-torque
command changes with them.

\subsection{Coriolis and Gravity Compensation in libfranka}
\ReviewMark{green}{The distinction between user-added Coriolis compensation and
robot-side gravity compensation is explicit and supported by the interface
documentation.}

In a general robot-dynamics formulation, the impedance torques are combined with
two model-based compensation terms: a gravity term and a Coriolis/centrifugal
term.
The commanded joint torque can therefore be written as

\begin{equation}
\tau_{\mathrm{cmd}}
=
J^\top(q)F
+
\tau_g(q)
+
\tau_c(q,\dot{q}).
\label{eq:commanded_torque}
\end{equation}

This is the general model-compensated structure, in which \(\tau_g(q)\) is the
gravity torque and \(\tau_c(q,\dot q)\) the Coriolis and centrifugal torque. The
two are handled differently in the implementation used here.

The Coriolis vector provided by the libfranka model was added explicitly to the
commanded torque, since the robot does not compensate it internally. A separate
gravity term was not added: in the external joint-torque mode of the Franka
Control Interface, the robot runs its own rigid-body model and compensates
gravity and motor friction on board, so the commanded torque is interpreted as
the torque excluding gravity \cite{FrankaFCI2023}. Adding \(\tau_g(q)\) again
would compensate the same effect twice.

The implemented command therefore follows
\begin{equation}
\tau_{\mathrm{cmd,impl}}
=
J^\top(q)F
+
\tau_c(q,\dot{q}),
\label{eq:commanded_torque_libfranka}
\end{equation}
which matches the official libfranka Cartesian impedance
example~\cite{libfrankaCartesianExample}. The secondary null-space term is added
once it has been introduced, giving the complete command of
\cref{eq:nullspace_complete_torque_command}.

\section{Surface Task Frame}
\label{subsec:surface_task_frame}
\ReviewMark{green}{The construction defines the surface-frame order,
normalisation condition, and relationship to the gain coordinates unambiguously.}

The contact experiment uses a task frame tied to the surface geometry. This
frame is defined before the stiffness and damping matrices are introduced, so
that the local gain entries can later be interpreted as normal and tangential
values instead of as abstract base-frame directions.

Let \(n_s\in\mathbb{R}^3\) with \(\|n_s\|=1\) be the unit surface normal,
expressed in the robot base frame. To complete the frame, choose an auxiliary
direction \(a_s\in\mathbb{R}^3\) that is not parallel to \(n_s\). This auxiliary
direction is only used to define a repeatable tangent direction on the surface.
The first tangent vector is obtained by projecting \(a_s\) onto the plane
orthogonal to \(n_s\), and the second tangent vector is then obtained by a cross
product. With \(\tilde{t}_1\) denoting the unnormalised projected tangent, the
construction is a Gram--Schmidt orthonormalization step applied to the surface
normal and the auxiliary direction \cite{Siciliano2009,Craig2005}:

\begin{equation}
\begin{aligned}
\tilde{t}_1
&=
a_s - n_s\left(n_s^\top a_s\right),\\
t_1
&=
\frac{\tilde{t}_1}{\|\tilde{t}_1\|},\\
t_2
&=
n_s\times t_1 .
\end{aligned}
\label{eq:surface_frame_tangents}
\end{equation}

Here, \(t_1\) and \(t_2\) are unit tangent vectors lying in the surface plane.
The construction is valid as long as \(\|\tilde{t}_1\|\neq0\), which means that
the auxiliary direction \(a_s\) must not be parallel to the normal \(n_s\). To
match the implemented controller and its parameter order, the surface task
frame collects the two tangents first and the normal last,
\begin{equation}
R_{\mathrm{surface}}
=
\begin{bmatrix}
t_1 & t_2 & n_s
\end{bmatrix}.
\label{eq:surface_task_frame_matrix}
\end{equation}

The column order is fixed: every surface-frame three-vector in the
implementation uses
\([\,\text{tangent 1},\text{tangent 2},\text{normal}\,]^\top\).
In the gain representation below, the contact task uses this frame by setting
\(R_{\mathrm{task}}=R_{\mathrm{surface}}\). This makes stiffness values such as
\(K_{t_1}\), \(K_{t_2}\), and \(K_n\) local surface-frame quantities in that
same order.

\section{Stiffness and Damping Representation}
\label{subsec:KD_matrices}
\ReviewMark{yellow}{The frame transformation is sound, but the section repeats
the same consequence several times and only near the end states that active
set-up translation remains diagonal in base coordinates.}

The positional stiffness matrix \(K_p\) and damping matrix \(D_p\) do not have
to be diagonal in the robot base frame \(\{0\}\).
Here, the subscript \(p\) refers to the positional part of the impedance
controller.
A diagonal matrix is obtained only when the coordinate axes of the chosen frame
are aligned with the principal stiffness directions.
For this reason, the positional stiffness and damping matrices are first
defined in a chosen task frame, where the principal directions are easier to
specify, and are then transformed into the base frame \(\{0\}\).
Let \(R_{\mathrm{task}}\in SO(3)\) be the orientation of this chosen task frame
with respect to the base frame. Its columns are the task-frame axes expressed
in the base frame. If the local task-frame matrices are
\(K_{p,\mathrm{task}}\), \(D_{p,\mathrm{task}}\),
\(K_{R,\mathrm{task}}\), and \(D_{R,\mathrm{task}}\), then the matrices used in
the command of \cref{eq:cartesian_force_moment_vector} are

\begin{equation}
\begin{aligned}
K_{p,0}
&=
R_{\mathrm{task}}K_{p,\mathrm{task}}R_{\mathrm{task}}^\top,
&
D_{p,0}
&=
R_{\mathrm{task}}D_{p,\mathrm{task}}R_{\mathrm{task}}^\top,\\
K_{R,0}
&=
R_{\mathrm{task}}K_{R,\mathrm{task}}R_{\mathrm{task}}^\top,
&
D_{R,0}
&=
R_{\mathrm{task}}D_{R,\mathrm{task}}R_{\mathrm{task}}^\top .
\end{aligned}
\label{eq:general_task_frame_gain_transform}
\end{equation}

The same notation can be written for the full six-dimensional stiffness and
damping matrices. For an uncoupled translational--rotational impedance in the
chosen task frame,

\begin{equation}
K_{\mathrm{task}}
=
\begin{bmatrix}
K_{p,\mathrm{task}} & 0\\
0 & K_{R,\mathrm{task}}
\end{bmatrix},
\qquad
D_{\mathrm{task}}
=
\begin{bmatrix}
D_{p,\mathrm{task}} & 0\\
0 & D_{R,\mathrm{task}}
\end{bmatrix}.
\label{eq:full_task_frame_gain_blocks}
\end{equation}

With
\(T_R=\operatorname{diag}(R_{\mathrm{task}},R_{\mathrm{task}})\), the
corresponding full base-frame matrices are

\begin{equation}
K_0=T_R K_{\mathrm{task}}T_R^\top,
\qquad
D_0=T_R D_{\mathrm{task}}T_R^\top .
\label{eq:full_task_frame_gain_transform}
\end{equation}

Consequently, changing the surface orientation changes the base-frame gain
matrices even when the local diagonal entries remain unchanged. The controller
commands \(K_{p,0}\), \(D_{p,0}\), \(K_{R,0}\), and \(D_{R,0}\) after this
transformation rather than the diagonal local matrices, so a change in
\(R_{\mathrm{task}}\) propagates through the Cartesian wrench to the joint
torque command.

For the contact task, the task frame is the surface task frame introduced in
Section~\ref{subsec:surface_task_frame}. Setting
\(R_{\mathrm{task}}=R_{\mathrm{surface}}\) gives, for example,

\begin{equation}
K_{p,0}
=
R_{\mathrm{surface}}\,
\operatorname{diag}\!\left(K_{t_1},K_{t_2},K_n\right)\,
R_{\mathrm{surface}}^\top,
\label{eq:spatial_gain_matrix}
\end{equation}

and analogously for \(D_{p,0}\), \(K_{R,0}\), and \(D_{R,0}\). In particular,
the rotational diagonal follows the same column order,
\(\operatorname{diag}(K_{r,t_1},K_{r,t_2},K_{r,n})\), as do the translational
and rotational damping triples. In the implementation this operation is the
function \texttt{makeSpatialGainMatrix}.
Thus, choosing the directions through \(n_s\), \(t_1\), and \(t_2\) instead of
fixed base-frame axes lets the same impedance law act on any planar surface
orientation. If the surface normal changes, \(R_{\mathrm{surface}}\) changes,
so the base-frame matrices in the command change even though the local diagonal
entries \(K_{t_1}\), \(K_{t_2}\), and \(K_n\) may remain numerically identical.
This is the generic surface-frame construction and is used for both gain blocks
during approach. In the current set-up and grind implementation, only the
rotational block uses this construction; translational stiffness and damping
remain diagonal in base \([x,y,z]\), as detailed in
Chapter~\ref{ch:implementation}. The controller does not rotate active gains
with the live end-effector orientation \(R_{\mathrm{EE}}\).

\section{Centre of Compliance}
\label{sec:coupled_pole_stiffness}
\ReviewMark{green}{This is a strong derivation: the lever convention, adjoint,
congruence, coupling sign, definiteness, and local-model limitation are all
made checkable.}

The task-frame construction of \cref{eq:full_task_frame_gain_transform} rotates
the principal stiffness directions but keeps the centre of compliance at the
end-effector origin, here identified with the tool centre point (TCP). A second
and independent design choice is \emph{where} the virtual spring is centred.
Shifting the impedance reference from the TCP to the centre of compliance
\(p_c\) transforms a block-diagonal impedance at \(p_c\) into a coupled
impedance at the TCP
\cite{Murray1994,Fasse1997SpatialImpedance,Stramigioli2000IPC,LynchPark2017}.
The derivation below corresponds to the control law applied during set-up, in
which the point-shifted impedance biases alignment while contact constrains the
motion.

The implemented lever, expressed in the base frame, is
\begin{equation}
r_c = p_{\mathrm{TCP}}-p_c \in \R{3},
\qquad p_{\mathrm{TCP}}=p_{\mathrm{EE}} .
\label{eq:pole_to_tcp_lever}
\end{equation}

Let \([r_c]_\times\) denote the skew-symmetric matrix satisfying
\([r_c]_\times a=r_c\times a\). Under the small-angle approximation, the
position error at the centre of compliance is
\begin{equation}
e_{p,c}
=
e_p+[r_c]_\times e_R .
\label{eq:pole_small_motion_error}
\end{equation}
The positive sign follows from the chosen lever: the vector from
the TCP to the centre of compliance is \(-r_c\), and
\(e_R\times(-r_c)=[r_c]_\times e_R\). Stacking the translational
and rotational errors therefore gives

\begin{equation}
e_c
=
\begin{bmatrix}e_{p,c}\\[2pt]e_R\end{bmatrix}
=
\mathrm{Ad}(r_c)
\begin{bmatrix}e_p\\[2pt]e_R\end{bmatrix},
\qquad
\mathrm{Ad}(r_c)
=
\begin{bmatrix}
\mI_3 & [r_c]_\times\\[2pt]
0 & \mI_3
\end{bmatrix}.
\label{eq:offset_adjoint}
\end{equation}

The matrix \(\mathrm{Ad}(r_c)\) is the offset (point-shift) adjoint. It is the
translational counterpart of the block-diagonal rotation
\(T_R=\operatorname{diag}(R_{\mathrm{task}},R_{\mathrm{task}})\) of
\cref{eq:full_task_frame_gain_transform}: \(T_R\) rotates the principal axes,
whereas \(\mathrm{Ad}(r_c)\) moves the reference point. Let the decoupled gains
defined at the centre of compliance be
\begin{equation}
K_c
=
\begin{bmatrix}K_p&0\\0&K_R\end{bmatrix},
\qquad
D_c
=
\begin{bmatrix}D_p&0\\0&D_R\end{bmatrix}.
\label{eq:pole_block_gains}
\end{equation}
The compliance-centre wrench is
\(w_c=K_ce_c\). Transforming this
power-conjugate wrench to the TCP gives
\(w_{\mathrm{TCP}}=\mathrm{Ad}(r_c)^\top w_c\), and therefore
the stiffness expressed at the TCP is

\begin{equation}
K_{\mathrm{TCP}}
=
\mathrm{Ad}(r_c)^\top K_c\,\mathrm{Ad}(r_c)
=
\begin{bmatrix}
K_p & K_p[r_c]_\times\\[4pt]
[r_c]_\times^\top K_p & K_R + [r_c]_\times^\top K_p\,[r_c]_\times
\end{bmatrix},
\label{eq:coupled_tcp_stiffness}
\end{equation}

The same point shift must be applied to damping:
\begin{equation}
D_{\mathrm{TCP}}
=
\mathrm{Ad}(r_c)^\top D_c\,\mathrm{Ad}(r_c)
=
\begin{bmatrix}
D_p & D_p[r_c]_\times\\[4pt]
[r_c]_\times^\top D_p &
D_R+[r_c]_\times^\top D_p[r_c]_\times
\end{bmatrix}.
\label{eq:coupled_tcp_damping}
\end{equation}
The off-diagonal blocks \(K_p[r_c]_\times\) and \([r_c]_\times^\top K_p\) couple a
translational error into a restoring moment and a rotational error into a
restoring force. They are exactly the structured translation--rotation coupling
that the decoupled command of \cref{eq:cartesian_force_moment_vector} does not
contain. Physically, a pure infinitesimal rotation about the centre of compliance has
zero translational error at that centre but produces the corresponding
lever-induced translation at the TCP. The coupled TCP wrench is the wrench
representation of that same virtual spring.

The sign of the translation-to-moment block can be checked without a rotational
error. For \(e_R=0\), let \(f=K_p e_p\). The elastic moment generated at the TCP
is then
\begin{equation}
m_c=[r_c]_\times^\top f=-\,r_c\times f .
\label{eq:pole_translation_moment_sign_check}
\end{equation}
Changing the direction of \(r_c\) therefore reverses this coupling moment. This
relation is also the direct sign test for a proposed centre of compliance and press direction.

Two properties follow directly from \cref{eq:coupled_tcp_stiffness}. First, the
coupling vanishes when \(r_c=0\). Conversely, if \(K_p\) is nonsingular, zero
coupling implies \(r_c=0\). Second, because \(\mathrm{Ad}(r_c)\) is invertible,
the congruence transformation preserves definiteness:
\begin{equation}
K_c\succeq0
\ \Longrightarrow\
K_{\mathrm{TCP}}\succeq0,
\qquad
K_c\succ0
\ \Longrightarrow\
K_{\mathrm{TCP}}\succ0,
\label{eq:pole_gain_definiteness}
\end{equation}
and the same statements hold for \(D_c\) and
\(D_{\mathrm{TCP}}\). Individual negative off-diagonal entries are therefore
coupling terms, not negative stiffness directions.

This construction is a local, small-motion model with \(r_c\) held fixed while
the wrench is evaluated. In the implemented set-up mode, the selected
lever is kept constant, so the mapped gain matrices remain constant during the
motion segment. If the centre of compliance or lever is updated continuously, the gain matrices
become configuration dependent. Accordingly, \(p_c\) is a
\emph{local linearised centre of compliance}, not a guaranteed measured pivot.
It need not coincide with the physical contact point \(p_C\); finite rotational
stiffness, the lever, the environment constraint and friction, and the
active sigma term introduced below all influence the observed motion.

\section{Null-Space Torque}
\label{sec:nullspace_svd}
\ReviewMark{green}{The SVD roles, cutoff, projector convention, unit-scaling
limitation, and lack of dynamic consistency are distinguished rigorously.}

The Franka Emika Panda has seven joints, while the Cartesian motion of the
end-effector is described by six degrees of freedom: three translational and
three rotational components.
The robot is therefore kinematically redundant.
This redundancy can be used to pursue a secondary objective that is
kinematically invisible to the primary Cartesian task to first order.

The relationship between the joint velocities and the Cartesian end-effector
velocity is described by the geometric Jacobian
\begin{equation}
\dot{x}
=
J(q)\dot{q},
\qquad
J(q)\in\mathbb{R}^{6\times7},
\label{eq:nullspace_jacobian_relation}
\end{equation}
where
\begin{equation}
\dot{x}
=
\begin{bmatrix}
\dot{x} & \dot{y} & \dot{z} &
\omega_x & \omega_y & \omega_z
\end{bmatrix}^{\top}
\in \mathbb{R}^{6},
\qquad
\dot{q}
=
\begin{bmatrix}
\dot{q}_1 & \cdots & \dot{q}_7
\end{bmatrix}^{\top}
\in \mathbb{R}^{7}.
\label{eq:nullspace_velocities}
\end{equation}

Since the joint space has seven dimensions while the Cartesian task has six,
a one-dimensional null space exists whenever the Jacobian has full rank.
A joint velocity in this null space satisfies
\begin{equation}
J(q)\dot{q}_0 = 0.
\label{eq:jacobian_nullspace_condition}
\end{equation}
The velocity \(\dot{q}_0\) therefore produces no first-order Cartesian
end-effector velocity and can be used for a secondary joint-space task at the
kinematic level.

In this work, the full six-dimensional geometric Jacobian is used for the
null-space construction because the controller regulates the complete
end-effector pose. The implementation forms a truncated Moore--Penrose
pseudoinverse from the singular value decomposition (\abbr{SVD}); it does not
use a damped normal-equation inverse. The full \abbr{SVD} is
\cite{Chiaverini1994DampedLeastSquares,Dietrich2015NullspaceSurvey}
\begin{equation}
J
=
U_J\Sigma_J V_J^{\top},
\qquad
U_J\in\mathbb{R}^{6\times6},\quad
\Sigma_J\in\mathbb{R}^{6\times7},\quad
V_J\in\mathbb{R}^{7\times7},
\label{eq:jacobian_svd}
\end{equation}
with the six reported singular values ordered as
\begin{equation}
\sigma_1\geq\sigma_2\geq\cdots\geq\sigma_6\geq0,
\qquad
Jv_i=\sigma_i u_i,\quad i=1,\ldots,6 .
\label{eq:svd_direction_mapping}
\end{equation}
The seventh column of the full right-singular-vector matrix is unpaired with a
reported singular value and satisfies
\begin{equation}
Jv_7=0 .
\label{eq:nullspace_search_direction}
\end{equation}
Thus \(v_6\), not \(v_7\), is associated with the smallest reported singular
value
\begin{equation}
\sigma_{\min}=\sigma_6 .
\label{eq:min_singular_value}
\end{equation}
For a full-row-rank \(6\times7\) Jacobian, \(v_7\) spans the single structural
null direction. The vectors \(v_6\) and \(v_7\) therefore have distinct roles:
\(v_6\) identifies the weakest task-producing direction, whereas \(v_7\) is
the redundant joint-motion direction.

The equality
\(\sigma_i=\sqrt{\lambda_i(JJ^\top)}\) explains why a decreasing
\(\sigma_6\) indicates approach to a row-rank singularity. However, the
geometric Jacobian stacks linear and angular velocity rows with different
physical units. Consequently, its numerical singular values, their cutoff,
and comparisons of their magnitudes depend on the chosen coordinate scaling
or characteristic length. Here \(\sigma_{\min}\) is used as an
implementation-specific conditioning indicator, not as a unit-invariant
manipulability measure.

Let \(\rho\geq0\) be the configured relative SVD tolerance and define
\begin{equation}
\varepsilon_\rho=\rho\sigma_1,
\qquad
\mathcal R_\rho=\{\,i\in\{1,\ldots,6\}\mid
\sigma_i>\varepsilon_\rho\,\}.
\label{eq:svd_relative_cutoff}
\end{equation}
Only the retained singular directions are inverted. The exact formula used by
the controller is
\begin{equation}
J_\rho^+
=
\sum_{i\in\mathcal R_\rho}
\frac{1}{\sigma_i}\,v_i u_i^\top
\in\mathbb{R}^{7\times6}.
\label{eq:truncated_svd_pseudoinverse}
\end{equation}
The reciprocal is \(1/\sigma_i\), and directions at or below the cutoff are
omitted rather than regularised. The corresponding joint-velocity and torque
projectors are
\begin{equation}
N_q=I_7-J_\rho^+J,
\qquad
N_\tau=N_q^\top
=I_7-J^\top J_\rho^{+\top}
\in\mathbb{R}^{7\times7}.
\label{eq:nullspace_velocity_torque_projectors}
\end{equation}
For this truncated SVD, \(J_\rho^+J\) is an orthogonal projector onto the
retained right-singular subspace. Hence \(N_q=N_\tau\) analytically; the
implementation symmetrizes \(N_\tau\) only to remove floating-point roundoff.
If all six directions are retained and \(J\) has full row rank, then
\(N_q=N_\tau=v_7v_7^\top\) and the null relation is exact. If directions are
truncated, they are deliberately admitted to the numerical null space and
\begin{equation}
\lVert JN_q\rVert_2\leq\varepsilon_\rho ,
\label{eq:nullspace_projected_vector_task_effect}
\end{equation}
up to floating-point error.

This projector is Euclidean and kinematic, not dynamically consistent. In
particular, \(JN_q=0\) does not in general imply
\(JM(q)^{-1}N_\tau\tau=0\). A projected torque can therefore couple into
Cartesian acceleration through the inertia matrix even when it is orthogonal
to the retained task subspace. Claims of exact dynamic task decoupling would
require an inertia-weighted projector
\cite{Khatib1987,Dietrich2015NullspaceSurvey}.

\subsection{Projected Damping}
\ReviewMark{green}{The damping term is defined concisely and its absence of a
preferred posture is stated without overclaiming.}

Projected damping dissipates joint motion inside the null space,
\begin{equation}
\tau_d=-d_{\mathrm{null}}N_\tau\dot q,
\label{eq:nullspace_damping_torque}
\end{equation}
where \(d_{\mathrm{null}}\geq0\) is the projected damping coefficient. The
implemented law contains no restoring spring towards
\(q_{\mathrm{start}}\). It therefore reduces projected joint velocity without
defining a preferred redundant posture.

\subsection{Smallest-Singular-Value Bias}
\ReviewMark{green}{The two-point selector, sign invariance, deadband, fixed
torque magnitude, and first-order limitation are explained clearly.}

The columns of \(V_J\) are unit vectors, so \(v_7\) is already the unit
structural null direction. The conditioning term compares
\(\sigma_{\min}\) at two nearby configurations,
\begin{equation}
q_+=q+\alpha_{\mathrm{probe}}v_7,
\qquad
q_-=q-\alpha_{\mathrm{probe}}v_7,
\label{eq:nullspace_candidates}
\end{equation}
and the Jacobian and \(\sigma_6\) are recomputed at each sample. With
\(\sigma_+=\sigma_{\min}(J(q_+))\),
\(\sigma_-=\sigma_{\min}(J(q_-))\), and
\(\Delta_\sigma=\sigma_+-\sigma_-\), the sign selector is
\begin{equation}
s
=
\begin{cases}
+1, & \Delta_\sigma>\delta_\sigma,\\
-1, & \Delta_\sigma<-\delta_\sigma,\\
0,  & |\Delta_\sigma|\leq\delta_\sigma .
\end{cases}
\label{eq:nullspace_direction_selection}
\end{equation}
The deadband \(\delta_\sigma\geq0\) suppresses the conditioning torque when the
two probes are indistinguishable. The arbitrary SVD sign of \(v_7\) does not
change the result: reversing \(v_7\) swaps \(q_+\) and \(q_-\), reverses \(s\),
and leaves \(s v_7\) unchanged. The angle \(\alpha_{\mathrm{probe}}\)
specifies only where \(\sigma_{\min}\) is sampled and is not multiplied into the
commanded torque.

One approximation is inherent to the construction. The direction \(v_7\) is
the instantaneous tangent to the constant-pose self-motion at \(q\), so for
finite \(\alpha_{\mathrm{probe}}\) neither \(q_+\) nor \(q_-\) is guaranteed to
lie on that nonlinear manifold, so preservation of the end-effector pose is only
a first-order property of the probe direction. The sign selection also discards
the sampled slope and applies a fixed torque magnitude, which makes the term a
two-point conditioning heuristic rather than a gradient method: it does not
guarantee monotonic improvement of \(\sigma_{\min}\).

The second contribution is the resulting bias itself,
\begin{equation}
\tau_\sigma=k_\sigma N_\tau(s v_7),
\label{eq:nullspace_sigma_torque}
\end{equation}
where \(k_\sigma\) is the commanded torque magnitude. Unlike
\cref{eq:nullspace_damping_torque} it is an active, switched,
constant-magnitude term rather than a dissipative one.

\subsection{Complete Null-Space Torque}
\ReviewMark{green}{The two terms are combined without obscuring their distinct
dissipative and active roles.}

Both contributions act through the same projector and therefore add. The
complete secondary torque is

\begin{equation}
\tau_{\mathrm{null}}
=
\tau_d+\tau_\sigma
=
-d_{\mathrm{null}}N_\tau\dot q
+
k_\sigma N_\tau\!\left(s v_7\right)
\in\mathbb{R}^{7}.
\label{eq:nullspace_torque}
\end{equation}

The two terms have different roles. The damping term dissipates energy associated
with motion in the projected subspace, whereas the singular-value term applies
an active constant-magnitude bias along \(v_7\). Projected damping consequently
limits the motion induced by that bias when both terms are active.

The construction assumes full row rank. At full row rank the kinematic null
space of a seven-joint arm is one-dimensional and \(v_7\) spans it, so a
single probed direction covers the whole subspace. If the Jacobian loses row
rank the null space gains a dimension, while \(v_7\) still names only the
last right-singular vector of the full decomposition; the bias then acts along
one direction of a larger subspace rather than across it.

Which of the two terms is active is a configuration choice rather than a
property of the law, and \cref{sec:nullspace_conditioning_implementation}
gives the selectable combinations and the one used for the reported runs.

\section{Complete Joint-Torque Command}
\ReviewMark{green}{The final equation closes the theory chapter cleanly and
routes each torque contribution to its earlier definition.}

The complete torque command of the controller is therefore
\begin{equation}
\tau_{\mathrm{cmd}}
=
J^{\top}(q)\,F_{\mathrm{cmd}}
+
\tau_{\mathrm{null}}
+
\tau_c,
\label{eq:nullspace_complete_torque_command}
\end{equation}
where \(J^{\top}F_{\mathrm{cmd}}\) is the Cartesian impedance torque,
\(\tau_{\mathrm{null}}\) is the null-space term, and \(\tau_c\) is the Coriolis
compensation added in the user callback. The gravity compensation is handled by
the Franka Control Interface for the external torque command, as explained
around Equation~\eqref{eq:commanded_torque_libfranka}.
