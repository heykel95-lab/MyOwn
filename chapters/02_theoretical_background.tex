This chapter presents the mathematical basis of the Cartesian impedance
controller. The required frame conventions and pose errors are introduced
first. The subsequent sections derive the kinematic mappings, the impedance
wrench \mbox{\(F\)}, the compliance-centre transformation, and the null-space
torque \mbox{\(\tau_{\mathrm{null}}\)}.

\section{Reference Frames}
\label{sec:reference_frames}

The following right-handed coordinate frames are used in the theoretical
formulation and the controller
\mbox{\cite{Siciliano2009,Craig2005}}.

\begin{itemize}
  \item \mbox{\textbf{Base frame \mbox{\(\{0\}\)}:}}
        The base frame is fixed to the robot mounting surface. Its origin is
        the robot base reference point used by the kinematic model. For the
        upright Panda configuration used in this thesis, the \mbox{\(z_0\)}-axis is
        normal to the mounting surface, while the \mbox{\(x_0\)}- and \mbox{\(y_0\)}-axes
        lie in the mounting plane. Unless stated otherwise, Cartesian
        positions, directions, velocities, forces, and moments are expressed
        in this frame.

  \item \mbox{\textbf{Joint frames
        \mbox{\(\{1\},\{2\},\ldots,\{n\}\)}:}}
        The joint frames follow the robot kinematic convention and are required
        for forward kinematics and Jacobian construction; the real-time
        controller obtains the corresponding quantities from the libfranka
        model.

  \item \mbox{\textbf{End-effector frame
        \mbox{\(\{\mathrm{EE}\}\)}:}}
        The end-effector frame is attached to the controlled TCP and moves with
        the tool. In the implemented robot model, the end-effector origin and
        TCP are identified, so \mbox{\(p_{\mathrm{TCP}}=p_{\mathrm{EE}}\)}. The
        physical tool face is represented separately by its calibrated
        geometric offset from this frame. The position
        \mbox{\(p_{\mathrm{EE}}\)} and orientation
        \mbox{\(R_{\mathrm{EE}}\)}, both expressed relative to the base frame,
        define the current Cartesian pose of the robot. The axes
        \mbox{\(x_{\mathrm{EE}}\)}, \mbox{\(y_{\mathrm{EE}}\)}, and
        \mbox{\(z_{\mathrm{EE}}\)} describe the current end-effector
        orientation. The configured tool approach axis is defined in this frame
        and is transformed to the base frame using
        \mbox{\(R_{\mathrm{EE}}\)}.

  \item \mbox{\textbf{Desired end-effector frame \mbox{\(\{d\}\)}:}}
        The desired frame represents the commanded TCP pose. Its origin is
        \mbox{\(p_d\)}, and its orientation is \mbox{\(R_d\)}, both expressed relative to
        the base frame. The difference between the desired and current
        end-effector frames provides the translational and rotational errors
        used by the impedance controller.

  \item \mbox{\textbf{Surface frame \mbox{\(\{S\}\)}:}}
        The surface frame is tied to the configured surface rather than
        to the moving tool. Its origin is placed at the configured surface
        point \mbox{\(p_s\)}. Its orientation \mbox{\(R_{\mathrm{surface}}\)} is
        defined by the two surface tangents \mbox{\(t_1\)} and \mbox{\(t_2\)} and
        the surface normal \mbox{\(n_s\)}:

        \begin{equation}
        R_{\mathrm{surface}}
        =
        \begin{bmatrix}
          t_1 & t_2 & n_s
        \end{bmatrix}.
        \label{eq:surface_frame_overview}
        \end{equation}

        The directions \mbox{\(t_1\)} and \mbox{\(t_2\)} lie in the surface plane and are
        mutually orthogonal. The unit normal \mbox{\(n_s\)} is defined to point
        outward from the surface towards the free-space region above the plate.
        This sign convention is kept throughout the contact sequence, so motion
        towards the surface is along \mbox{\(-n_s\)}. The surface frame is used to
        describe the commanded tool orientation offset and to assign stiffness
        and damping values according to the two tangential directions and the
        normal direction.
\end{itemize}

\mbox{\Cref{fig:reference_frames}} shows these frames together with the pose
error between the current and the desired end-effector frame.

\section{Rigid-Body Pose and Pose Error}
\label{sec:rigid_transformations}

Unless stated otherwise, Cartesian quantities are expressed in the robot base
frame. A Cartesian pose consists of a position \mbox{\(p\in\mathbb{R}^3\)} and
an orientation \mbox{\(R\in SO(3)\)}, collected in the homogeneous
transformation \mbox{\(T\)}:

\begin{equation}
T=
\begin{bmatrix}
R & p\\
0^\top & 1
\end{bmatrix}.
\label{eq:homogeneous_transformation}
\end{equation}

The measured end-effector pose is therefore

\begin{equation}
T_{\mathrm{EE}}
=
\begin{bmatrix}
R_{\mathrm{EE}} & p_{\mathrm{EE}}\\
0^\top & 1
\end{bmatrix}.
\label{eq:end_effector_pose}
\end{equation}

The controller obtains \mbox{\(T_{\mathrm{EE}}\)} directly from the libfranka
robot state. Rotation matrices are used throughout; the pose is not converted
to Euler angles.

\subsection{Rotation Matrices}
\label{sec:rotation_matrices}

An orientation is represented by a rotation matrix
\mbox{\(R\in SO(3)\)}, satisfying

\begin{equation}
R^\top R=I,
\qquad
\det R=1,
\qquad
R^{-1}=R^\top.
\end{equation}

Here, \mbox{\(I\)} is the \mbox{\(3\times3\)} identity matrix and
\mbox{\(\det(\cdot)\)} denotes the determinant.

The notation \mbox{\({}^bR_a\)} denotes the orientation of frame \mbox{\(\{a\}\)}
expressed in frame \mbox{\(\{b\}\)}. Its columns contain the axes of frame
\mbox{\(\{a\}\)} expressed in frame \mbox{\(\{b\}\)}. A vector \mbox{\(v\)} is transformed from frame
\mbox{\(\{a\}\)} to frame \mbox{\(\{b\}\)} according to

\begin{equation}
{}^bv = {}^bR_a\,{}^av.
\label{eq:rotation_transformation}
\end{equation}

The elementary right-handed rotations use the rotation angles
\mbox{\(\alpha\)}, \mbox{\(\beta\)}, and \mbox{\(\gamma\)} about the \mbox{\(x\)}-,
\mbox{\(y\)}-, and \mbox{\(z\)}-axes, respectively:

\begin{equation}
% Tightened so the whole row plus the equation tag fits the text width: at the
% default column spacing the tag is forced onto a line of its own.
\setlength{\arraycolsep}{1pt}
R_x(\alpha)=
\begin{bmatrix}
1&0&0\\
0&\cos\alpha&-\sin\alpha\\
0&\sin\alpha&\cos\alpha
\end{bmatrix},\;
R_y(\beta)=
\begin{bmatrix}
\cos\beta&0&\sin\beta\\
0&1&0\\
-\sin\beta&0&\cos\beta
\end{bmatrix},\;
R_z(\gamma)=
\begin{bmatrix}
\cos\gamma&-\sin\gamma&0\\
\sin\gamma&\cos\gamma&0\\
0&0&1
\end{bmatrix}.
\label{eq:elementary_rotations}
\end{equation}

\subsection{Homogeneous Transformations}
\label{sec:homogeneous_transformations}

A position is represented by a vector \mbox{\(p\in\mathbb{R}^3\)}. The
homogeneous transformation \mbox{\({}^bT_a\)} combines the rotation and
translation from frame \mbox{\(\{a\}\)} to frame \mbox{\(\{b\}\)}
\mbox{\cite{Craig2005,Siciliano2009}}:

\begin{equation}
{}^bT_a=
\begin{bmatrix}
{}^bR_a & {}^bp_a\\
0^\top & 1
\end{bmatrix},
\label{eq:homogeneous_transformation}
\end{equation}

where \mbox{\({}^bp_a\)} is the position of the origin of frame \mbox{\(\{a\}\)} expressed
in frame \mbox{\(\{b\}\)}. A point expressed in frame \mbox{\(\{a\}\)} is transformed to
frame \mbox{\(\{b\}\)} according to

\begin{equation}
{}^bp
=
{}^bR_a\,{}^ap+{}^bp_a.
\label{eq:point_transformation}
\end{equation}

Successive transformations are composed by matrix multiplication:

\begin{equation}
{}^cT_a
=
{}^cT_b\,{}^bT_a.
\label{eq:transformation_composition}
\end{equation}




\subsection{End-Effector Pose}
\label{sec:position_orientation}

The controlled pose consists of the end-effector position

\begin{equation}
p_{\mathrm{EE}}
=
\begin{bmatrix}
p_{x,\mathrm{EE}} &
p_{y,\mathrm{EE}} &
p_{z,\mathrm{EE}}
\end{bmatrix}^{\top}
\in\mathbb{R}^3
\label{eq:end_effector_position}
\end{equation}

and the orientation \mbox{\(R_{\mathrm{EE}}\in SO(3)\)}. Both are expressed in the
base frame and combined in the end-effector transformation
\mbox{\(T_{\mathrm{EE}}\)}:

\begin{equation}
T_{\mathrm{EE}}
=
\begin{bmatrix}
R_{\mathrm{EE}} & p_{\mathrm{EE}}\\
0^\top & 1
\end{bmatrix}.
\label{eq:end_effector_pose}
\end{equation}

In the implementation, \mbox{\(T_{\mathrm{EE}}\)} is obtained directly from the
reported robot state, and the orientation is not converted to an Euler-angle
representation.

\subsection{Cartesian Pose Error}
\label{subsec:cartesian_pose_error}

The desired end-effector transformation \mbox{\(T_d\)} is

\begin{equation}
T_d
=
\begin{bmatrix}
R_d & p_d\\
0^\top & 1
\end{bmatrix},
\label{eq:desired_transformation}
\end{equation}

where \mbox{\(p_d\)} and \mbox{\(R_d\)} are the desired TCP position and orientation,
respectively. The current end-effector pose is denoted by
\mbox{\(T_{\mathrm{EE}}\)}.

The relative pose-error transformation \mbox{\(T_{\mathrm{err}}\)} maps the
current end-effector frame to the desired frame:

\begin{equation}
T_{\mathrm{err}}
=
T_{\mathrm{EE}}^{-1}T_d
=
\begin{bmatrix}
R_{\mathrm{EE}}^\top R_d &
R_{\mathrm{EE}}^\top(p_d-p_{\mathrm{EE}})\\
0^\top & 1
\end{bmatrix}.
\label{eq:expanded_transformation_error}
\end{equation}

When the current and desired poses coincide,
\mbox{\(T_{\mathrm{err}}=I_4\)}. The rotational and translational blocks of
\mbox{\Cref{eq:expanded_transformation_error}} therefore both represent zero error in
this case.

\paragraph{Position error.}

The translational block of \mbox{\Cref{eq:expanded_transformation_error}} expresses
the position error \mbox{\(e_p\)} in the current end-effector frame. The position error
\mbox{\(e_p\)} used by the Cartesian impedance law is expressed in the base frame:

\begin{equation}
e_p
=
p_d-p_{\mathrm{EE}}
\in\mathbb{R}^3.
\label{eq:position_control_error}
\end{equation}

A positive position error \mbox{\(e_p\)} therefore points from the current TCP position towards
the desired TCP position.

\paragraph{Orientation error.}

The relative rotation \mbox{\(\Delta R\)} is the rotational block of
\mbox{\Cref{eq:expanded_transformation_error}}:

\begin{equation}
\Delta R
=
R_{\mathrm{EE}}^\top R_d.
\label{eq:relative_rotation_error}
\end{equation}

It represents the rotation required to carry the current end-effector
orientation to the desired orientation. If both orientations coincide, then
\mbox{\(\Delta R=I\)}.

For use in the impedance controller, \mbox{\(\Delta R\)} is represented by a rotation
angle \mbox{\(\phi\)} and a unit rotation axis \mbox{\(u\in\mathbb{R}^3\)}
\mbox{\cite{Siciliano2009,Murray1994}}. The rotation-matrix and axis--angle
representations can be converted in both directions. Given a unit axis
\mbox{\(u\)} and an angle \mbox{\(\phi\)}, the corresponding rotation matrix is
obtained from Rodrigues' formula,

\begin{equation}
R(u,\phi)
=
I
+\sin\phi\,[u]_\times
+(1-\cos\phi)[u]_\times^2.
\label{eq:rodrigues_formula}
\end{equation}

For the relative rotation considered here, \mbox{\(R(u,\phi)=\Delta R\)}. The
skew-symmetric matrix of the rotation axis is

\begin{equation}
[u]_\times
=
\begin{bmatrix}
0 & -u_z & u_y\\
u_z & 0 & -u_x\\
-u_y & u_x & 0
\end{bmatrix},
\qquad
[u]_\times^\top=-[u]_\times.
\label{eq:skew_symmetric_axis_matrix}
\end{equation}

Conversely, when the rotation matrix \mbox{\(\Delta R\)} is known, the angle
follows from

\begin{equation}
\operatorname{tr}(\Delta R)
=
1+2\cos\phi,
\label{eq:trace_angle_relation}
\end{equation}

and therefore

\begin{equation}
\phi
=
\arccos\!\left(
\frac{\operatorname{tr}(\Delta R)-1}{2}
\right),
\qquad
\phi\in[0,\pi].
\label{eq:angle_from_trace}
\end{equation}

Subtracting the transpose of \mbox{\(\Delta R\)} isolates its skew-symmetric part:

\begin{equation}
\frac{1}{2}
\left(
\Delta R-\Delta R^\top
\right)
=
\sin\phi\,[u]_\times.
\label{eq:skew_part_rotation_matrix}
\end{equation}

For \mbox{\(\sin\phi\neq0\)}, the rotation axis is obtained from

\begin{equation}
u
=
\frac{1}{2\sin\phi}
\begin{bmatrix}
\Delta R_{32}-\Delta R_{23}\\
\Delta R_{13}-\Delta R_{31}\\
\Delta R_{21}-\Delta R_{12}
\end{bmatrix}.
\label{eq:rotation_axis_from_matrix}
\end{equation}

Multiplying the unit axis by the angle gives the current-frame orientation
error \mbox{\(e_{R,\mathrm{EE}}\)}. Rotating it into the base frame gives the
controller orientation error \mbox{\(e_R\)}:

\begin{equation}
e_{R,\mathrm{EE}}
=
\phi u,
\qquad
e_R
=
R_{\mathrm{EE}}e_{R,\mathrm{EE}}
\in\mathbb{R}^3.
\label{eq:rotation_error_base_frame}
\end{equation}

The direction of \mbox{\(e_R\)} defines the axis of the required correction, while
its magnitude is the angular error in radians. When the current and desired
orientations coincide, \mbox{\(\phi=0\)} and \mbox{\(e_R=0\)}. Together,
\mbox{\(e_p=p_d-p_{\mathrm{EE}}\)} and \mbox{\(e_R\)} are expressed in the
base frame and point in the translational and rotational directions required to
reduce the pose error.

The complete Cartesian error \mbox{\(e\)} stacks the position error
\mbox{\(e_p\)} and orientation error \mbox{\(e_R\)} used by the impedance controller:

\begin{equation}
e
=
\begin{bmatrix}
e_p\\
e_R
\end{bmatrix}
\in\mathbb{R}^6.
\label{eq:cartesian_error_vector}
\end{equation}


\section{Kinematics of a 7-DOF Manipulator}
\label{sec:kinematics}

Having defined the end-effector pose as the controlled variable, its relation
to the joint configuration is introduced next.

For the joint configuration \mbox{\(q\in\mathbb{R}^7\)}, forward kinematics
defines the end-effector pose \mbox{\(T_{\mathrm{EE}}(q)\)}. The
Denavit--Hartenberg convention fixes the joint and frame indexing
\mbox{\cite{Denavit1955,Craig2005}}, but the real-time controller obtains the
pose and geometric Jacobian from the libfranka model interface.

\iffalse
\subsection{Forward Kinematics}
\label{subsec:forward_kinematics}

Forward kinematics maps the joint-configuration vector \mbox{\(q\)}

\begin{equation}
q =
\begin{bmatrix}
q_1 & q_2 & \cdots & q_n
\end{bmatrix}^{\top}
\in\mathbb{R}^n
\label{eq:joint_configuration_vector}
\end{equation}

to the end-effector pose. Using the joint frames introduced in
\mbox{\Cref{sec:reference_frames}}, the transformation from the base frame
\mbox{\(\{0\}\)} to the final link frame \mbox{\(\{n\}\)} is

\begin{equation}
{}^0T_n(q)
=
{}^0T_1(q_1)\,
{}^1T_2(q_2)\cdots
{}^{n-1}T_n(q_n).
\label{eq:forward_kinematics_chain}
\end{equation}

In the standard Denavit--Hartenberg convention
\mbox{\cite{Denavit1955,Craig2005,Siciliano2009}}, joint \mbox{\(i\)} rotates by \mbox{\(q_i\)}
about the axis \mbox{\(z_{i-1}\)}. The transformation between two consecutive joint
frames is

\begin{equation}
{}^{i-1}T_i(q_i)
=
R_z(q_i)\,T_z(d_i)\,T_x(a_i)\,R_x(\alpha_i),
\label{eq:dh_elementary_transformations}
\end{equation}

where \mbox{\(a_i\)}, \mbox{\(d_i\)}, and \mbox{\(\alpha_i\)} describe the fixed geometry between
the consecutive joint frames, while \mbox{\(q_i\)} is the revolute-joint coordinate.

The complete transformation gives the end-effector pose in the base frame:

\begin{equation}
{}^0T_n(q)
=
T_{\mathrm{EE}}(q)
=
\begin{bmatrix}
R_{\mathrm{EE}}(q) & p_{\mathrm{EE}}(q)\\
0^\top & 1
\end{bmatrix}.
\label{eq:forward_kinematics_result}
\end{equation}

This convention establishes the standard kinematic relation and the joint and
frame indexing used in the subsequent Jacobian \mbox{\(J(q)\)} formulation.
The real-time controller does not reconstruct the Panda pose from these
Denavit--Hartenberg transformations. Instead, the current end-effector pose is
read from the libfranka robot state, while the Jacobian is obtained from the
libfranka model interface. The Denavit--Hartenberg formulation is therefore
used here as the theoretical kinematic basis rather than as a second
real-time kinematic implementation.

The geometric Jacobian \mbox{\(J(q)\in\mathbb{R}^{6\times7}\)} maps joint
velocity to the base-frame end-effector twist,

\begin{equation}
\begin{bmatrix}
\dot p_{\mathrm{EE}}\\
\omega_{\mathrm{EE}}
\end{bmatrix}
=J(q)\dot q.
\label{eq:differential_kinematics}
\end{equation}

Its transpose maps the commanded Cartesian wrench to joint torque. These two
mappings are the kinematic relations required by the impedance and null-space

\subsection{Differential Kinematics}
\label{subsec:differential_kinematics}

Differential kinematics maps the joint-velocity vector \mbox{\(\dot q\)} to
the end-effector twist \mbox{\(\dot x\)}:

\begin{equation}
\dot{x}
=
\begin{bmatrix}
\dot{p}_{\mathrm{EE}}\\
\omega_{\mathrm{EE}}
\end{bmatrix}
=
J(q)\dot{q},
\label{eq:differential_kinematics}
\end{equation}

where \mbox{\(\dot{p}_{\mathrm{EE}}\in\mathbb{R}^3\)} and
\mbox{\(\omega_{\mathrm{EE}}\in\mathbb{R}^3\)} are the linear and angular velocities
of the end-effector, respectively. The joint-velocity vector is

\begin{equation}
\dot{q}
=
\begin{bmatrix}
\dot{q}_1 & \dot{q}_2 & \cdots & \dot{q}_n
\end{bmatrix}^{\top}
\in\mathbb{R}^n,
\label{eq:joint_velocity_vector}
\end{equation}

and \mbox{\(J(q)\in\mathbb{R}^{6\times n}\)} is the geometric Jacobian. For the
7-\mbox{\abbr{DOF}} Panda, \mbox{\(n=7\)}. The end-effector twist and the Jacobian used in
the controller are expressed in the base frame. This relation provides the
Cartesian velocity \mbox{\(\dot{x}\)} required by the damping term of the impedance controller.


\subsection{Geometric Jacobian}
\label{subsec:geometric_jacobian}

The geometric Jacobian \mbox{\(J(q)\)} is written column-wise as

\begin{equation}
J(q)
=
\begin{bmatrix}
J_1 & J_2 & \cdots & J_n
\end{bmatrix}
\in\mathbb{R}^{6\times n},
\label{eq:geometric_jacobian}
\end{equation}

where \mbox{\(J_i\)} describes the contribution of joint \mbox{\(i\)} to the end-effector
twist. Since all joints of the Panda are revolute, each column is given by

\begin{equation}
J_i
=
\begin{bmatrix}
z_{i-1}\times
\left(p_{\mathrm{EE}}-p_{i-1}\right)\\
z_{i-1}
\end{bmatrix}
\in\mathbb{R}^6,
\label{eq:jacobian_column}
\end{equation}

where \mbox{\(z_{i-1}\)} is the axis of joint \mbox{\(i\)}, and \mbox{\(p_{i-1}\)} is a point on
that axis. Both are expressed in the base frame. The upper block describes the
linear velocity generated at the end-effector, while the lower block describes
the corresponding angular velocity
\mbox{\cite{Siciliano2009,Craig2005}}.

Because the joint axes and their positions depend on the current configuration,
the Jacobian also depends on \mbox{\(q\)}. In the implemented controller,
\mbox{\(J(q)\)} is obtained directly from the libfranka model interface.

\section{Cartesian Impedance Control}
\label{sec:cartesian_impedance_control}

The geometric Jacobian \mbox{\(J(q)\)} is also used through its transpose to map a Cartesian
wrench \mbox{\(F\)} to joint torques \mbox{\(\tau\)} \mbox{\cite{Khatib1987}}. Before introducing this mapping, the
desired Cartesian interaction behaviour is defined.

In this thesis, Cartesian impedance control is formulated as a
spring--damper relation between the end-effector motion and the commanded
wrench \mbox{\cite{Hogan1985,Ott2008,Mayr2024CartesianImpedance}}. The Cartesian pose
error \mbox{\(e\)}, defined in \mbox{\Cref{subsec:cartesian_pose_error}}, produces a
restoring wrench through the stiffness matrix \mbox{\(K\)}, while the Cartesian
velocity \mbox{\(\dot{x}\)} produces an opposing wrench through the damping matrix
\mbox{\(D\)}. Their
entries determine the translational and rotational compliance in the selected
compliance directions \mbox{\cite{Pollayil2023ImpedancePlanning}}. Physically, the
stiffness terms determine how strongly a pose error generates a restoring force
or moment, while the damping terms oppose the corresponding Cartesian motion.






\subsection{Impedance Wrench}
\label{subsec:impedance_wrench}

The impedance wrench is evaluated in the robot base frame. The corresponding
translational stiffness and damping matrices are
\mbox{\(K_{p,0},D_{p,0}\in\mathbb{R}^{3\times3}\)}, while the rotational matrices are
\mbox{\(K_{R,0},D_{R,0}\in\mathbb{R}^{3\times3}\)}. These matrices are not generally
diagonal in the base frame because the direction-dependent gains are defined
relative to the surface frame. Their base-frame representation therefore
changes with the surface-frame orientation, even when the local gain values
remain unchanged.

The commanded Cartesian force \mbox{\(f\)} is given by the translational spring--damper law
\mbox{\cite{Hogan1985,Mayr2024CartesianImpedance}}

\begin{equation}
f
=
K_{p,0}e_p
+
D_{p,0}
\left(
\dot{p}_d-\dot{p}_{\mathrm{EE}}
\right)
\in\mathbb{R}^3,
\label{eq:positional_impedance_force}
\end{equation}

where \mbox{\(\dot{p}_d\)} and \mbox{\(\dot{p}_{\mathrm{EE}}\)} are the desired and measured
linear velocities expressed in the base frame. For a fixed position setpoint,
\mbox{\(\dot{p}_d=\mathbf{0}\)}, and the damping term becomes
\mbox{\(-D_{p,0}\dot{p}_{\mathrm{EE}}\)}.

The commanded Cartesian moment \mbox{\(m\)} is

\begin{equation}
m
=
K_{R,0}e_R
-
D_{R,0}\omega_{\mathrm{EE}}
\in\mathbb{R}^3,
\label{eq:rotational_impedance_moment}
\end{equation}

where \mbox{\(\omega_{\mathrm{EE}}\)} is the measured end-effector angular velocity
expressed in the base frame. Since no angular reference velocity is commanded,
the rotational damping acts directly against
\mbox{\(\omega_{\mathrm{EE}}\)}.

The Cartesian wrench \mbox{\(F\)} stacks the force and moment:

\begin{equation}
F
=
\begin{bmatrix}
f\\
m
\end{bmatrix}
=
\begin{bmatrix}
K_{p,0}e_p
+
D_{p,0}\left(\dot{p}_d-\dot{p}_{\mathrm{EE}}\right)
\\[1mm]
K_{R,0}e_R
-
D_{R,0}\omega_{\mathrm{EE}}
\end{bmatrix}
\in\mathbb{R}^6.
\label{eq:cartesian_force_moment_vector}
\end{equation}

The wrench is ordered as force followed by moment, and all its components are
expressed in the base frame. It is mapped to joint torques
\mbox{\(\tau_{\mathrm{cart}}\)} through the
Jacobian transpose in the following subsection.

\mbox{\Cref{eq:cartesian_force_moment_vector}} defines the impedance about the
TCP, with translation and rotation decoupled.
\mbox{\Cref{sec:centre_of_compliance}} extends the same law to an impedance
defined about a displaced centre of compliance.

\subsection{Joint-Torque Control Law}
\label{sec:control_law}

By the principle of virtual work, the commanded Cartesian wrench
\mbox{\(F\)} is mapped to joint torques \mbox{\(\tau_{\mathrm{cart}}\)} through
the transpose of the geometric Jacobian \mbox{\(J(q)\)}
\mbox{\cite{Khatib1987}}:

\begin{equation}
\tau_{\mathrm{cart}}
=
J^\top(q)F,
\label{eq:joint_torque_mapping}
\end{equation}

where \mbox{\(\tau_{\mathrm{cart}}\in\mathbb{R}^n\)} is the Cartesian impedance
torque contribution and
\mbox{\(J^\top(q)\in\mathbb{R}^{n\times6}\)}. Substituting
\mbox{\Cref{eq:cartesian_force_moment_vector}} gives

\begin{equation}
\tau_{\mathrm{cart}}
=
J^\top(q)
\begin{bmatrix}
K_{p,0}e_p
+
D_{p,0}\left(\dot{p}_d-\dot{p}_{\mathrm{EE}}\right)
\\[1mm]
K_{R,0}e_R
-
D_{R,0}\omega_{\mathrm{EE}}
\end{bmatrix}.
\label{eq:cartesian_impedance_control_law}
\end{equation}

The gain matrices in this expression are the base-frame matrices obtained from
the selected surface-coordinate gains. A change in the surface-frame
orientation therefore changes the Cartesian wrench \mbox{\(F\)} and the
resulting joint-torque contribution \mbox{\(\tau_{\mathrm{cart}}\)}, even when
the local gain values remain unchanged.

\subsection{Coriolis and Gravity Compensation in libfranka}
\label{subsec:coriolis_gravity_compensation}

In a general model-compensated torque controller, the commanded joint torque
\mbox{\(\tau_{\mathrm{cmd}}\)} combines Cartesian impedance, gravity, and
Coriolis/centrifugal compensation:

\begin{equation}
\tau_{\mathrm{cmd}}
=
\tau_{\mathrm{cart}}
+
\tau_g(q)
+
\tau_c(q,\dot{q}),
\label{eq:commanded_torque}
\end{equation}

where \mbox{\(\tau_g(q)\)} is the gravity torque and
\mbox{\(\tau_c(q,\dot{q})\)} is the Coriolis and centrifugal torque.

These terms are handled differently by the Franka Control Interface. The
Coriolis vector provided by the libfranka model is added explicitly to the
command because it is not compensated internally. A separate gravity term is
not added. In external joint-torque mode, the robot compensates gravity and
motor friction internally, and the commanded torque is interpreted as excluding
gravity \mbox{\cite{FrankaFCI2023}}. Adding \mbox{\(\tau_g(q)\)} would therefore compensate
gravity twice.

The implemented commanded torque \mbox{\(\tau_{\mathrm{cmd,impl}}\)} is consequently

\begin{equation}
\tau_{\mathrm{cmd,impl}}
=
\tau_{\mathrm{cart}}
+
\tau_c(q,\dot{q})
=
J^\top(q)F
+
\tau_c(q,\dot{q}),
\label{eq:commanded_torque_libfranka}
\end{equation}

which follows the official libfranka Cartesian impedance example
\mbox{\cite{libfrankaCartesianExample}}. The null-space torque
\mbox{\(\tau_{\mathrm{null}}\)} introduced later is
added to this expression to obtain the complete commanded torque in
\mbox{\Cref{eq:nullspace_complete_torque_command}}.

\section{Surface Frame}
\label{subsec:surface_task_frame}

The surface frame provides the tangential and normal directions used for the
Cartesian controller quantities. Its orientation is constructed from the unit
surface normal \mbox{\(n_s\)}, expressed in the robot base frame. The sign of
\mbox{\(n_s\)} is chosen so that it points outward from the surface towards the
free-space region above the plate, which makes \mbox{\(-n_s\)} the approach and
set-up direction.

The first surface tangent is chosen to follow the base-frame
\mbox{\(x_0\)}-direction as closely as possible while remaining in the surface
plane. Since the surface is not exactly parallel to the base
\mbox{\(x_0y_0\)}-plane, \mbox{\(e_{x_0}\)} is projected onto the surface plane
and normalised:

\begin{equation}
t_1
=
\frac{e_{x_0}-n_s\left(n_s^\top e_{x_0}\right)}
{\left\|e_{x_0}-n_s\left(n_s^\top e_{x_0}\right)\right\|}.
\label{eq:surface_frame_tangents}
\end{equation}

The second tangent completes the right-handed frame,

\begin{equation}
t_2=n_s\times t_1 .
\label{eq:surface_frame_second_tangent}
\end{equation}

The construction requires \mbox{\(e_{x_0}\)} not to be parallel to
\mbox{\(n_s\)}, and \mbox{\(t_1\)} and \mbox{\(t_2\)} are then orthogonal unit
vectors in the surface plane. Because the plane and its normal do not depend on
that reference direction, a different in-plane reference would rotate
\mbox{\(t_1\)} and \mbox{\(t_2\)} within the surface without changing the
surface geometry, so an axis-specific result holds with respect to the
direction that was configured, the first-tangent hint recorded in
\mbox{\Cref{app:controller_parameters}}. The tangents obtained for the reported
experiments are given in \mbox{\Cref{sec:surface_tool_calibration}}.

The orientation of the surface frame relative to the robot base frame is

\begin{equation}
R_{\mathrm{surface}}
=
\begin{bmatrix}
t_1 & t_2 & n_s
\end{bmatrix}.
\label{eq:surface_task_frame_matrix}
\end{equation}

Surface-frame quantities therefore use the coordinate order

\[
\begin{bmatrix}
t_1 & t_2 & n_s
\end{bmatrix}^\top .
\]

This convention is used consistently for the directional Cartesian quantities.
For example, \mbox{\(K_{p,t_1}\)}, \mbox{\(K_{p,t_2}\)}, and
\mbox{\(K_{p,n}\)} denote the translational stiffness entries along the two
surface tangents and the surface normal, respectively. The same ordering is
used for the rotational gains and for surface-frame components of the
compliance-centre displacement.


\section{Stiffness and Damping Representation}
\label{sec:stiffness_damping_representation}

The Cartesian impedance law of \mbox{\Cref{sec:cartesian_impedance_control}} is
evaluated in the robot base frame. The directional stiffness and damping values
are specified relative to the calibrated surface, because the relevant
directions for the contact problem are the two surface tangents
\mbox{\(t_1\)} and \mbox{\(t_2\)} and the surface normal \mbox{\(n_s\)}.

The translational and rotational stiffness and damping matrices are first
defined in surface coordinates as

\begin{align}
K_{p,S}
&=
\begin{bmatrix}
K_{p,t_1} & 0 & 0\\
0 & K_{p,t_2} & 0\\
0 & 0 & K_{p,n}
\end{bmatrix},
&
D_{p,S}
&=
\begin{bmatrix}
D_{p,t_1} & 0 & 0\\
0 & D_{p,t_2} & 0\\
0 & 0 & D_{p,n}
\end{bmatrix},
\label{eq:surface_gain_blocks_translational}
\\[1.2ex]
K_{R,S}
&=
\begin{bmatrix}
K_{R,t_1} & 0 & 0\\
0 & K_{R,t_2} & 0\\
0 & 0 & K_{R,n}
\end{bmatrix},
&
D_{R,S}
&=
\begin{bmatrix}
D_{R,t_1} & 0 & 0\\
0 & D_{R,t_2} & 0\\
0 & 0 & D_{R,n}
\end{bmatrix}.
\label{eq:full_task_frame_gain_blocks}
\end{align}

The common axis order is \mbox{\([t_1,t_2,n_s]\)}.

For use in the base-frame impedance law, these matrices are rotated into the
robot base frame:

\begin{equation}
K_{p,0}
=
R_{\mathrm{surface}}K_{p,S}R_{\mathrm{surface}}^\top,
\qquad
D_{p,0}
=
R_{\mathrm{surface}}D_{p,S}R_{\mathrm{surface}}^\top,
\label{eq:spatial_gain_matrix}
\end{equation}
\begin{equation}
K_{R,0}
=
R_{\mathrm{surface}}K_{R,S}R_{\mathrm{surface}}^\top,
\qquad
D_{R,0}
=
R_{\mathrm{surface}}D_{R,S}R_{\mathrm{surface}}^\top.
\label{eq:general_task_frame_gain_transform}
\end{equation}

The complete base-frame stiffness and damping matrices are then

\begin{equation}
K_0
=
\begin{bmatrix}
K_{p,0} & 0\\
0 & K_{R,0}
\end{bmatrix},
\qquad
D_0
=
\begin{bmatrix}
D_{p,0} & 0\\
0 & D_{R,0}
\end{bmatrix}.
\label{eq:full_task_frame_gain_transform}
\end{equation}

At this stage translation and rotation remain decoupled. The
centre-of-compliance transformation introduced in
\mbox{\Cref{sec:centre_of_compliance}} can subsequently introduce coupling
between the two blocks.

The transformation changes only the coordinate representation of the gain
matrices. The physical directions associated with the gains remain tied to the
calibrated surface. For example, \mbox{\(K_{p,n}\)} acts along \mbox{\(n_s\)},
even when the surface normal is not aligned with a robot-base axis.

For the reported contact experiments, the directional gain values were assigned
along \mbox{\([t_1,t_2,n_s]\)} and then transformed to the base frame before
evaluating the Cartesian impedance law. The gains therefore remained fixed
relative to the calibrated surface and did not rotate with the current
end-effector orientation \mbox{\(R_{\mathrm{EE}}\)}. The phase-indexed numerical
entries are given in \mbox{\Cref{tab:experimental_common_gains}}.

\section{Centre of Compliance}
\label{sec:centre_of_compliance}

The surface-frame transformations of
\mbox{\Cref{eq:spatial_gain_matrix,eq:general_task_frame_gain_transform}}
change the directions along
which the Cartesian stiffness and damping act, while leaving their reference
point at the tool centre point (TCP). Independently of that orientation choice,
the Cartesian impedance can be defined about a virtual \abbr{CoC}
\mbox{\(p_c\)}. Shifting this point away from the TCP introduces
translation--rotation coupling into the Cartesian impedance
\mbox{\cite{Murray1994,Fasse1997SpatialImpedance,Stramigioli2000IPC,LynchPark2017}}.
The construction is applied during contact set-up, where it lets the normal
pressing action generate a rotational moment as well as a translational contact
load.

For a dual-arm assembly task, Suomalainen \mbox{et al.\ }experimentally
showed that compliance-centre placement and compliant-axis selection affect the
alignment response \mbox{\citep{Suomalainen2019DualArmCompliance}}. This
experimental result motivates the placement study in this thesis. The point-shift
adjoint below remains derived from the spatial-transformation formulation.

The displacement from the TCP to the \abbr{CoC} is

\begin{equation}
r_c
=
p_c-p_{\mathrm{TCP}}
\in\R{3},
\qquad
p_{\mathrm{TCP}}=p_{\mathrm{EE}}.
\label{eq:pole_to_tcp_lever}
\end{equation}

The vector \mbox{\(r_c\)} describes only the virtual shift of the impedance
reference point.

Let \mbox{\([r_c]_\times\)} denote the skew-symmetric matrix satisfying
\mbox{\([r_c]_\times a=r_c\times a\)}. Under the local small-angle
formulation, the translational error referred to the \abbr{CoC} is

\begin{equation}
e_{p,c}
=
e_p-[r_c]_\times e_R.
\label{eq:pole_small_motion_error}
\end{equation}

The negative sign follows from the selected lever convention. The vector from
the TCP to \mbox{\(p_c\)} is \mbox{\(r_c\)} itself, and therefore

\[
e_R\times r_c
=
-[r_c]_\times e_R.
\]

The stacked error at the \abbr{CoC} collects \mbox{\(e_{p,c}\)} and the
rotational error \mbox{\(e_R\)}. The point-shift adjoint
\mbox{\(\mathrm{Ad}(r_c)\)} maps the corresponding TCP error onto it:

\begin{equation}
e_c
=
\begin{bmatrix}
e_{p,c}\\[2pt]
e_R
\end{bmatrix}
=
\mathrm{Ad}(r_c)
\begin{bmatrix}
e_p\\[2pt]
e_R
\end{bmatrix},
\qquad
\mathrm{Ad}(r_c)
=
\begin{bmatrix}
I_3 & -[r_c]_\times\\[2pt]
0 & I_3
\end{bmatrix}.
\label{eq:offset_adjoint}
\end{equation}

The surface-frame rotation and the point shift have different functions.
The transformations in
\mbox{\Cref{eq:spatial_gain_matrix,eq:general_task_frame_gain_transform}}
change the principal directions of the impedance. The point-shift adjoint
\mbox{\(\mathrm{Ad}(r_c)\)} changes the point about which the impedance is
defined.

After the gains have been expressed in the base frame, the translational and
rotational stiffness and damping matrices are assembled at the \abbr{CoC} as

\begin{equation}
K_c
=
\begin{bmatrix}
K_p & 0\\
0 & K_R
\end{bmatrix},
\qquad
D_c
=
\begin{bmatrix}
D_p & 0\\
0 & D_R
\end{bmatrix}.
\label{eq:pole_block_gains}
\end{equation}

All blocks in \mbox{\Cref{eq:pole_block_gains}} are expressed in the base-frame
orientation. The compliance-centre wrench is \mbox{\(F_c=K_ce_c\)}, and
transforming this power-conjugate wrench gives the corresponding TCP wrench
\mbox{\(F_{\mathrm{TCP}}=\mathrm{Ad}(r_c)^\top F_c\)}. The matrices at the TCP
therefore follow from the congruence transformations

\begin{equation}
K_{\mathrm{TCP}}
=
\mathrm{Ad}(r_c)^\top
K_c
\mathrm{Ad}(r_c)
=
\begin{bmatrix}
K_p
&
-K_p[r_c]_\times
\\[4pt]
-[r_c]_\times^\top K_p
&
K_R+[r_c]_\times^\top K_p[r_c]_\times
\end{bmatrix}
\label{eq:coupled_tcp_stiffness}
\end{equation}

and

\begin{equation}
D_{\mathrm{TCP}}
=
\mathrm{Ad}(r_c)^\top
D_c
\mathrm{Ad}(r_c)
=
\begin{bmatrix}
D_p
&
-D_p[r_c]_\times
\\[4pt]
-[r_c]_\times^\top D_p
&
D_R+[r_c]_\times^\top D_p[r_c]_\times
\end{bmatrix}.
\label{eq:coupled_tcp_damping}
\end{equation}

The off-diagonal blocks introduce translation--rotation coupling. A
translational position or velocity error can therefore contribute to the
commanded moment, while a rotational error or velocity can contribute to the
commanded force. For \mbox{\(r_c=0\)} the point shift disappears and the
block-diagonal impedance at the TCP is recovered.

\subsection{Commanded Moment}
\label{subsec:coc_commanded_moment}

The Cartesian wrench generated by the impedance controller is
\mbox{\(F=[f^\top,\ m^\top]^\top\)}, where \mbox{\(f\)} and \mbox{\(m\)}
denote the complete commanded force and moment at the TCP.

Without a \abbr{CoC} displacement, the commanded moment is given by the
ordinary rotational impedance,

\begin{equation}
m_R=K_Re_R-D_R\omega_{\mathrm{EE}}.
\label{eq:zero_lever_commanded_moment}
\end{equation}

A non-zero \abbr{CoC} displacement adds the translation--rotation coupling
contribution \mbox{\(r_c\times f\)}, generated by the complete commanded
force. The complete commanded TCP moment is therefore

\begin{equation}
m=m_R+r_c\times f.
\label{eq:commanded_moment_decomposition}
\end{equation}

This relation is exact for the point-shifted stiffness and damping matrices
above. The controller evaluates the complete shifted spring--damper wrench and
does not separately command an elastic or a damping coupling moment.

For \mbox{\(r_c=0\)} the coupling contribution vanishes and
\mbox{\(m=m_R\)}. The TCP-centred condition is therefore a zero-coupling
condition rather than a zero-moment condition. The rotational compliance
remains finite and the commanded rotational impedance remains active.

  \centering

  \caption[Compliance-centre displacement and commanded TCP moment]{
  Centre-of-compliance displacement and commanded TCP moment. Shifting the
  virtual centre from the TCP by \mbox{\(r_c\)} introduces the coupling
  contribution \mbox{\(r_c\times f\)}.}
  \label{fig:moment_bookkeeping}
\end{figure}


\subsection{Surface-Frame and Tangential Compliance-Centre Displacement}
\label{subsec:coc_tangential_displacement}

For the surface-contact task the \abbr{CoC} displacement is resolved along the
two surface tangents and the surface normal. Its coordinates in the surface
frame are

\begin{equation}
r_{c,S}
=
\begin{bmatrix}
r_{c,t_1}\\
r_{c,t_2}\\
r_{c,n}
\end{bmatrix}.
\label{eq:coc_surface_components}
\end{equation}

Since \mbox{\(R_{\mathrm{surface}}=[\,t_1\;\;t_2\;\;n_s\,]\)} holds those
surface axes expressed in the base frame, the same physical displacement in
base coordinates is

\begin{equation}
r_{c,0}
=
R_{\mathrm{surface}}r_{c,S}
=
r_{c,t_1}t_1+r_{c,t_2}t_2+r_{c,n}n_s,
\label{eq:coc_surface_to_base}
\end{equation}

with the inverse relation
\mbox{\(r_{c,S}=R_{\mathrm{surface}}^\top r_{c,0}\)}. The tangential part
collects the two in-plane components,

\begin{equation}
r_{c,t}
=
r_{c,t_1}t_1+r_{c,t_2}t_2,
\label{eq:coc_tangential_component}
\end{equation}

while \mbox{\(r_{c,n}n_s\)} is the part along the surface normal. The
quantities have to be kept apart: \mbox{\(r_{c,t_1}\)} and
\mbox{\(r_{c,t_2}\)} are signed scalar coordinates, \mbox{\(r_{c,t}\)} is
the tangential vector they form, and its magnitude is written
\mbox{\(\lVert r_{c,t}\rVert\)}. For a purely tangential displacement
\mbox{\(r_{c,n}=0\)}, and therefore
\mbox{\(\lVert r_c\rVert=\lVert r_{c,t}\rVert\)}.

A non-zero displacement is specified by the frame in which its components are
held constant as well as by its magnitude and direction. The two definitions
follow the same pattern and differ only in the rotation that carries the
configured vector into the base frame:

\begin{equation}
r_{c,0}=R_{\mathrm{surface}}r_{c,S},
\qquad
r_{c,0}=R_{\mathrm{EE}}r_{c,\mathrm{EE}}.
\label{eq:coc_surface_fixed_definition}
\end{equation}

For a surface-fixed definition \mbox{\(r_{c,S}\)} remains constant, and
because the surface frame is fixed for one controller session the base-frame
displacement is constant during set-up. For a tool-fixed definition
\mbox{\(r_{c,\mathrm{EE}}\)} remains constant instead, so
\mbox{\(r_{c,0}\)} rotates with the end effector.
\mbox{\Cref{sec:self_alignment_virtual_center}} describes both
implementations.

The commanded force enters through \mbox{\(r_c\times f\)}, and
it decomposes exactly into a normal and a tangential component,

\begin{equation}
f=f_n+f_t,
\qquad
f_n=F_n n_s,
\qquad
f_t=f-f_n,
\label{eq:commanded_force_normal_split}
\end{equation}

with \mbox{\(F_n=n_s^\top f\)} the signed commanded normal force. The normal
component is the projection of \mbox{\(f\)} onto \mbox{\(n_s\)}, and the
tangential component is what remains of \mbox{\(f\)} in the surface plane. The
complete \abbr{CoC} contribution is therefore

\begin{equation}
r_c\times f
=
r_c\times f_n
+
r_c\times f_t.
\label{eq:coc_moment_normal_tangential}
\end{equation}

Both terms remain part of the complete commanded moment \mbox{\(m\)}.

The normal-press term simplifies. Substituting
\mbox{\(r_c=r_{c,t}+r_{c,n}n_s\)} and using
\mbox{\(f_n\parallel n_s\)}, the normal component of the displacement drops
out, \mbox{\(r_{c,n}n_s\times f_n=\mathbf{0}\)}, which leaves

\begin{equation}
r_c\times f_n
=
r_{c,t}\times f_n.
\label{eq:coc_normal_press_tangential_only}
\end{equation}

Only the tangential part of the displacement produces a moment from the
commanded normal press. A displacement along the surface normal contributes
nothing to this term. Consequently, the moment generated by the normal press
depends on the tangential projection \mbox{\(r_{c,t}\)}, not on the total
distance \mbox{\(\lVert r_c\rVert\)} between the TCP and the \abbr{CoC}.

The right-handed surface frame satisfies

\begin{equation}
t_1\times t_2=n_s,
\qquad
t_2\times n_s=t_1,
\qquad
t_1\times n_s=-t_2.
\label{eq:surface_frame_cross_product_relations}
\end{equation}

Let the commanded orientation offset in the surface plane be

\begin{equation}
\theta_{\mathrm{tilt}}
=
\theta_{t_1}t_1+\theta_{t_2}t_2.
\label{eq:commanded_offset_surface_components}
\end{equation}

For a prescribed tangential magnitude \mbox{\(\lVert r_{c,t}\rVert\)}, the
displacement used to assist the commanded alignment rotation is chosen
perpendicular to the orientation-offset direction,

\begin{equation}
r_{c,t}
=
\lVert r_{c,t}\rVert
\frac{
\theta_{t_1}t_2-\theta_{t_2}t_1
}{
\sqrt{\theta_{t_1}^2+\theta_{t_2}^2}
}.
\label{eq:general_compliance_lever_selection}
\end{equation}

The choice fixes the direction; the magnitude remains a free parameter of the
configuration. Using
\mbox{\Cref{eq:surface_frame_cross_product_relations}}, the normal-press
contribution becomes

\begin{equation}
r_{c,t}\times f_n
=
\lVert r_{c,t}\rVert F_n
\frac{
\theta_{t_1}t_1+\theta_{t_2}t_2
}{
\sqrt{\theta_{t_1}^2+\theta_{t_2}^2}
}.
\label{eq:general_compliance_lever_moment}
\end{equation}

During the set-up press \mbox{\(F_n<0\)}, so this contribution acts opposite to
\mbox{\(\theta_{\mathrm{tilt}}\)}. For the two principal directions the rule
reduces to

\begin{equation}
r_{c,t}=+\lVert r_{c,t}\rVert t_2
\quad\text{for }+\theta_{t_1},
\qquad
r_{c,t}=-\lVert r_{c,t}\rVert t_1
\quad\text{for }+\theta_{t_2},
\label{eq:principal_lever_selection}
\end{equation}

giving \mbox{\(r_{c,t}\times f_n=\lVert r_{c,t}\rVert F_nt_1\)} and
\mbox{\(r_{c,t}\times f_n=\lVert r_{c,t}\rVert F_nt_2\)} respectively.
Reversing the orientation offset reverses \mbox{\(r_{c,t}\)} and therefore
reverses this contribution.
\mbox{\Cref{fig:compliance_lever_moment}} shows the geometry for the two signs.

\begin{figure}[H]
  \centering
  \input{figures/compliance_lever_moment.tex}
  \caption[Normal-force contribution for opposite tangential displacements]{
  Normal-force contribution to the compliance-centre coupling moment for
  opposite tangential displacements: (a) the displacement selected from the
  orientation offset; (b) the displacement with the opposite sign.}
  \label{fig:compliance_lever_moment}
\end{figure}

The complete commanded moment remains \mbox{\(m=m_R+r_c\times f\)}. The
normal-force decomposition is used only to explain the directional effect of
the \abbr{CoC} displacement. Tangential commanded-force components and damping
effects remain contained in the complete wrench generated by the controller.

The coupling vanishes for \mbox{\(r_c=0\)}. Conversely, if \mbox{\(K_p\)} is
nonsingular, zero coupling implies \mbox{\(r_c=0\)}. Since
\mbox{\(\mathrm{Ad}(r_c)\)} is invertible, the congruence transformation also
preserves definiteness:

\begin{equation}
K_c\succeq0
\ \Longrightarrow\
K_{\mathrm{TCP}}\succeq0,
\qquad
K_c\succ0
\ \Longrightarrow\
K_{\mathrm{TCP}}\succ0.
\label{eq:pole_gain_definiteness}
\end{equation}

The same statements apply to \mbox{\(D_c\)} and \mbox{\(D_{\mathrm{TCP}}\)}.
Negative off-diagonal entries therefore represent coupling terms rather than
negative stiffness directions. Closed-loop robot--controller--contact behaviour
with phase switching was assessed experimentally under the operating conditions
reported in \mbox{\Cref{ch:methodology}}.

This formulation is a local small-motion model in which the displacement is
treated as fixed during one controller evaluation. A surface-fixed
\mbox{\(r_{c,S}\)} therefore keeps \mbox{\(r_{c,0}\)} constant during
set-up, whereas a tool-fixed displacement does not, and the controller updates
the base-frame vector and the shifted gain matrices during each control cycle
in that case.

The virtual \abbr{CoC} \mbox{\(p_c\)} is consequently a selected impedance
reference point and is not interpreted as a measured physical pivot. It need
not coincide with the physical contact location. The resulting contact response
additionally depends on the finite rotational stiffness, the contact
constraint, friction, the robot configuration, and tool-mount compliance.

\section{Null-Space Torque}
\label{sec:nullspace_svd}

The Panda has one redundant joint direction when its
\mbox{\(6\times7\)} geometric Jacobian \mbox{\(J(q)\)} has full row rank. The
singular value decomposition (\mbox{\abbr{SVD}}) supplies two different
quantities: \mbox{\(v_7\)} spans the redundant direction, while
\mbox{\(\sigma_{\min}=\sigma_6\)} indicates the weakest task-producing
mapping. A thresholded pseudoinverse gives the velocity and torque projectors.
The controller then combines projected joint damping with a two-probe
conditioning torque.

\subsection{SVD and Null-Space Direction}
\label{subsec:svd_nullspace_characterisation}

The decomposition of the current Jacobian is

\begin{equation}
J(q)=U_J\Sigma_JV_J^\top,
\label{eq:jacobian_svd}
\end{equation}

where the six task-producing singular values satisfy
\mbox{\(\sigma_1\geq\cdots\geq\sigma_6\geq0\)}. At full row rank, the
seventh right-singular vector satisfies

\begin{equation}
Jv_7=0,
\label{eq:nullspace_search_direction}
\end{equation}

and spans the one-dimensional kinematic null space. The conditioning indicator
is

\begin{equation}
\sigma_{\min}=\sigma_6.
\label{eq:min_singular_value}
\end{equation}

The vector \mbox{\(v_7\)} and scalar \mbox{\(\sigma_6\)} therefore have
different roles. The first defines redundant motion; the second describes the
weakest of the six task mappings. Because the geometric Jacobian mixes linear
and angular rows, \mbox{\(\sigma_{\min}\)} is used only as a relative indicator
under one fixed Jacobian convention.

\subsection{Pseudoinverse and Projectors}
\label{subsec:svd_pseudoinverse}
\label{subsec:nullspace_projectors}

A relative tolerance \mbox{\(\rho\)} defines the retained singular values by
\mbox{\(\sigma_i>\rho\sigma_1\)}. The corresponding Moore--Penrose
pseudoinverse is

\begin{equation}
J^+=
\sum_{\sigma_i>\rho\sigma_1}^{6}
\frac{1}{\sigma_i}v_i u_i^\top.
\label{eq:svd_pseudoinverse}
\end{equation}

The joint-velocity projector extracts redundant motion,

\begin{equation}
N_q=I_7-J^+J,
\qquad
\dot q_{\mathrm{null}}=N_q\dot q,
\label{eq:nullspace_velocity_projector}
\end{equation}

while the transpose projector applies a secondary joint torque without a
first-order task component,

\begin{equation}
N_\tau=N_q^\top=I_7-J^\top J^{+\top}.
\label{eq:nullspace_torque_projector}
\end{equation}

With the thresholded \mbox{\abbr{SVD}} construction, the projector is
orthogonal and symmetric. At full row rank it reduces to
\mbox{\(N_q=N_\tau=v_7v_7^\top\)}. The implemented separation is kinematic
rather than inertia weighted; Cartesian position retention is therefore checked
in the pose-hold experiment.

\subsection{Projected Damping and Conditioning}
\label{subsec:nullspace_damping_torque}
\label{subsec:singular_value_conditioning_torque}

Projected null-space damping opposes the redundant joint velocity,

\begin{equation}
\tau_d=-d_{\mathrm{null}}N_\tau\dot q.
\label{eq:nullspace_damping_torque}
\end{equation}

The conditioning term samples two configurations along the current redundant
direction. For the probe angle \mbox{\(\alpha_{\mathrm{probe}}\)}, these are

\begin{equation}
q_+=q+\alpha_{\mathrm{probe}}v_7,
\qquad
q_-=q-\alpha_{\mathrm{probe}}v_7.
\label{eq:nullspace_candidates}
\end{equation}

Their minimum singular values are

\begin{equation}
\sigma_+=\sigma_{\min}(J(q_+)),
\qquad
\sigma_-=\sigma_{\min}(J(q_-)),
\label{eq:nullspace_sampled_singular_values}
\end{equation}

with the sampled difference

\begin{equation}
\Delta_\sigma=\sigma_+-\sigma_-.
\label{eq:nullspace_singular_value_difference}
\end{equation}

The direction selector applies a deadband \mbox{\(\delta_\sigma\)},

\begin{equation}
s_\sigma=
\begin{cases}
+1,&\Delta_\sigma>\delta_\sigma,\\
-1,&\Delta_\sigma<-\delta_\sigma,\\
0,&|\Delta_\sigma|\leq\delta_\sigma,
\end{cases}
\label{eq:nullspace_direction_selection}
\end{equation}

and the conditioning torque is

\begin{equation}
\tau_\sigma=k_\sigma N_\tau(s_\sigma v_7).
\label{eq:nullspace_sigma_torque}
\end{equation}

Sampling \mbox{\(q_+\)} and \mbox{\(q_-\)} selects the neighbouring
configuration with the larger \mbox{\(\sigma_{\min}\)}. The finite probes are
local tangent approximations to the nonlinear self-motion. The commanded
magnitude \mbox{\(k_\sigma\)} is independent of the probe angle.

\subsection{Complete Null-Space Torque}
\label{subsec:complete_nullspace_torque}

The complete secondary torque is

\begin{equation}
\tau_{\mathrm{null}}
=\tau_d+\tau_\sigma
=-d_{\mathrm{null}}N_\tau\dot q
+k_\sigma N_\tau(s_\sigma v_7).
\label{eq:nullspace_torque}
\end{equation}

The damping term acts only while redundant motion is present. The conditioning
term is an active configuration objective and can act at zero joint velocity.
Its direction is invariant to the arbitrary sign returned for \mbox{\(v_7\)},
because reversing \mbox{\(v_7\)} also exchanges the two probes and reverses
\mbox{\(s_\sigma\)}.

\section{Null-Space Torque}
\label{sec:nullspace_svd}

The Franka Emika Panda has seven joints, while the controlled end-effector task
contains six components: three translational and three rotational. The robot is
therefore kinematically redundant. The Cartesian impedance behaviour at the
end-effector is the primary objective, and the remaining joint-space freedom can
be used for a secondary objective.

In this thesis, the secondary joint-space action consists of a null-space
damping contribution and an active singular-value conditioning contribution.
Both are projected using the joint-torque null-space projector \mbox{\(N_\tau\)}.
For the full-row-rank \mbox{\(6\times7\)} Panda Jacobian, one joint-space
direction remains after the six task-producing directions are accounted for.
The singular value decomposition (\mbox{\abbr{SVD}}) is used here for two
distinct purposes: \mbox{\(v_7\)} identifies this redundant direction, while
\mbox{\(\sigma_6\)} describes the weakest task-producing mapping. The controller
damps motion in the projected null space and can additionally drive the robot
toward the sampled null-space direction with the larger \mbox{\(\sigma_6\)}.
The required null-space direction, pseudoinverse, and projector are introduced
before the two torque contributions are defined.


\subsection{SVD-Based Null-Space Characterisation}
\label{subsec:svd_nullspace_characterisation}

The geometric Jacobian \mbox{\(J(q)\)} relates the current joint velocity \mbox{\(\dot q\)} to the
end-effector velocity \mbox{\(\dot x\)}:

\begin{equation}
\dot{x}
=
J(q)\dot q,
\qquad
J(q)\in\mathbb{R}^{6\times7},
\label{eq:nullspace_jacobian_relation}
\end{equation}

where

\begin{equation}
\dot{x}
=
\begin{bmatrix}
\dot p_{\mathrm{EE}}\\
\omega_{\mathrm{EE}}
\end{bmatrix}
\in\mathbb{R}^6
\label{eq:nullspace_end_effector_velocity}
\end{equation}

contains the three linear and three angular velocity components of the
end-effector.

A joint velocity \mbox{\(\dot q\)} belongs to the kinematic null space when

\begin{equation}
J(q)\dot q
=
0.
\label{eq:jacobian_nullspace_condition}
\end{equation}

Such a joint velocity \mbox{\(\dot q\)} changes the robot configuration while preserving the
end-effector position and orientation to first order. The Jacobian
\mbox{\(J(q)\)} is the
first-order term in the expansion of the forward kinematics about the current
configuration, so a finite displacement along the null space leaves a pose error
of second order in its magnitude. When \mbox{\(J(q)\)} has full
row rank, the null space of the \mbox{\(6\times7\)} Jacobian \mbox{\(J(q)\)} is
one-dimensional.

The \mbox{\abbr{SVD}} is applied to the Jacobian
\mbox{\(J(q)\)} at
the current joint configuration \mbox{\(q\)}
\mbox{\cite{Chiaverini1994DampedLeastSquares,Dietrich2015NullspaceSurvey}}:

\begin{equation}
J(q)
=
U_J\Sigma_JV_J^\top,
\qquad
U_J\in\mathbb{R}^{6\times6},
\quad
\Sigma_J\in\mathbb{R}^{6\times7},
\quad
V_J\in\mathbb{R}^{7\times7}.
\label{eq:jacobian_svd}
\end{equation}

The Jacobian \mbox{\(J(q)\)} is the input to the decomposition. The \mbox{\abbr{SVD}}
returns the matrices \mbox{\(U_J\)}, \mbox{\(\Sigma_J\)}, and \mbox{\(V_J\)}, with the singular
values contained in \mbox{\(\Sigma_J\)}.

The columns of \mbox{\(V_J\)} form an orthonormal basis of the
seven-dimensional joint-velocity space. The joint velocity \mbox{\(\dot q\)} can
therefore be expressed as

\begin{equation}
\dot q
=
V_Jc
=
\sum_{i=1}^{7}c_i v_i,
\label{eq:joint_velocity_svd_basis}
\end{equation}

where \mbox{\(v_i\)} is the \mbox{\(i\)}-th column of \mbox{\(V_J\)}, and
\mbox{\(c\in\mathbb{R}^7\)} contains the components of \mbox{\(\dot q\)} along these basis
directions.

Similarly, the columns of \mbox{\(U_J\)} form an orthonormal basis of the
six-dimensional end-effector-velocity space. Substituting
\mbox{\(\dot q=V_Jc\)} into \mbox{\Cref{eq:nullspace_jacobian_relation}} gives

\begin{equation}
\dot x
=
J(q)V_Jc
=
U_J\Sigma_Jc.
\label{eq:end_effector_velocity_svd_basis}
\end{equation}

Thus, \mbox{\(V_J\)} provides the basis directions in which the joint velocity
\mbox{\(\dot q\)} is expressed, while \mbox{\(U_J\)} provides the corresponding basis
directions in which the end-effector velocity \mbox{\(\dot x\)} is expressed. The
matrix \mbox{\(\Sigma_J\)} scales the mapping between these directions.

For the \mbox{\(6\times7\)} Jacobian \mbox{\(J(q)\)}, the ordered Jacobian singular values
\mbox{\(\sigma_i\)} occupy the diagonal of the singular-value matrix:

\begin{equation}
\Sigma_J
=
\begin{bmatrix}
\operatorname{diag}
\left(
\sigma_1,\sigma_2,\ldots,\sigma_6
\right)
&
0_{6\times1}
\end{bmatrix},
\label{eq:singular_value_matrix_structure}
\end{equation}

where

\begin{equation}
\sigma_1
\geq
\sigma_2
\geq
\cdots
\geq
\sigma_6
\geq
0.
\label{eq:ordered_singular_values}
\end{equation}

For the first six basis directions,

\begin{equation}
Jv_i
=
\sigma_i u_i,
\qquad
i=1,\ldots,6,
\label{eq:svd_direction_mapping}
\end{equation}

where \mbox{\(u_i\)} is the \mbox{\(i\)}-th column of \mbox{\(U_J\)}. A joint velocity
\mbox{\(\dot q\)} in the
direction \mbox{\(v_i\)},

\begin{equation}
\dot q
=
c_i v_i,
\end{equation}

therefore produces the end-effector velocity

\begin{equation}
\dot x
=
c_i\sigma_i u_i.
\end{equation}

The singular value \mbox{\(\sigma_i\)} scales the mapping from the joint-velocity
basis direction \mbox{\(v_i\)} to the corresponding end-effector-velocity basis
direction \mbox{\(u_i\)}.

The seventh column of \mbox{\(V_J\)} corresponds to the zero column of
\mbox{\(\Sigma_J\)} and therefore satisfies

\begin{equation}
Jv_7
=
0.
\label{eq:nullspace_search_direction}
\end{equation}

A null-space joint velocity \mbox{\(\dot q\)} may be scaled by the scalar amplitude
\mbox{\(c_7\)}:

\begin{equation}
\dot q
=
c_7v_7
\end{equation}

therefore changes the joint configuration without producing an
end-effector velocity. When the Jacobian \mbox{\(J(q)\)} has full row rank,
\mbox{\(v_7\)} spans its
one-dimensional kinematic null space.

The minimum task-producing singular value \mbox{\(\sigma_{\min}\)} is

\begin{equation}
\sigma_{\min}
=
\sigma_6.
\label{eq:min_singular_value}
\end{equation}

The quantities \mbox{\(v_7\)} and \mbox{\(\sigma_6\)} have different purposes. The vector
\mbox{\(v_7\)} represents the redundant joint-motion direction, whereas
\mbox{\(\sigma_6\)} describes the weakest of the six mappings from joint velocity
\mbox{\(\dot q\)} to
end-effector velocity. As \mbox{\(\sigma_6\)} approaches zero, one end-effector
velocity direction becomes increasingly difficult to produce, indicating an
approach to a loss of Jacobian \mbox{\(J(q)\)} row rank.
Because the geometric Jacobian combines translational and rotational rows with
different physical units, the numerical value of \mbox{\(\sigma_{\min}\)}
depends on the chosen Jacobian representation and length unit. It is therefore
used in this thesis as a relative conditioning indicator for comparisons made
with the same Jacobian convention, rather than as a unit-invariant
manipulability measure. Within that fixed convention,
\mbox{\(\sigma_{\min}\)} is used to compare the conditioning of nearby joint
configurations and to select the direction of the conditioning torque.


\subsection{SVD-Based Pseudoinverse}
\label{subsec:svd_pseudoinverse}

The Jacobian pseudoinverse \mbox{\(J^+\)} maps end-effector-velocity components back to the
corresponding joint-velocity directions. Its calculation uses the singular
directions obtained from the \mbox{\abbr{SVD}}.

Small singular values lead to large reciprocal values and can amplify
numerical errors. A relative singular-value threshold is therefore applied.
Let \mbox{\(\rho\geq0\)} denote the configured relative \mbox{\abbr{SVD}} tolerance. The
corresponding absolute singular-value threshold \mbox{\(\sigma_{\mathrm{thr}}\)} is
calculated from the largest singular value \mbox{\(\sigma_1\)}:

\begin{equation}
\sigma_{\mathrm{thr}}
=
\rho\sigma_1.
\label{eq:svd_singular_value_threshold}
\end{equation}

A singular direction is retained when

\begin{equation}
\sigma_i
>
\sigma_{\mathrm{thr}}.
\label{eq:svd_retention_condition}
\end{equation}

The Jacobian pseudoinverse \mbox{\(J^+\)} is defined as

\begin{equation}
J^+
=
\sum_{\substack{i=1\\
\sigma_i>\sigma_{\mathrm{thr}}}}^{6}
\frac{1}{\sigma_i}
v_i u_i^\top
\in\mathbb{R}^{7\times6}.
\label{eq:svd_pseudoinverse}
\end{equation}

For each retained singular direction, \mbox{\(J^+\)} maps an end-effector-velocity
component along \mbox{\(u_i\)} to the corresponding joint-velocity direction \mbox{\(v_i\)}
and scales it by \mbox{\(1/\sigma_i\)}. Directions at or below
\mbox{\(\sigma_{\mathrm{thr}}\)} receive a reciprocal value of zero.


\subsection{Null-Space Projectors}
\label{subsec:nullspace_projectors}

The pseudoinverse is used to separate a joint-space vector into a
task-related component and a null-space component. For an arbitrary joint
velocity \mbox{\(\dot q\)}, this decomposition is

\begin{equation}
\dot q
=
\underbrace{J^+J\dot q}_{\text{task-related component}}
+
\underbrace{\left(I_7-J^+J\right)\dot q}_{\text{null-space component}}.
\label{eq:joint_velocity_task_null_decomposition}
\end{equation}

The product \mbox{\(J^+J\)} projects \mbox{\(\dot q\)} onto the joint-space directions
that contribute to the end-effector velocity. Since \mbox{\(J\)} includes all three
linear and all three angular velocity components, this task-related component
accounts for the complete controlled end-effector pose at the velocity level.

The complementary joint-velocity projector \mbox{\(N_q\)} is

\begin{equation}
N_q
=
I_7-J^+J
\in\mathbb{R}^{7\times7}.
\label{eq:nullspace_velocity_projector}
\end{equation}

Applying \mbox{\(N_q\)} to an arbitrary joint velocity \mbox{\(\dot q\)} gives the projected
null-space velocity \mbox{\(\dot q_{\mathrm{null}}\)}:

\begin{equation}
\dot q_{\mathrm{null}}
=
N_q\dot q.
\label{eq:projected_nullspace_velocity}
\end{equation}

When \mbox{\(J\)} has full row rank and all six task-producing singular directions
are retained,

\begin{equation}
J\dot q_{\mathrm{null}}
=
JN_q\dot q
=
0.
\label{eq:projected_velocity_null_condition}
\end{equation}

The projected joint velocity \mbox{\(\dot q_{\mathrm{null}}\)} can therefore change the robot configuration
while preserving the end-effector position and orientation to first order.

A corresponding decomposition is used for a secondary joint torque
\mbox{\(\tau_0\)}. The joint-torque projector \mbox{\(N_\tau\)} is the transpose of
the velocity projector:

\begin{equation}
N_\tau
=
N_q^\top
=
I_7-J^\top J^{+\top}
\in\mathbb{R}^{7\times7}.
\label{eq:nullspace_torque_projector}
\end{equation}

The secondary torque can then be written as

\begin{equation}
\tau_0
=
\underbrace{J^\top J^{+\top}\tau_0}_{\text{task-related component}}
+
\underbrace{N_\tau\tau_0}_{\text{null-space component}}.
\label{eq:joint_torque_task_null_decomposition}
\end{equation}

The first component lies in the joint-torque subspace associated with
Cartesian wrenches \mbox{\(F\)} through \mbox{\(J^\top\)}. The second component is retained as the
secondary null-space torque \mbox{\(\tau_{\mathrm{null}}\)}:

\begin{equation}
\tau_{\mathrm{null}}
=
N_\tau\tau_0.
\label{eq:general_projected_nullspace_torque}
\end{equation}

The transpose relation follows from the mechanical power
\mbox{\(\tau^\top\dot q\)} between joint torques \mbox{\(\tau\)} and joint velocities
\mbox{\(\dot q\)}. Thus, \mbox{\(N_q\)} is applied to joint velocities
\mbox{\(\dot q\)}, while \mbox{\(N_\tau\)} is applied to secondary joint torques
\mbox{\(\tau_0\)}.

The projector provides an orthogonal kinematic separation with respect to the
Jacobian and the Moore--Penrose pseudoinverse. It is not the inertia-weighted
dynamically consistent null-space projector used in some operational-space
formulations. Cartesian position retention under the implemented secondary
torques is therefore also evaluated experimentally.

For the \mbox{\abbr{SVD}}-based pseudoinverse, \mbox{\(J^+J\)} is an orthogonal and
symmetric projector. Hence,

\begin{equation}
N_q
=
N_\tau
\label{eq:equal_velocity_torque_projectors}
\end{equation}

holds analytically. The separate symbols distinguish the quantity on which the
projector acts. In the implementation, \mbox{\(N_\tau\)} is additionally symmetrised
to reduce floating-point asymmetry.

When \mbox{\(J\)} has full row rank and all six task-producing singular directions
are retained,

\begin{equation}
N_q
=
N_\tau
=
v_7v_7^\top.
\label{eq:full_rank_nullspace_projector}
\end{equation}

In this case, projection retains only the component along the redundant
direction \mbox{\(v_7\)}. When a singular value lies at or below the selected
threshold, its associated right-singular direction is also assigned to the
numerical null space.


\subsection{Null-Space Damping Torque}
\label{subsec:nullspace_damping_torque}

The current joint velocity \mbox{\(\dot q\)} is separated into task-related and null-space
components according to
\mbox{\Cref{eq:joint_velocity_task_null_decomposition}}. The null-space damping
torque \mbox{\(\tau_d\)} acts on the component \mbox{\(N_q\dot q\)}:

\begin{equation}
\tau_d
=
-d_{\mathrm{null}}N_\tau\dot q
=
-d_{\mathrm{null}}N_q\dot q,
\label{eq:nullspace_damping_torque}
\end{equation}

where \mbox{\(d_{\mathrm{null}}\geq0\)} is the null-space damping coefficient. The
negative sign makes the torque oppose motion along the redundant joint-space
direction and dissipate the associated motion.


\subsection{Singular-Value Conditioning Torque}
\label{subsec:singular_value_conditioning_torque}

The second contribution applies an active torque along the redundant direction
\mbox{\(v_7\)}. Its sign is selected by comparing \mbox{\(\sigma_{\min}\)} at two nearby
joint configurations.

The probe angle \mbox{\(\alpha_{\mathrm{probe}}\)} specifies the joint-space
step from the current configuration \mbox{\(q\)}. The sampled configurations in
the positive and negative null directions are \mbox{\(q_+\)} and \mbox{\(q_-\)}:

\begin{equation}
q_+
=
q+\alpha_{\mathrm{probe}}v_7,
\qquad
q_-
=
q-\alpha_{\mathrm{probe}}v_7.
\label{eq:nullspace_candidates}
\end{equation}

The Jacobian \mbox{\(J(q)\)} is recalculated at both configurations. The minimum singular
values \mbox{\(\sigma_+\)} and \mbox{\(\sigma_-\)} correspond to
\mbox{\(q_+\)} and \mbox{\(q_-\)}, respectively:

\begin{equation}
\sigma_+
=
\sigma_{\min}\!\left(J(q_+)\right),
\qquad
\sigma_-
=
\sigma_{\min}\!\left(J(q_-)\right).
\label{eq:nullspace_sampled_singular_values}
\end{equation}

The sampled singular-value difference \mbox{\(\Delta_\sigma\)} is

\begin{equation}
\Delta_\sigma
=
\sigma_+-\sigma_-.
\label{eq:nullspace_singular_value_difference}
\end{equation}

The singular-value-conditioning direction selector \mbox{\(s_\sigma\)} is defined as

\begin{equation}
s_\sigma
=
\begin{cases}
+1, & \Delta_\sigma>\delta_\sigma,\\
-1, & \Delta_\sigma<-\delta_\sigma,\\
0,  & |\Delta_\sigma|\leq\delta_\sigma,
\end{cases}
\label{eq:nullspace_direction_selection}
\end{equation}

where \mbox{\(\delta_\sigma\geq0\)} is the comparison deadband. Within this deadband,
the selector \mbox{\(s_\sigma\)} is set to zero and the conditioning contribution is suppressed.

The selected direction is invariant to the sign convention of \mbox{\(v_7\)}
returned by the \mbox{\abbr{SVD}}. Reversing \mbox{\(v_7\)} exchanges \mbox{\(q_+\)} and \mbox{\(q_-\)}
and reverses \mbox{\(s_\sigma\)}, so the product \mbox{\(s_\sigma v_7\)} remains unchanged.

The conditioning-torque magnitude \mbox{\(k_\sigma\)} is specified
independently of the probe angle.

The vector \mbox{\(v_7\)} represents the instantaneous null-space direction at the
current configuration. For a finite probe angle, \mbox{\(q_+\)} and \mbox{\(q_-\)} provide
local approximations of the nonlinear constant-pose self-motion. The
corresponding preservation of the end-effector pose is therefore a first-order
property of the sampled direction.

The selector \mbox{\(s_\sigma\)} uses the sign of \mbox{\(\Delta_\sigma\)} to choose the sampled
direction with the larger value of \mbox{\(\sigma_{\min}\)}. This produces a
two-point directional conditioning strategy based on local comparison rather
than on the magnitude of the sampled variation.

The vector \mbox{\(s_\sigma v_7\)} defines the selected secondary joint-space direction.
Projecting it with \mbox{\(N_\tau\)} gives the singular-value conditioning torque
\mbox{\(\tau_\sigma\)}:

\begin{equation}
\tau_\sigma
=
k_\sigma N_\tau\left(s_\sigma v_7\right),
\label{eq:nullspace_sigma_torque}
\end{equation}

where \mbox{\(k_\sigma\geq0\)} specifies the commanded torque magnitude.

When the Jacobian has full row rank and all six task-producing singular
directions are retained, \mbox{\(v_7\)} lies in the one-dimensional null space and

\begin{equation}
N_\tau v_7
=
v_7.
\label{eq:nullspace_projection_of_v7}
\end{equation}

The conditioning contribution therefore acts along the selected
redundant joint direction, while the damping contribution opposes motion
within the projected subspace.

\subsection{Complete Null-Space Torque}
\label{subsec:complete_nullspace_torque}

Combining the damping and conditioning contributions gives

\begin{equation}
\tau_{\mathrm{null}}
=
\tau_d+\tau_\sigma
=
-d_{\mathrm{null}}N_\tau\dot q
+
k_\sigma N_\tau\left(s_\sigma v_7\right)
\in\mathbb{R}^7.
\label{eq:nullspace_torque}
\end{equation}

When the Jacobian \mbox{\(J(q)\)} has full row rank, its null space is one-dimensional and is
spanned by \mbox{\(v_7\)}. If the Jacobian loses row rank, the null space becomes
multidimensional and the projector includes the additional numerical-null-space
directions. The damping contribution acts on motion in all these directions
through \mbox{\(N_\tau\dot q\)}. The implemented conditioning term retains its two
probes along \mbox{\(+v_7\)} and \mbox{\(-v_7\)}.

The two contributions therefore have different roles: \mbox{\(\tau_d\)}
opposes redundant joint motion that is already present, whereas
\mbox{\(\tau_\sigma\)} applies an active torque that moves the redundant
configuration along the selected direction toward the nearby sampled
configuration with the larger \mbox{\(\sigma_{\min}\)}.

The activation of the damping and conditioning contributions is a controller
configuration choice. The available combinations and the configuration used
in the reported experiments are described in
\mbox{\Cref{sec:nullspace_conditioning_implementation}}.

\section{Complete Joint-Torque Command}
\label{sec:complete_joint_torque_command}

The complete joint-torque command \mbox{\(\tau_{\mathrm{cmd}}\)} combines the
Cartesian impedance torque \mbox{\(\tau_{\mathrm{cart}}\)}, the null-space torque
\mbox{\(\tau_{\mathrm{null}}\)}, and the Coriolis compensation \mbox{\(\tau_c\)}:

\begin{equation}
\tau_{\mathrm{cmd}}
=
\tau_{\mathrm{cart}}
+
\tau_{\mathrm{null}}
+
\tau_c(q,\dot q)
=
J^\top(q)F
+
\tau_{\mathrm{null}}
+
\tau_c(q,\dot q).
\label{eq:nullspace_complete_torque_command}
\end{equation}

Here, \mbox{\(\tau_{\mathrm{cart}}=J^\top(q)F\)} is the Cartesian impedance torque,
\mbox{\(\tau_{\mathrm{null}}\)} is the secondary null-space torque, and
\mbox{\(\tau_c(q,\dot q)\)} is the Coriolis and centrifugal compensation obtained
from the robot model and added in the control callback. A separate gravity
term is not added because gravity compensation is handled internally by the
Franka Control Interface, as described in
\mbox{\Cref{subsec:coriolis_gravity_compensation}}.
The next chapter implements these torque contributions in the real-time control
loop and combines them with the surface-relative geometry, phase-dependent
references, and contact sequence.
