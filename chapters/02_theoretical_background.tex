This chapter introduces all mathematical concepts required to derive and
understand the Cartesian impedance controller used in this thesis.
The presentation follows a deliberate step-by-step order:
it begins with the geometry of coordinate frames, builds up the algebraic
tools for transformations, defines the controlled variable as the
end-effector position and orientation, derives the error signal, and then
develops the kinematic and dynamic machinery that connects joint torques
to Cartesian forces.
%% ─────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────
\section{Coordinate Systems}
\label{sec:coord_systems}
%% ─────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────

\subsection{Reference Frames Used in This Thesis}

To describe the motion of a robotic manipulator, multiple coordinate
frames are used simultaneously \cite{Siciliano2009, Craig2005}.
A coordinate frame is a right-handed orthogonal system of
three mutually perpendicular unit vectors anchored at a chosen origin.
For the joint frames, this thesis follows the
Denavit--Hartenberg (\abbr{DH}) convention, a standard robotics
method for assigning coordinate frames to consecutive links in a kinematic
chain \cite{Denavit1955, Craig2005}.
Three frames are central to this work:

\begin{itemize}
  \item Base frame $\{0\}$: fixed to the robot mounting
        surface. Its origin is the robot base reference point used by
        the kinematic model. For the upright Panda configuration used in
        this thesis, the $z_0$-axis points normal to the mounting surface
        and is aligned with the first joint axis, while the $x_0$- and
        $y_0$-axes lie in the mounting plane and complete a right-handed
        coordinate system according to the manufacturer convention.
        All positions, orientations, and forces are ultimately expressed in
        this frame unless stated otherwise.
  \item Joint frames $\{1\},\{2\},\ldots,\{n\}$: rigidly
        attached to the successive links of the kinematic chain.
        The origin of frame $\{i\}$ is placed at the reference point of
        joint $i$ used by the Denavit--Hartenberg model. Its $z_i$-axis
        is the rotation axis of joint $i$; its $x_i$-axis is chosen along
        the common normal to the neighbouring joint axes, and $y_i$
        completes the right-handed frame. These frames move with the
        robot and encode the relative geometry between consecutive links.
  \item End-effector frame $\{\mathrm{EE}\}$: attached to the tool
        centre point. Its origin is the controlled point at the tool tip
        or flange, depending on the selected tool definition. The axes
        describe the tool orientation: $z_{\mathrm{EE}}$ is taken as the tool approach
        direction, while $x_{\mathrm{EE}}$ and $y_{\mathrm{EE}}$ span the tool plane and complete
        a right-handed frame. The position and orientation of $\{\mathrm{EE}\}$
        relative to $\{0\}$ constitute the controlled position and orientation of the robot.
        In the implementation, this frame is the libfranka end-effector/tool
        frame selected through the robot model interface.
\end{itemize}

The coordinate-frame assignment and the corresponding Denavit--Hartenberg convention for assigning consecutive coordinate systems
are illustrated in \cref{fig:coordinate_frame_robot}.
\begin{figure}[H]
    \centering
    \includegraphics[width=0.95\textwidth]{figures/coordinate_frames.png}
    \caption{Coordinate-frame assignment on the left and Denavit--Hartenberg convention on the right.}
    \label{fig:coordinate_frame_robot}
\end{figure}
This frame assignment forms the basis for describing the robot kinematics using homogeneous transformation matrices.

\section{Coordinate Transformations}
\label{sec:coord_transf}
%% ─────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────

The relationship between any two coordinate frames is described by a
combination of translation and rotation
\cite{Denavit1955, Craig2005}.

\subsection{Translation}

A pure translation moves the origin of one frame to a new location
without changing its orientation.
It is represented by a position vector:

\begin{equation}
\label{eq:trans}
\mathbf{p} =
\begin{bmatrix} x \\ y \\ z \end{bmatrix}
\in \mathbb{R}^3
\end{equation}

where $x,y,z\in\mathbb{R}$ are the scalar displacements along the
three coordinate axes, measured in metres.

\subsection{Rotation Matrices}
\label{subsec:rotation}

A rotation matrix describes the orientation of one coordinate frame relative to another while leaving the origin unchanged. It therefore represents a pure change in orientation, without translation, scaling, or deformation. In three-dimensional space, rotations are commonly expressed by three successive elementary rotations about the coordinate axes.

The elementary rotations about the \(x\)-, \(y\)-, and \(z\)-axes are given by~\cite{Siciliano2009,Craig2005}:

\begin{equation}
R_x(\alpha)=
\begin{bmatrix}
1 & 0 & 0\\
0 & \cos\alpha & -\sin\alpha\\
0 & \sin\alpha & \cos\alpha
\end{bmatrix},
\label{eq:Rx}
\end{equation}

\begin{equation}
R_y(\beta)=
\begin{bmatrix}
\cos\beta & 0 & \sin\beta\\
0 & 1 & 0\\
-\sin\beta & 0 & \cos\beta
\end{bmatrix},
\label{eq:Ry}
\end{equation}

\begin{equation}
R_z(\gamma)=
\begin{bmatrix}
\cos\gamma & -\sin\gamma & 0\\
\sin\gamma & \cos\gamma & 0\\
0 & 0 & 1
\end{bmatrix}.
\label{eq:Rz}
\end{equation}

Here, \(\alpha\), \(\beta\), and \(\gamma\) denote rotations about the \(x\)-, \(y\)-, and \(z\)-axes, respectively. These angles are commonly referred to as roll, pitch, and yaw. In each elementary rotation matrix, the axis of rotation remains unchanged, while the two perpendicular axes are rotated within their plane.

In this work, the ZYX convention is used. This corresponds to applying a roll rotation about the \(x\)-axis, followed by a pitch rotation about the \(y\)-axis, and finally a yaw rotation about the \(z\)-axis~\cite{Siciliano2009,Craig2005}. The resulting rotation matrix is therefore

\begin{equation}
R = R_z(\gamma)R_y(\beta)R_x(\alpha).
\label{eq:ZYX_rotation}
\end{equation}

Expanding this product yields

\begin{equation}
R =
\begin{bmatrix}
\cos\gamma\cos\beta &
\cos\gamma\sin\beta\sin\alpha - \sin\gamma\cos\alpha &
\cos\gamma\sin\beta\cos\alpha + \sin\gamma\sin\alpha \\

\sin\gamma\cos\beta &
\sin\gamma\sin\beta\sin\alpha + \cos\gamma\cos\alpha &
\sin\gamma\sin\beta\cos\alpha - \cos\gamma\sin\alpha \\

-\sin\beta &
\cos\beta\sin\alpha &
\cos\beta\cos\alpha
\end{bmatrix}.
\label{eq:expanded_ZYX_rotation}
\end{equation}

Each element of \(R\) can be interpreted as a direction cosine. The entry \(R_{ij}\) gives the cosine of the angle between the \(i\)-th axis of the rotated frame and the \(j\)-th axis of the reference frame~\cite{Craig2005}. This interpretation is useful because it shows that the columns of \(R\) represent the rotated coordinate axes expressed in the reference frame.

A valid rotation matrix must preserve lengths and angles. This requirement leads to the orthogonality condition

\begin{equation}
R^\top R = I.
\label{eq:orthogonality_condition}
\end{equation}

As a consequence, the inverse of a rotation matrix is equal to its transpose~\cite{Siciliano2009,Murray1994},

\begin{equation}
R^{-1}=R^\top.
\label{eq:rotation_inverse}
\end{equation}

In addition, a proper rotation must have a determinant equal to one,

\begin{equation}
\det(R)=+1.
\label{eq:rotation_determinant}
\end{equation}

The determinant condition excludes reflections, which also preserve lengths and angles but reverse the handedness of the coordinate system. Together, these conditions ensure that \(R\) represents a pure rotation only.

\subsection{Homogeneous Transformation Matrices}
\label{sec:homogeneous}
%% ─────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────
The previous sections introduced the rotation matrix for describing orientation and the translation vector for describing position. For coordinate transformations and kinematic calculations, it is often convenient to combine rotation and translation into a single representation: the homogeneous transformation matrix. This representation is widely used in robotics and kinematics because it allows coordinate transformations and chains of frame changes to be expressed as matrix products~\cite{Craig2005,Siciliano2009}.

The homogeneous transformation from frame \(\{a\}\) to frame \(\{b\}\) is written as

\begin{equation}
{}^bT_a =
\begin{bmatrix}
{}^bR_a & {}^bp_a\\
0^\top & 1
\end{bmatrix}
\in \mathbb{R}^{4 \times 4}.
\label{eq:homogeneous_transformation}
\end{equation}

Here, \({}^bR_a\) is the rotation matrix that describes the orientation of frame \(\{a\}\) with respect to frame \(\{b\}\).
The vector \({}^bp_a\) describes the position of the origin of frame \(\{a\}\), expressed in frame \(\{b\}\).
The last row, \(0^\top = [0 \; 0 \; 0]\) and \(1\), is introduced by the homogeneous-coordinate representation and allows rotation and translation to be combined in one matrix equation.

A point expressed in frame \(\{a\}\) can be written in homogeneous coordinates as

\begin{equation}
{}^a\tilde{p}
=
\begin{bmatrix}
{}^ap \\
1
\end{bmatrix}.
\label{eq:homogeneous_point_a}
\end{equation}

The coordinates of the same point expressed in frame \(\{b\}\) are then obtained by

\begin{equation}
{}^b\tilde{p}
=
{}^bT_a \, {}^a\tilde{p}
=
\begin{bmatrix}
{}^bR_a & {}^bp_a\\
0^\top & 1
\end{bmatrix}
\begin{bmatrix}
{}^ap\\
1
\end{bmatrix}
=
\begin{bmatrix}
{}^bR_a \, {}^ap + {}^bp_a\\
1
\end{bmatrix}.
\label{eq:homogeneous_point_transform_expanded}
\end{equation}

In non-homogeneous form, this corresponds to

\begin{equation}
{}^bp
=
{}^bR_a \, {}^ap
+
{}^bp_a.
\label{eq:non_homogeneous_transform}
\end{equation}

This equation shows that the point coordinates are first rotated into the target frame and then shifted by the translation vector between the two frame origins.

One advantage of homogeneous transformations is that several frame transformations can be composed by matrix multiplication.
For example, if a transformation from frame \(\{a\}\) to frame \(\{b\}\) is followed by a transformation from frame \(\{b\}\) to frame \(\{c\}\), the combined transformation from frame \(\{a\}\) to frame \(\{c\}\) is

\begin{equation}
{}^cT_a
=
{}^cT_b \, {}^bT_a.
\label{eq:homogeneous_composition}
\end{equation}

This property is particularly useful for kinematic chains, where the position and orientation of one frame are often obtained through a sequence of intermediate frames.

For a homogeneous transformation to describe a valid rigid-body motion, the rotation part must satisfy

\begin{equation}
R^\top R = I,
\qquad
\det(R)=1.
\label{eq:valid_rotation_conditions}
\end{equation}

These conditions ensure that the transformation preserves lengths, angles, and the handedness of the coordinate system.
Therefore, the transformation can change the position and orientation of a frame, but it cannot introduce scaling, shearing, or reflection.

The inverse transformation can also be computed efficiently.
For

\begin{equation}
T =
\begin{bmatrix}
R & p\\
0^\top & 1
\end{bmatrix},
\label{eq:general_homogeneous_transformation}
\end{equation}

the inverse is given by~\cite{Murray1994}

\begin{equation}
T^{-1}
=
\begin{bmatrix}
R^\top & -R^\top p\\
0^\top & 1
\end{bmatrix}.
\label{eq:homogeneous_inverse}
\end{equation}

This follows from the orthogonality of the rotation matrix, since \(R^{-1}=R^\top\).
As a result, inverting a homogeneous transformation requires only a transpose of the rotation matrix and one matrix-vector multiplication, rather than a general \(4 \times 4\) matrix inversion.

%% ─────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────
\subsection{End-Effector Pose}
\label{sec:position_orientation}
%% ─────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────

The task of the controller is to move the end-effector to a desired pose.
In robotics, the pose of a rigid body means its position and orientation
together.
The position of the end-effector is described by the vector

\begin{equation}
p_{\mathrm{EE}}
=
\begin{bmatrix}
p_{x,\mathrm{EE}}\\
p_{y,\mathrm{EE}}\\
p_{z,\mathrm{EE}}
\end{bmatrix}
\in \mathbb{R}^3,
\label{eq:end_effector_position}
\end{equation}

where \(p_{x,\mathrm{EE}}\), \(p_{y,\mathrm{EE}}\), and
\(p_{z,\mathrm{EE}}\) denote the Cartesian position components of the
end-effector in the base frame.

The orientation of the end-effector is described by the rotation matrix

\begin{equation}
R_{\mathrm{EE}} \in \mathbb{R}^{3 \times 3}.
\label{eq:end_effector_rotation}
\end{equation}

Together, position and orientation describe the complete pose of the end-effector relative to the robot base.
Since both quantities are contained in the homogeneous transformation matrix, the end-effector transformation can be written as

\begin{equation}
T_{\mathrm{EE}}
=
\begin{bmatrix}
R_{\mathrm{EE}} & p_{\mathrm{EE}}\\
0^\top & 1
\end{bmatrix}.
\label{eq:end_effector_transformation}
\end{equation}

Here, \(R_{\mathrm{EE}}\) represents the orientation of the end-effector frame with respect to the base frame, and \(p_{\mathrm{EE}}\) represents the Cartesian position of the end-effector/tool-frame origin expressed in the base frame.
In the Franka control interface, this transformation is provided directly by \texttt{state.O\_T\_EE}.
The upper-left \(3 \times 3\) block contains the rotation matrix, while the upper-right \(3 \times 1\) block contains the Cartesian position in metres.

Using the homogeneous transformation matrix as the controlled variable is convenient because it combines the end-effector pose in a single representation.
It also avoids the need to describe orientation by Euler angles, which can suffer from singularities such as gimbal lock.
Since the rotation matrix is already provided directly by libfranka, no additional conversion is required.

\subsection{Position and Orientation Error}
\label{subsec:position_orientation_error}

In order to control the motion of the end-effector, the deviation between the desired and the current end-effector transformation must be computed.
Let the desired transformation be

\begin{equation}
T_d
=
\begin{bmatrix}
R_d & p_d\\
0^\top & 1
\end{bmatrix},
\label{eq:desired_transformation}
\end{equation}

and let the current end-effector transformation be

\begin{equation}
T_{\mathrm{EE}}
=
\begin{bmatrix}
R_{\mathrm{EE}} & p_{\mathrm{EE}}\\
0^\top & 1
\end{bmatrix}.
\label{eq:current_end_effector_transformation}
\end{equation}

For scalar or vector quantities, an error is usually computed by subtraction.
However, this is not suitable for homogeneous transformation matrices.
Subtracting two transformation matrices does not generally produce another meaningful rigid-body transformation.
Therefore, the error is computed using the relative transformation between the desired and the current end-effector frame.

The relative transformation is defined as

\begin{equation}
T_{\mathrm{err}}
=
T_d^{-1}T_{\mathrm{EE}}.
\label{eq:relative_transformation_error}
\end{equation}

This transformation describes the current end-effector frame relative to the desired frame.
If the current and desired end-effector transformations are identical, then

\begin{equation}
T_{\mathrm{err}}
=
T_d^{-1}T_d
=
I_4.
\label{eq:zero_transformation_error}
\end{equation}

Thus, the identity matrix represents zero position and orientation error.

Using the inverse of a homogeneous transformation \cite{Craig2005,Siciliano2009}, the desired inverse is

\begin{equation}
T_d^{-1}
=
\begin{bmatrix}
R_d^\top & -R_d^\top \mathbf{p}_d\\
0^\top & 1
\end{bmatrix}.
\label{eq:desired_transformation_inverse}
\end{equation}

Substituting \(T_d^{-1}\) and \(T_{\mathrm{EE}}\) gives

\begin{equation}
T_{\mathrm{err}}
=
\begin{bmatrix}
R_d^\top & -R_d^\top \mathbf{p}_d\\
0^\top & 1
\end{bmatrix}
\begin{bmatrix}
R_{\mathrm{EE}} & \mathbf{p}_{\mathrm{EE}}\\
0^\top & 1
\end{bmatrix}.
\label{eq:transformation_error_product}
\end{equation}

Carrying out the block matrix multiplication yields

\begin{equation}
T_{\mathrm{err}}
=
\begin{bmatrix}
R_d^\top R_{\mathrm{EE}} & R_d^\top(\mathbf{p}_{\mathrm{EE}}-\mathbf{p}_d)\\
0^\top & 1
\end{bmatrix}.
\label{eq:expanded_transformation_error}
\end{equation}

The translational part of this matrix gives the displacement of the current
end-effector position relative to the desired frame:

\begin{equation}
\Delta \mathbf{p}_d
=
R_d^\top(\mathbf{p}_{\mathrm{EE}}-\mathbf{p}_d)
\in \mathbb{R}^3.
\label{eq:relative_position_displacement}
\end{equation}

This displacement is expressed in the desired frame.
Therefore, its components describe where the current end-effector position lies
relative to the desired frame axes. The positional control error used in the
impedance law is defined with the restoring sign

\begin{equation}
\mathbf{e}_p
=
\mathbf{p}_d
-
\mathbf{p}_{\mathrm{EE}}
\in \mathbb{R}^3.
\label{eq:position_control_error}
\end{equation}

If the error is expressed in the desired frame, this corresponds to
\(-\Delta \mathbf{p}_d\).

The rotational part of the relative transformation is

\begin{equation}
\Delta R
=
R_d^\top R_{\mathrm{EE}}.
\label{eq:relative_rotation_error}
\end{equation}

This matrix describes the orientation difference between the desired and current end-effector frames.
If the current orientation is equal to the desired orientation, then \(R_{\mathrm{EE}} = R_d\), and therefore

\begin{equation}
\Delta R
=
R_d^\top R_d
=
\mathbf{I}.
\label{eq:zero_rotation_error}
\end{equation}

Hence, the identity matrix represents zero orientation error.

For use in the impedance controller, the orientation error must be represented as a three-dimensional vector.
This is done using the axis-angle representation.
A rotation matrix \(\Delta R\) can be described by a rotation angle \(\phi\) about a rotation axis vector \(u \in \mathbb{R}^3\) \cite{Siciliano2009,Murray1994}.
The vector \(u\) is chosen to have unit length, so it describes only the direction of the rotation axis.

The rotation angle \(\phi\) can be obtained from the trace of the relative rotation matrix \(\Delta R\).
The trace of a matrix is the sum of its diagonal elements,

\begin{equation}
\operatorname{tr}(\Delta R)
=
\Delta R_{11}
+
\Delta R_{22}
+
\Delta R_{33}.
\label{eq:rotation_trace}
\end{equation}

For a three-dimensional rotation matrix, the trace is related to the rotation angle by

\begin{equation}
\operatorname{tr}(\Delta R)
=
1 + 2\cos\phi.
\label{eq:trace_angle_relation}
\end{equation}

Solving this equation for \(\phi\) gives

\begin{equation}
\cos\phi
=
\frac{\operatorname{tr}(\Delta R)-1}{2},
\label{eq:cos_angle_from_trace}
\end{equation}

and therefore

\begin{equation}
\phi
=
\arccos
\left(
\frac{\operatorname{tr}(\Delta R)-1}{2}
\right).
\label{eq:angle_from_trace}
\end{equation}

Thus, the rotation angle can be computed directly from the diagonal elements of \(\Delta R\).

The rotation axis vector \(u\) can be obtained from the skew-symmetric part of \(\Delta R\).
This relation follows from Rodrigues' rotation formula, which expresses a rotation matrix as \cite{Siciliano2009,Murray1994}

\begin{equation}
\Delta R
=
I
+
\sin\phi [u]_\times
+
(1-\cos\phi)[u]_\times^2.
\label{eq:rodrigues_formula}
\end{equation}

Here, \([u]_\times\) is the skew-symmetric matrix corresponding to the rotation axis vector

\begin{equation}
u =
\begin{bmatrix}
u_x\\
u_y\\
u_z
\end{bmatrix},
\label{eq:rotation_axis_vector}
\end{equation}

and is defined as

\begin{equation}
[u]_\times
=
\begin{bmatrix}
0 & -u_z & u_y\\
u_z & 0 & -u_x\\
-u_y & u_x & 0
\end{bmatrix}.
\label{eq:skew_symmetric_axis_matrix}
\end{equation}

The term \(\sin\phi [u]_\times\) is the part of Rodrigues' formula that contains the rotation-axis information.
The factor \(\sin\phi\) appears because this skew-symmetric part of the rotation matrix depends on the sine of the rotation angle.

To isolate this part, the transpose of \(\Delta R\) is subtracted from \(\Delta R\).
Since

\begin{equation}
[u]_\times^\top = -[u]_\times,
\label{eq:skew_symmetric_property}
\end{equation}

the transpose of Rodrigues' formula is

\begin{equation}
\Delta R^\top
=
I
-
\sin\phi [u]_\times
+
(1-\cos\phi)[u]_\times^2.
\label{eq:rodrigues_transpose}
\end{equation}

Subtracting \(\Delta R^\top\) from \(\Delta R\) gives

\begin{equation}
\Delta R - \Delta R^\top
=
2\sin\phi [u]_\times.
\label{eq:skew_part_derivation}
\end{equation}

The identity terms and the symmetric term \((1-\cos\phi)[u]_\times^2\) cancel out.
Dividing by two gives the skew-symmetric part of \(\Delta R\):

\begin{equation}
\frac{1}{2}
\left(
\Delta R-\Delta R^\top
\right)
=
\sin\phi [u]_\times.
\label{eq:skew_part_rotation_matrix}
\end{equation}

This equation shows that the skew-symmetric part of \(\Delta R\) contains the rotation axis vector, scaled by \(\sin\phi\).
Therefore, to recover \(u\), the corresponding entries must be divided by \(\sin\phi\).

Reading off the entries gives

\begin{equation}
u_x =
\frac{\Delta R_{32}-\Delta R_{23}}{2\sin\phi},
\label{eq:rotation_axis_x}
\end{equation}

\begin{equation}
u_y =
\frac{\Delta R_{13}-\Delta R_{31}}{2\sin\phi},
\label{eq:rotation_axis_y}
\end{equation}

and

\begin{equation}
u_z =
\frac{\Delta R_{21}-\Delta R_{12}}{2\sin\phi}.
\label{eq:rotation_axis_z}
\end{equation}

Therefore, the rotation axis vector is

\begin{equation}
u
=
\frac{1}{2\sin\phi}
\begin{bmatrix}
\Delta R_{32}-\Delta R_{23}\\
\Delta R_{13}-\Delta R_{31}\\
\Delta R_{21}-\Delta R_{12}
\end{bmatrix}.
\label{eq:rotation_axis_from_matrix}
\end{equation}

The rotational error vector is then defined as the rotation axis vector scaled by the rotation angle:

\begin{equation}
\mathbf{e}_R
=
\phi u.
\label{eq:rotation_error_axis_angle}
\end{equation}

Substituting the expression for \(u\) gives

\begin{equation}
\mathbf{e}_R
=
\frac{\phi}{2\sin\phi}
\begin{bmatrix}
\Delta R_{32}-\Delta R_{23}\\
\Delta R_{13}-\Delta R_{31}\\
\Delta R_{21}-\Delta R_{12}
\end{bmatrix}
\in \mathbb{R}^3.
\label{eq:rotation_error_vector}
\end{equation}

The direction of \(\mathbf{e}_R\) gives the axis of the required rotational correction, while its magnitude gives the orientation error in radians.
If the desired and current orientations are equal, then \(\Delta R = \mathbf{I}\), \(\phi = 0\), and \(\mathbf{e}_R = \mathbf{0}\).
For angles close to \(\pi\), the expression becomes numerically sensitive because \(\sin\phi\) approaches zero.
This case is therefore handled separately in the implementation.

Finally, the complete Cartesian error vector used by the impedance controller is obtained by stacking the translational and rotational errors:

\begin{equation}
\mathbf{e}
=
\begin{bmatrix}
\mathbf{e}_p\\
\mathbf{e}_R
\end{bmatrix}
\in \mathbb{R}^6.
\label{eq:cartesian_error_vector}
\end{equation}

This vector contains the three position-error components and the three orientation-error components of the end-effector.
It therefore provides a compact representation of the deviation between the desired and current end-effector configuration.

\section{Forward Kinematics}
%% ─────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────

After defining the end-effector position and orientation as the controlled
variable, the next step is to describe how these quantities are obtained from
the robot joint angles.
This relationship is given by forward kinematics, which maps the joint
configuration of the robot to the corresponding end-effector transformation.

Forward kinematics describes the relationship between the robot joint
configuration and the resulting end-effector position and orientation.
For a serial robot manipulator, the end-effector transformation is obtained by
multiplying the homogeneous transformation matrices between neighboring link
frames.

For a robot with \(n\) joints, the forward kinematics can be written as

\begin{equation}
{}^0T_n(q)
=
{}^0T_1(q_1)
{}^1T_2(q_2)
\cdots
{}^{n-1}T_n(q_n).
\label{eq:forward_kinematics_chain}
\end{equation}

Here, \(q\) denotes the joint configuration vector,

\begin{equation}
q =
\begin{bmatrix}
q_1 & q_2 & \cdots & q_n
\end{bmatrix}^\top
\in \mathbb{R}^n.
\label{eq:joint_configuration_vector}
\end{equation}

The vector \(q\) contains the joint angles of the robot.
According to the Denavit--Hartenberg convention, each component \(q_i\)
denotes the amount of rotation of joint \(i\) about its local joint axis
\(z_i\).
The angle \(q_i\) is measured in radians.

Each matrix \({}^{i-1}T_i(q_i)\) describes the relative position and
orientation between two neighboring link frames.
In the standard Denavit--Hartenberg convention, this transformation is
obtained as a product of four elementary homogeneous transformations:

\begin{equation}
{}^{i-1}T_i(q_i)
=
R_z(q_i)\,T_z(d_i)\,T_x(a_i)\,R_x(\alpha_i).
\label{eq:dh_elementary_transformations}
\end{equation}

Here, \(R_z(q_i)\) and \(R_x(\alpha_i)\) are homogeneous rotation matrices,
while \(T_z(d_i)\) and \(T_x(a_i)\) are homogeneous translation matrices.
All four matrices have dimension \(4 \times 4\), so their product gives the
homogeneous transformation from frame \(\{i\}\) to frame \(\{i-1\}\).

Since column vectors are used, the transformations act from right to left when
applied to a point.
Thus, \(R_x(\alpha_i)\) represents the rotation about the \(x\)-axis by the
link twist angle \(\alpha_i\).
The translation \(T_x(a_i)\) then represents the displacement along the
\(x\)-axis by the link length \(a_i\).
The translation \(T_z(d_i)\) represents the displacement along the \(z\)-axis
by the offset \(d_i\).
Finally, \(R_z(q_i)\) represents the joint rotation about the local \(z\)-axis
by the joint angle \(q_i\).

Written as a single homogeneous transformation matrix, this product becomes

\begin{equation}
{}^{i-1}T_i(q_i)
=
\begin{bmatrix}
\cos q_i & -\sin q_i\cos\alpha_i & \sin q_i\sin\alpha_i & a_i\cos q_i\\
\sin q_i & \cos q_i\cos\alpha_i & -\cos q_i\sin\alpha_i & a_i\sin q_i\\
0 & \sin\alpha_i & \cos\alpha_i & d_i\\
0 & 0 & 0 & 1
\end{bmatrix}.
\label{eq:dh_transformation_matrix}
\end{equation}

The remaining Denavit–Hartenberg parameters \(d_i\), \(a_i\), and \(\alpha_i\) describe the fixed
geometry between neighboring link frames.
The parameter \(d_i\) describes a translation along the \(z\)-axis, and
\(a_i\) describes a translation along the \(x\)-axis.
The parameter \(\alpha_i\), called the link twist, describes the angle between
two neighboring joint axes.
More precisely, \(\alpha_i\) describes how much the axis \(z_i\) is tilted
with respect to the previous axis \(z_{i-1}\), as illustrated in
\cref{fig:dh_parameters}.

\begin{figure}[H]
    \centering
    \includegraphics[width=0.75\textwidth]{figures/DH_parameters.png}
    \caption{Denavit--Hartenberg parameters between neighboring link frames.}
    \label{fig:dh_parameters}
\end{figure}

The final transformation \({}^0T_n(q)\) describes the position and orientation
of the end-effector frame with respect to the robot base frame.
It can therefore be written as

\begin{equation}
{}^0T_n(q)
=
T_{\mathrm{EE}}(q)
=
\begin{bmatrix}
R_{\mathrm{EE}}(q) & p_{\mathrm{EE}}(q)\\
0^\top & 1
\end{bmatrix}.
\label{eq:forward_kinematics_result}
\end{equation}

The upper-left \(3 \times 3\) block contains the end-effector orientation
\(R_{\mathrm{EE}}(q)\), while the upper-right \(3 \times 1\) column contains
the end-effector position \(p_{\mathrm{EE}}(q)\).

This mapping is nonlinear because the individual link transformations contain
trigonometric functions of the joint angles.
Therefore, a change in a joint angle generally produces a nonlinear change in
the end-effector position and orientation.

Forward kinematics describes how the end-effector position and orientation are
obtained from the joint angles.
For control, however, it is also necessary to describe how changes in the joint
angles affect the motion of the end-effector.
This leads to differential kinematics, which relates joint velocities to
Cartesian velocities.

\section{Jacobian}
%% ─────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────

Forward kinematics provides the end-effector position and orientation as a
function of the joint configuration \(q\).
However, in control applications, the velocity relationship is also required.
Differential kinematics describes how the end-effector velocity changes with
respect to the joint velocities.

Let the Cartesian end-effector state be written as

\begin{equation}
x = f(q),
\label{eq:cartesian_state_forward_kinematics}
\end{equation}

where \(x\) represents the Cartesian position and orientation of the
end-effector, and \(q\) is the joint configuration vector.
Differentiating this relationship with respect to time gives

\begin{equation}
\dot{x}
=
J(q)\dot{q}.
\label{eq:differential_kinematics}
\end{equation}

Here, \(\dot{x} \in \mathbb{R}^6\) is the Cartesian velocity vector of the
end-effector.
It consists of the linear velocity and angular velocity,

\begin{equation}
\dot{x}
=
\begin{bmatrix}
\dot{p}_{\mathrm{EE}}\\
\omega_{\mathrm{EE}}
\end{bmatrix},
\label{eq:cartesian_velocity_vector}
\end{equation}

where \(\dot{p}_{\mathrm{EE}} \in \mathbb{R}^3\) is the linear velocity of the
end-effector and \(\omega_{\mathrm{EE}} \in \mathbb{R}^3\) is its angular
velocity.

The vector \(\dot{q} \in \mathbb{R}^n\) contains the joint velocities of the
robot,

\begin{equation}
\dot{q}
=
\begin{bmatrix}
\dot{q}_1 & \dot{q}_2 & \cdots & \dot{q}_n
\end{bmatrix}^\top,
\label{eq:joint_velocity_vector}
\end{equation}

where each \(\dot{q}_i\) describes the angular velocity of joint \(i\),
measured in radians per second.

The matrix \(J(q) \in \mathbb{R}^{6 \times n}\) is the geometric Jacobian
\cite{Siciliano2009,Craig2005}.
It maps joint velocities to the corresponding Cartesian velocity of the
end-effector at the current configuration \(q\).
Since the Jacobian depends on \(q\), the same joint velocity can produce
different end-effector velocities depending on the current robot configuration.

%% ─────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────
The previous section introduced the velocity relation between joint space and
Cartesian space.
The matrix that describes this relation is the Jacobian.
It determines how each individual joint velocity contributes to the linear and
angular velocity of the end-effector.

\subsection{Jacobian Matrix}
%% ─────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────

The Jacobian matrix describes the local relationship between joint velocities
and the Cartesian velocity of the end-effector.
It appears in the differential kinematics equation

\begin{equation}
\dot{x} = J(q)\dot{q}.
\label{eq:jacobian_velocity_relation}
\end{equation}

For a robot with \(n\) joints, the geometric Jacobian is written as

\begin{equation}
J(q)
=
\begin{bmatrix}
J_1 & J_2 & \cdots & J_n
\end{bmatrix}
\in \mathbb{R}^{6 \times n}.
\label{eq:geometric_jacobian}
\end{equation}

Each column \(J_i\) describes the contribution of joint \(i\) to the
end-effector velocity.
Since the Franka Emika Panda consists of rotational joints, the \(i\)-th
column of the Jacobian is given by

\begin{equation}
J_i
=
\begin{bmatrix}
z_i \times \left(p_{\mathrm{EE}} - p_i\right)\\
z_i
\end{bmatrix}
\in \mathbb{R}^6.
\label{eq:jacobian_column}
\end{equation}

This standard geometric-Jacobian column follows from the instantaneous velocity
of a point under a revolute joint motion \cite{Siciliano2009,Craig2005}.
Here, \(z_i \in \mathbb{R}^3\) is the unit vector along the rotation axis of
joint \(i\), expressed in the base frame.
It is obtained from the corresponding transformation matrix as the \(z\)-axis
of frame \(\{i\}\).
The vector \(p_i \in \mathbb{R}^3\) is the position of frame \(\{i\}\),
expressed in the base frame, and \(p_{\mathrm{EE}} \in \mathbb{R}^3\) is the
end-effector position.

The vector

\begin{equation}
p_{\mathrm{EE}} - p_i
\label{eq:lever_arm}
\end{equation}

is the lever arm from joint \(i\) to the end-effector.
The cross product

\begin{equation}
z_i \times \left(p_{\mathrm{EE}} - p_i\right)
\label{eq:linear_velocity_contribution}
\end{equation}

therefore gives the linear velocity contribution at the end-effector caused by
the rotation of joint \(i\).
The lower part of the column, \(z_i\), gives the corresponding angular
velocity contribution.

Thus, each Jacobian column consists of two parts:

\begin{equation}
J_i
=
\begin{bmatrix}
\text{linear velocity contribution}\\
\text{angular velocity contribution}
\end{bmatrix}.
\label{eq:jacobian_column_parts}
\end{equation}

Because \(z_i\), \(p_i\), and \(p_{\mathrm{EE}}\) all depend on the current
joint configuration, the Jacobian is configuration-dependent.
Therefore, the influence of each joint on the end-effector motion changes as
the robot moves.

\section{Cartesian Impedance Control}
%% ─────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────

The previous section introduced the Jacobian matrix, which relates joint
velocities to Cartesian end-effector velocities.
In the controller, this relationship is also used to map a Cartesian wrench to
joint torques \cite{Khatib1987}.
Before this mapping is introduced, the desired Cartesian interaction behavior
is defined using impedance control.

Impedance control imposes a desired dynamic behavior between the end-effector
motion and the forces acting on it \cite{Hogan1985,Ott2008,Mayr2024CartesianImpedance}.
Instead of commanding only a desired position, the robot is made to behave like
a virtual spring-damper system in Cartesian space.
In this way, deviations from the desired pose generate restoring forces and
moments, while the damping terms reduce oscillations and dissipate energy.
The numerical choice of the stiffness and damping gains is therefore part of
the controller design, because the same impedance law can produce very
different contact behavior depending on the selected values
\cite{Pollayil2023ImpedancePlanning,Hjorth2023EnergyAwareImpedance}.

The Cartesian error vector was defined as

\begin{equation}
\mathbf{e}
=
\begin{bmatrix}
\mathbf{e}_p\\
\mathbf{e}_R
\end{bmatrix}
\in \mathbb{R}^6,
\label{eq:cartesian_error_impedance}
\end{equation}

where \(\mathbf{e}_p\) is the translational error and \(\mathbf{e}_R\) is the rotational error.
These two error components are used to compute the Cartesian impedance forces
and moments.

\subsection{Impedance Wrench}

The positional impedance force is defined as the translational part of a
Cartesian spring-damper law \cite{Hogan1985,Mayr2024CartesianImpedance},

\begin{equation}
\mathbf{f}
=
K_p \mathbf{e}_p
-
D_p \dot{\mathbf{p}}_{\mathrm{EE}}
\in \mathbb{R}^3.
\label{eq:positional_impedance_force}
\end{equation}

Here, \(K_p \in \mathbb{R}^{3 \times 3}\) is the positional stiffness
matrix and \(D_p \in \mathbb{R}^{3 \times 3}\) is the positional damping
matrix.
The stiffness term \(K_p \mathbf{e}_p\) generates a restoring force proportional to the
position error, while the damping term \(D_p \dot{\mathbf{p}}_{\mathrm{EE}}\) opposes
the Cartesian linear velocity of the end-effector.

The rotational impedance moment is defined as

\begin{equation}
\mathbf{m}
=
K_R \mathbf{e}_R
-
D_R \omega_{\mathrm{EE}}
\in \mathbb{R}^3.
\label{eq:rotational_impedance_moment}
\end{equation}

Here, \(K_R \in \mathbb{R}^{3 \times 3}\) is the rotational stiffness matrix
and \(D_R \in \mathbb{R}^{3 \times 3}\) is the rotational damping matrix.
The stiffness term \(K_R \mathbf{e}_R\) generates a restoring moment proportional to the
orientation error, while the damping term \(D_R \omega_{\mathrm{EE}}\) opposes
the angular velocity of the end-effector.

The positional force and rotational moment are combined into the Cartesian
wrench. In this thesis, a wrench means a six-dimensional vector containing a
Cartesian force and a Cartesian moment:

\begin{equation}
\mathbf{F}
=
\begin{bmatrix}
\mathbf{f}\\
\mathbf{m}
\end{bmatrix}
=
\begin{bmatrix}
K_p \mathbf{e}_p - D_p \dot{\mathbf{p}}_{\mathrm{EE}}\\
K_R \mathbf{e}_R - D_R \omega_{\mathrm{EE}}
\end{bmatrix}
\in \mathbb{R}^6.
\label{eq:cartesian_force_moment_vector}
\end{equation}

The vector \(\mathbf{F}\) is therefore the Cartesian wrench generated by the
impedance controller in response to position, orientation, linear velocity,
and angular velocity errors.
This wrench is later mapped to joint torques using the transpose of the
Jacobian.


\subsection{Stiffness and Damping Matrices in the End-Effector Frame and Base Frame}
\label{subsec:KD_matrices}

The positional stiffness matrix \(K_p\) and damping matrix \(D_p\) do not have
to be diagonal in the robot base frame \(\{0\}\).
Here, the subscript \(p\) refers to the positional part of the impedance
controller.
A diagonal matrix is obtained only when the coordinate axes of the chosen frame
are aligned with the principal stiffness directions.
For this reason, the positional stiffness and damping matrices are first
defined in the end-effector frame \(\{\mathrm{EE}\}\), where the principal
directions are easier to specify, and are then transformed into the base frame
\(\{0\}\).
For contact-rich tasks, choosing these directions and gains should follow the
task geometry and safety requirements rather than only numerical convenience
\cite{Pollayil2023ImpedancePlanning}.

Let the orientation of the end-effector frame \(\{\mathrm{EE}\}\) with respect
to the base frame \(\{0\}\) be given by

\begin{equation}
R_{\mathrm{EE}}
=
\begin{bmatrix}
x_{\mathrm{EE}} &
y_{\mathrm{EE}} &
z_{\mathrm{EE}}
\end{bmatrix}.
\label{eq:ee_axes_in_base}
\end{equation}

The vectors \(x_{\mathrm{EE}}\), \(y_{\mathrm{EE}}\), and \(z_{\mathrm{EE}}\)
denote the unit direction vectors of the \(x\)-, \(y\)-, and \(z\)-axes of the
end-effector frame, expressed in the base frame.
They should not be confused with the Cartesian position components of the
end-effector.

In the end-effector frame, the positional stiffness matrix is chosen diagonal:

\begin{equation}
K_{p,\mathrm{EE}}
=
\operatorname{diag}(K_1,K_2,K_3)
=
\begin{bmatrix}
K_1 & 0 & 0\\
0 & K_2 & 0\\
0 & 0 & K_3
\end{bmatrix}.
\label{eq:positional_stiffness_ee}
\end{equation}

Here, \(K_1\), \(K_2\), and \(K_3\) are positive stiffness values along the
\(x\)-, \(y\)-, and \(z\)-axes of the end-effector frame, respectively.
If all three values are equal, the virtual spring has the same stiffness in
every direction.
If the values are different, the stiffness depends on direction.

Since the impedance force is computed in the base frame, the stiffness matrix
must also be expressed in the base frame.
This is done by rotating the stiffness matrix from the end-effector frame into
the base frame:

\begin{equation}
K_{p,0}
=
R_{\mathrm{EE}}
K_{p,\mathrm{EE}}
R_{\mathrm{EE}}^\top .
\label{eq:positional_stiffness_base}
\end{equation}

This equation shows that the stiffness matrix can be diagonal in the
end-effector frame but become a full matrix in the base frame.
The off-diagonal terms are not errors; they describe coupling between the
base-frame directions caused by the orientation of the end-effector frame.

The same transformation can also be understood by first expressing the
position error in the end-effector frame. With
\(\mathbf{e}_{p,0}\) denoting the positional error expressed in the base
frame, the corresponding error in the end-effector frame is

\begin{equation}
\mathbf{e}_{p,\mathrm{EE}}
=
R_{\mathrm{EE}}^\top
\mathbf{e}_{p,0}.
\label{eq:positional_error_ee}
\end{equation}

The local spring force is then computed with the diagonal stiffness matrix,

\begin{equation}
\mathbf{f}_{\mathrm{EE}}
=
K_{p,\mathrm{EE}}
\mathbf{e}_{p,\mathrm{EE}},
\label{eq:positional_force_ee}
\end{equation}

and transformed back into the base frame:

\begin{equation}
\mathbf{f}_0
=
R_{\mathrm{EE}}
\mathbf{f}_{\mathrm{EE}}.
\label{eq:positional_force_base_from_ee}
\end{equation}

Substituting \cref{eq:positional_error_ee,eq:positional_force_ee} into
\cref{eq:positional_force_base_from_ee} gives

\begin{equation}
\mathbf{f}_0
=
R_{\mathrm{EE}}
K_{p,\mathrm{EE}}
R_{\mathrm{EE}}^\top
\mathbf{e}_{p,0}
=
K_{p,0}\mathbf{e}_{p,0}.
\label{eq:positional_force_base_equivalent}
\end{equation}

Thus, it is equivalent to either transform the stiffness matrix into the base
frame once and multiply it by the base-frame error, or transform the error
into the local frame, compute the local spring force, and rotate the resulting
force back into the base frame.
This interpretation is useful for task frames as well.
If a local frame is chosen such that two axes lie tangential to a virtual
plane and the third axis is normal to it, the diagonal entries of
\(K_{p,\mathrm{EE}}\) can be interpreted directly as tangential and normal
stiffness values.
After transformation with \cref{eq:positional_stiffness_base}, the resulting
matrix \(K_{p,0}\) realizes the same physical stiffness directions while
remaining compatible with a controller that evaluates forces in the base
frame.

Equivalently, the stiffness matrix in the base frame can be written as

\begin{equation}
K_{p,0}
=
K_1\,x_{\mathrm{EE}}x_{\mathrm{EE}}^\top
+
K_2\,y_{\mathrm{EE}}y_{\mathrm{EE}}^\top
+
K_3\,z_{\mathrm{EE}}z_{\mathrm{EE}}^\top .
\label{eq:positional_stiffness_dyadic}
\end{equation}

This form shows how each end-effector axis contributes to the full stiffness
matrix in the base frame.

The damping matrix is constructed in the same way.
In the end-effector frame, it is chosen as

\begin{equation}
D_{p,\mathrm{EE}}
=
\operatorname{diag}(D_1,D_2,D_3).
\label{eq:positional_damping_ee}
\end{equation}

The damping values can be compared with the critical damping condition.
Critical damping describes the case in which a second-order spring-damper
system returns to its equilibrium position as quickly as possible without
oscillating~\cite{Ogata2010}.

For each principal direction, the corresponding one-dimensional
spring-damper system can be written as

\begin{equation}
M_i \ddot{x}_i + D_i \dot{x}_i + K_i x_i = 0,
\label{eq:one_dimensional_spring_damper}
\end{equation}

where \(M_i\), \(D_i\), and \(K_i\) are the virtual mass, damping, and
stiffness in direction \(i\).
The critical damping value is given by

\begin{equation}
D_i = 2\sqrt{M_iK_i}.
\label{eq:critical_damping_general}
\end{equation}

In the robot implementation, the mass value is not assumed to be one.
Instead, the directional mass is taken from the virtual Cartesian mass matrix
obtained from the libfranka model quantities. If this matrix is denoted by
\(M_x\), it can be computed from the joint-space mass matrix \(M_q(q)\) and
the Jacobian \(J(q)\) as in operational-space dynamics \cite{Khatib1987}

\begin{equation}
M_x
=
\left(J(q)M_q^{-1}(q)J^\top(q)\right)^{-1}.
\label{eq:cartesian_virtual_mass_matrix}
\end{equation}

The mass associated with principal direction \(i\) is then

\begin{equation}
M_i = (M_x)_{ii}.
\label{eq:directional_virtual_mass}
\end{equation}

The corresponding critical damping reference therefore becomes

\begin{equation}
D_i = 2\sqrt{(M_x)_{ii}K_i},
\qquad i=1,2,3.
\label{eq:critical_damping_directional_mass}
\end{equation}

In the implemented controller, however, the damping values are selected
explicitly as controller parameters rather than being computed from this
critical damping expression. Thus, the positional damping matrix in the
end-effector frame is

\begin{equation}
D_{p,\mathrm{EE}}
=
\operatorname{diag}
\left(
D_1,
D_2,
D_3
\right).
\label{eq:positional_damping_ee_selected}
\end{equation}

The damping matrix expressed in the base frame is then

\begin{equation}
D_{p,0}
=
R_{\mathrm{EE}}
D_{p,\mathrm{EE}}
R_{\mathrm{EE}}^\top .
\label{eq:positional_damping_base}
\end{equation}

The same construction can be applied to the rotational stiffness matrix
\(K_{R,0}\) and the rotational damping matrix \(D_{R,0}\).
In this case, rotational stiffness is expressed in \(\mathrm{Nm/rad}\),
whereas positional stiffness is expressed in \(\mathrm{N/m}\).

For the full Cartesian impedance law, the translational and rotational
stiffness matrices can be collected into one block matrix. In the
end-effector frame this matrix is

\begin{equation}
K_{\mathrm{EE}}
=
\begin{bmatrix}
K_{p,\mathrm{EE}} & 0\\
0 & K_{R,\mathrm{EE}}
\end{bmatrix}.
\label{eq:cartesian_stiffness_ee_block}
\end{equation}

The corresponding block rotation matrix is

\begin{equation}
T_{R,\mathrm{EE}}
=
\begin{bmatrix}
R_{\mathrm{EE}} & 0\\
0 & R_{\mathrm{EE}}
\end{bmatrix}.
\label{eq:block_rotation_ee}
\end{equation}

Using this block matrix, the full Cartesian stiffness matrix expressed in the
base frame becomes

\begin{equation}
K_0
=
T_{R,\mathrm{EE}}
K_{\mathrm{EE}}
T_{R,\mathrm{EE}}^\top .
\label{eq:cartesian_stiffness_base_block}
\end{equation}

With the Cartesian error vector
\(\mathbf{e}=[\mathbf{e}_{p,0}^{\top}\ \mathbf{e}_{R,0}^{\top}]^{\top}\),
the stiffness part of the Cartesian wrench can be written compactly as

\begin{equation}
\mathbf{F}_{K,0}
=
K_0\mathbf{e}
=
\begin{bmatrix}
K_{p,0}\mathbf{e}_{p,0}\\
K_{R,0}\mathbf{e}_{R,0}
\end{bmatrix}.
\label{eq:cartesian_stiffness_force_block}
\end{equation}

This block notation is only a compact way of writing the same translational
and rotational impedance terms used in \cref{eq:cartesian_force_moment_vector}.
The resulting wrench remains expressed in the base frame and is therefore
mapped to joint torques by \(J^\top(q)\) in the following control law.

\subsection{Virtual Center of Rotation for Axis-Constrained Plane Alignment}
\label{subsec:virtual_center_of_rotation}

In the implemented controller, plane-like behavior is generated with the
axis constraint mode.
For example, choosing
\(\mathrm{fix}_p=[1\;0\;0]\) fixes the \(x\)-position and leaves motion in
the \(y\)- and \(z\)-directions free.
This creates a virtual \(y\)-\(z\) plane without explicitly modeling a
separate wall or surface object.
For such an axis-constrained plane task, the end-effector should not only
regulate the constrained translational direction.
It should also be able to rotate compliantly until the tool face becomes
parallel to the intended plane.
This behavior can be described by the idea of a virtual center of rotation.
Instead of interpreting the rotational compliance as a pure rotation about the
origin of the end-effector frame, the rotation is interpreted as taking place
about a chosen point related to the virtual plane.

Let \(p_{\mathrm{EE}}\in\mathbb{R}^3\) be the end-effector position and let
\(p_c\in\mathbb{R}^3\) be the selected virtual center of rotation.
The vector from the virtual center to the end-effector is then

\begin{equation}
r_c
=
p_{\mathrm{EE}}
-
p_c .
\label{eq:virtual_center_lever_arm}
\end{equation}

The point \(p_c\) may be chosen on the virtual plane, above the virtual plane,
or below the virtual plane.
This choice changes the effective lever arm of the rotational motion.
For a small angular displacement \(\delta \theta\in\mathbb{R}^3\), the
corresponding displacement of the end-effector caused by rotation about
\(p_c\) can be approximated by

\begin{equation}
\delta p_{\mathrm{rot}}
\approx
\delta \theta
\times
r_c .
\label{eq:virtual_center_position_coupling}
\end{equation}

Equation~\eqref{eq:virtual_center_position_coupling} shows why the virtual
center of rotation couples orientation and position.
If the virtual center is moved farther away from the end-effector, the same
angular displacement produces a larger translational displacement.
If the virtual center is placed on the virtual plane, the resulting motion
resembles a rotation around a point on that plane.
If it is placed above or below the virtual plane, the tool rotates around a
point offset from the plane, which changes the self-aligning behavior.

In a simplified virtual-center formulation compatible with the Cartesian
impedance structure used in this work, this coupling can be included directly
in the task error.
The coupled differential motion of the end-effector can be written as

\begin{equation}
\delta x_c
=
\begin{bmatrix}
\delta p
-
\delta \theta \times r_c\\
\delta \theta
\end{bmatrix}.
\label{eq:virtual_center_task_error}
\end{equation}

The stiffness and damping matrices would then be applied to this coupled task
error instead of to position and orientation as two completely independent
parts.
For the finite-error controller, this leads to the coupled translational error

\begin{equation}
\mathbf{e}_{p,c}
=
\mathbf{e}_p
-
\mathbf{e}_R
\times
\mathbf{r}_c .
\label{eq:virtual_center_coupled_position_error}
\end{equation}

The translational impedance force can then be computed from
\(\mathbf{e}_{p,c}\), while the rotational impedance moment is still computed from
\(\mathbf{e}_R\):

\begin{equation}
\mathbf{f}_c
=
K_p \mathbf{e}_{p,c}
-
D_p\dot{\mathbf{p}}_{\mathrm{EE}},
\qquad
\mathbf{m}_R
=
K_R \mathbf{e}_R
-
D_R\omega_{\mathrm{EE}} .
\label{eq:virtual_center_force_moment}
\end{equation}

Because the force is generated at the end-effector while the selected virtual
center is located at \(\mathbf{p}_c\), the lever arm \(\mathbf{r}_c\) also produces a moment

\begin{equation}
m_{\mathrm{lever}}
=
r_c
\times
f_c .
\label{eq:virtual_center_lever_moment}
\end{equation}

The wrench used by the torque controller is therefore

\begin{equation}
F_c
=
\begin{bmatrix}
f_c\\
m_R + r_c\times f_c
\end{bmatrix}.
\label{eq:virtual_center_corrected_wrench}
\end{equation}

This formulation is not the full spatial impedance formulation of
Fasse and Broenink \cite{Fasse1997SpatialImpedance}.
It is a simpler virtual-center coupling that preserves the main physical idea:
the selectable point \(p_c\) influences both the translational error and the
moment generated by the translational force.
Thus, translation and rotation are no longer treated as completely independent
springs.

In the implemented controller of this work, the full selectable remote-center
mapping was not implemented as an independent control mode.
Instead, self-alignment is approximated through rotational compliance: the
rotational stiffness and damping values are chosen so that the end-effector can
rotate about the compliant axes while the constrained translational axes maintain
the desired axis-constrained plane behavior.
For a known virtual plane, the controller can therefore keep or regulate the
tool orientation that is defined by the desired orientation \(R_d\).
For example, \(R_d\) can be chosen such that the tool frame is parallel to the
intended plane.
However, the controller does not automatically discover an unknown physical
surface and does not self-align to such a surface purely from contact.
It regulates the translational and rotational directions selected by
\(\mathrm{fix}_p\) and \(\mathrm{fix}_R\), based on the reference orientation
that is defined by the user.
Thus, it can maintain a chosen orientation for a known task, but it is not a
general surface-detection or contact-based self-alignment controller.
It also does not explicitly enforce a user-selectable virtual center above,
below, or on the virtual plane.
Therefore, the virtual center of rotation is treated as an extension of the
task-to-impedance mapping rather than as a fully evaluated part of the final
implementation.

\subsection{Joint-Torque Control Law}
\label{sec:control_law}
%% ─────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────

After defining the Cartesian impedance wrench, the final step is to convert it
into joint torques that can be commanded to the robot actuators.
This mapping is performed using the transpose of the Jacobian
\cite{Khatib1987}.

The Cartesian wrench is given by

\begin{equation}
\mathbf{F} =
\begin{bmatrix}
\mathbf{f}\\
\mathbf{m}
\end{bmatrix}
\in \mathbb{R}^6,
\label{eq:cartesian_force_moment_control_law}
\end{equation}

where \(\mathbf{f} \in \mathbb{R}^3\) is the Cartesian force and
\(\mathbf{m} \in \mathbb{R}^3\) is the Cartesian moment.
Using the principle of virtual work, this wrench is mapped to joint torques
\cite{Khatib1987} by

\begin{equation}
\boldsymbol{\tau}
=
J^\top(q)\mathbf{F}.
\label{eq:joint_torque_mapping}
\end{equation}

Here, \(\boldsymbol{\tau} \in \mathbb{R}^n\) is the joint torque vector,

\begin{equation}
\boldsymbol{\tau} =
\begin{bmatrix}
\tau_1 & \tau_2 & \cdots & \tau_n
\end{bmatrix}^\top,
\label{eq:joint_torque_vector}
\end{equation}

where each \(\tau_i\) is measured in \(\mathrm{Nm}\).
The matrix \(J^\top(q) \in \mathbb{R}^{n \times 6}\) is the transpose of the
Jacobian and maps the Cartesian wrench into joint torques.

Substituting the Cartesian impedance forces and moments gives the complete
Cartesian impedance control law:

\begin{equation}
\boldsymbol{\tau}
=
J^\top(q)
\begin{bmatrix}
K_{p,0} \mathbf{e}_p - D_{p,0} \dot{\mathbf{p}}_{\mathrm{EE}}\\
K_{R,0} \mathbf{e}_R - D_{R,0} \omega_{\mathrm{EE}}
\end{bmatrix}.
\label{eq:cartesian_impedance_control_law}
\end{equation}

This equation is the computational core of the controller.
At each control cycle, the current end-effector transformation is read from
the robot state and compared with the desired transformation.
From this comparison, the positional error \(\mathbf{e}_p\) and rotational error
\(\mathbf{e}_R\) are computed.
The corresponding Cartesian forces and moments are then evaluated and mapped
to joint torques using the Jacobian transpose.

\subsection{Coriolis Compensation in libfranka}

In a general robot-dynamics formulation, the impedance torques are combined
with model-based compensation terms.
These terms compensate for gravity and velocity-dependent effects in the robot
dynamics.
The commanded joint torque can therefore be written as

\begin{equation}
\tau_{\mathrm{cmd}}
=
J^\top(q)\mathbf{F}
+
\tau_g(q)
+
\tau_c(q,\dot{q}).
\label{eq:commanded_torque}
\end{equation}

Here, \(\tau_g(q) \in \mathbb{R}^n\) is the gravity compensation torque, which
depends on the joint configuration \(q\).
The term \(\tau_c(q,\dot{q}) \in \mathbb{R}^n\) compensates for Coriolis and
centrifugal effects, which depend on both the joint configuration \(q\) and
the joint velocity \(\dot{q}\).

This equation represents the general model-compensated impedance-control
structure.
In the libfranka implementation used in this work, the Coriolis vector is read
from the model interface and added explicitly in the callback, while the
gravity compensation is not added as a separate user-level term.
For external joint-torque control, the Franka Control Interface applies the
desired torque command together with robot-side gravity and motor-friction
compensation \cite{FrankaFCI2023}.
Consequently, the implementation returns
\begin{equation}
\tau_{\mathrm{cmd,impl}}
=
J^\top(q)\mathbf{F}
+
\tau_c(q,\dot{q}),
\label{eq:commanded_torque_libfranka}
\end{equation}
or, when the null-space controller is active,
\begin{equation}
\tau_{\mathrm{cmd,impl}}
=
J^\top(q)\mathbf{F}
+
\tau_{\mathrm{null}}
+
\tau_c(q,\dot{q}).
\label{eq:commanded_torque_libfranka_null}
\end{equation}
Thus, gravity is not neglected physically; it is compensated by the robot-side
control interface rather than being added again in the user callback.
This avoids double compensation and keeps the implementation consistent with
the official libfranka Cartesian impedance example.

\subsection{Stability Analysis of the Implemented Controller}
\label{subsec:stability_analysis}

This subsection gives a single stability argument for the controller that is
actually implemented and evaluated in this thesis: the block-diagonal Cartesian
impedance law of \cref{eq:cartesian_impedance_control_law}, with decoupled
translational and rotational springs, together with the projected null-space
term and the model-based Coriolis compensation. The passive self-alignment
observed during table contact is an emergent property of this controller under
contact, produced by the rotational compliance and the contact geometry, rather
than by a separate control mode. It is therefore covered by the same analysis.

The analysis uses the following assumptions: the desired end-effector pose is
constant; gravity and Coriolis effects are exactly compensated; the commanded
torques are not saturated; and the stiffness and damping matrices
\(K_{p,0},\,D_{p,0},\,K_{R,0},\,D_{R,0}\) are symmetric positive definite.
The last property is preserved when diagonal end-effector-frame matrices are
rotated into the base frame: for a symmetric positive definite
\(K_{p,\mathrm{EE}}\) and a rotation \(R_d\),

\begin{equation}
K_{p,0}=R_d K_{p,\mathrm{EE}} R_d^\top
\label{eq:stability_spd_preserved}
\end{equation}

is again symmetric positive definite, and the same holds for the damping and
the rotational matrices. The translational and rotational springs remain
decoupled, so the full Cartesian stiffness and damping are block-diagonal even
though each \(3\times3\) block is generally a full matrix in the base frame.

After exact gravity and Coriolis compensation, the end-effector obeys the
operational-space dynamics \cite{Khatib1987,Siciliano2009}

\begin{equation}
\Lambda(q)\ddot{x}+\Gamma(q,\dot{q})\dot{x}=\mathbf{F},
\label{eq:operational_space_closed_loop}
\end{equation}

where \(\mathbf{F}\) is the commanded impedance wrench,
\(\Lambda(q)=\left(J(q)M_q^{-1}(q)J^\top(q)\right)^{-1}\) is the symmetric
positive definite operational-space inertia of
\cref{eq:cartesian_virtual_mass_matrix}, and \(\Gamma(q,\dot{q})\) is the
corresponding operational-space Coriolis matrix. As in the joint-space model,
\(\dot{\Lambda}-2\Gamma\) is skew-symmetric \cite{Siciliano2009,Ott2008}.

Collect the full Cartesian error and velocity as

\begin{equation}
\mathbf{e}=
\begin{bmatrix}\mathbf{e}_p\\ \mathbf{e}_R\end{bmatrix},
\qquad
\dot{x}=
\begin{bmatrix}\dot{\mathbf{p}}_{\mathrm{EE}}\\ \omega_{\mathrm{EE}}\end{bmatrix},
\qquad
K=
\begin{bmatrix}K_{p,0}&0\\0&K_{R,0}\end{bmatrix},
\qquad
D=
\begin{bmatrix}D_{p,0}&0\\0&D_{R,0}\end{bmatrix},
\label{eq:stability_stacked_quantities}
\end{equation}

so that the commanded wrench is \(\mathbf{F}=K\mathbf{e}-D\dot{x}\). The
block-diagonal structure of \(K\) and \(D\) is exactly the decoupling between
translation and rotation used in the implemented controller. A single Lyapunov
candidate for the complete six-dimensional task is the total virtual mechanical
energy

\begin{equation}
V=\tfrac{1}{2}\mathbf{e}^\top K\mathbf{e}
 +\tfrac{1}{2}\dot{x}^\top \Lambda\,\dot{x}.
\label{eq:unified_lyapunov}
\end{equation}

Since \(K\) and \(\Lambda\) are symmetric positive definite, \(V\) is positive
definite and vanishes only for \(\mathbf{e}=0,\ \dot{x}=0\).

For a constant desired pose the error satisfies \(\dot{\mathbf{e}}=-\dot{x}\).
Differentiating \cref{eq:unified_lyapunov} and inserting
\cref{eq:operational_space_closed_loop} gives

\begin{equation}
\dot{V}
=\mathbf{e}^\top K\dot{\mathbf{e}}
 +\dot{x}^\top\Lambda\ddot{x}
 +\tfrac{1}{2}\dot{x}^\top\dot{\Lambda}\dot{x}
=-\dot{x}^\top D\dot{x}
 +\dot{x}^\top\!\left(\tfrac{1}{2}\dot{\Lambda}-\Gamma\right)\!\dot{x}.
\label{eq:unified_lyapunov_derivative}
\end{equation}

The skew-symmetry of \(\dot{\Lambda}-2\Gamma\) makes the last term vanish, so

\begin{equation}
\dot{V}=-\dot{x}^\top D\,\dot{x}\le 0.
\label{eq:unified_lyapunov_result}
\end{equation}

Thus the damping matrix dissipates energy and the closed loop is stable in the
sense of Lyapunov. Because \(\dot{V}=0\) only for \(\dot{x}=0\), LaSalle's
invariance principle implies that the regulated Cartesian error converges to
zero in this ideal case \cite{Siciliano2009}.

The null-space term does not change this conclusion. By construction
\(\tau_{\mathrm{null}}=-D_{\mathrm{null}}N\dot{q}+N(K_\sigma s\alpha n)\) lies in
the null space of the constrained-task Jacobian, so to first order
\(J_c\dot{q}_{\mathrm{null}}\approx 0\) and it does not contribute to the
regulated Cartesian velocity \(\dot{x}\). Its damping part removes energy from
the redundant joint motion, and the singular-value term is bounded and
norm-limited (\cref{eq:nullspace_torque_limit}). Hence the secondary term acts
only on the redundant degree of freedom and cannot inject energy into the
Cartesian task.

In practice, torque limits, sampling effects, modeling errors, measurement
noise, and the contact interaction itself influence the observed behavior.
Therefore the experimental evaluation in Chapter~\ref{ch:experiments}
additionally examines convergence, steady-state error, overshoot, oscillation,
and commanded-torque limits from the logged data.

\section{Nullspace and Singular Value Decomposition}
\label{sec:nullspace_svd}

The Franka Emika Panda has seven joints, while the Cartesian motion of the
end-effector is described by six degrees of freedom: three translational and
three rotational components.
The robot is therefore kinematically redundant.
This redundancy can be used to pursue additional optimization objectives in
joint space without substantially disturbing the primary Cartesian task.

The relationship between the joint velocities and the Cartesian end-effector
velocity is described by the geometric Jacobian:

\begin{equation}
\dot{x}
=
J(q)\dot{q}.
\label{eq:nullspace_jacobian_relation}
\end{equation}

Here,

\begin{equation}
\dot{x}
=
\begin{bmatrix}
\dot{x} & \dot{y} & \dot{z} &
\omega_x & \omega_y & \omega_z
\end{bmatrix}^{\top}
\in \mathbb{R}^{6}
\label{eq:nullspace_cartesian_velocity}
\end{equation}

is the Cartesian end-effector velocity, and

\begin{equation}
\dot{q}
=
\begin{bmatrix}
\dot{q}_1 & \dot{q}_2 & \cdots & \dot{q}_7
\end{bmatrix}^{\top}
\in \mathbb{R}^{7}
\label{eq:nullspace_joint_velocity}
\end{equation}

is the joint velocity vector.
For the Panda, the Jacobian has the dimension

\begin{equation}
J(q) \in \mathbb{R}^{6 \times 7}.
\label{eq:nullspace_jacobian_dimension}
\end{equation}

Since the joint space has seven dimensions while the full Cartesian task has
six dimensions, a one-dimensional null space exists when the Jacobian has full
rank.
A joint velocity in this null space satisfies

\begin{equation}
J(q)\dot{q}_0 = 0.
\label{eq:jacobian_nullspace_condition}
\end{equation}

The velocity \(\dot{q}_0\) therefore produces no first-order Cartesian
end-effector velocity.
It can be used to execute a secondary joint-space task while preserving the
primary Cartesian task.

In this work, however, the controller does not always regulate the complete
six-dimensional Cartesian task.
The axis-selection mode can release individual translational or rotational
axes.
For the null-space optimization, a constrained-task Jacobian is therefore used:

\begin{equation}
J_c \in \mathbb{R}^{m \times 7},
\label{eq:active_jacobian_dimension}
\end{equation}

where \(m\) is the number of axes that are actually fixed or regulated.
The constrained-task Jacobian is formed by selecting the \(m\) rows of the full Jacobian
that correspond to the regulated Cartesian components:

\begin{equation}
J_c
=
\begin{bmatrix}
J_{a_1}\\
J_{a_2}\\
\vdots\\
J_{a_m}
\end{bmatrix}
\in \mathbb{R}^{m \times 7},
\label{eq:active_jacobian_general}
\end{equation}

where each index \(a_i\) denotes one selected translational or rotational row
of the full Cartesian Jacobian.

The null space of \(J_c\) therefore describes all joint motions
that have minimal influence on the regulated axes:

\begin{equation}
J_c\dot{q}_0 \approx 0.
\label{eq:active_jacobian_nullspace_condition}
\end{equation}

A damped null-space projector is used to project a joint-space vector into
this null space. This follows the general idea of null-space projection for
redundant manipulators, with the damping term improving numerical robustness
near singular configurations \cite{Khatib1987,Dietrich2015NullspaceSurvey,Chiaverini1994DampedLeastSquares}:

\begin{equation}
N
=
I
-
J_c^{\top}
\left(
J_cJ_c^{\top}
+
\lambda^2 I
\right)^{-1}
J_c.
\label{eq:damped_nullspace_projector}
\end{equation}

The parameter \(\lambda\) is a damping factor.
The damping improves numerical robustness, especially when \(J_c\)
is poorly conditioned or the robot is close to a singular configuration.
The projector has the dimension

\begin{equation}
N \in \mathbb{R}^{7 \times 7}.
\label{eq:nullspace_projector_dimension}
\end{equation}

The projector \(N\) separates a seven-dimensional joint-space vector into the
part that affects the constrained Cartesian task and the part that remains
approximately invisible to it.
For a generic joint-space vector \(v\in\mathbb{R}^7\), the null-space
component is

\begin{equation}
v_{\mathrm{null}}
=
Nv.
\label{eq:nullspace_component_projected}
\end{equation}

The complementary task-relevant component is

\begin{equation}
v_{\mathrm{task}}
=
\left(
I
-
N
\right)
v.
\label{eq:task_relevant_component_active_projector}
\end{equation}

The original vector can then be written as

\begin{equation}
v
=
v_{\mathrm{task}}
+
v_{\mathrm{null}}.
\label{eq:joint_vector_task_null_decomposition}
\end{equation}

Because only the rows contained in \(J_c\) are treated as constrained task
directions, applying \(N\) gives

\begin{equation}
J_cNv
\approx
0.
\label{eq:nullspace_projected_vector_task_effect}
\end{equation}

This relation holds up to the residual introduced by damping and numerical
conditioning.
Thus, secondary joint-space commands can be projected with \(N\) without
substantially disturbing the regulated Cartesian axes.

For example, \(N\) can be used to project a joint-space velocity into the
null space of the constrained task:

\begin{equation}
\dot{q}_{\mathrm{null}}
=
N\dot{q}.
\label{eq:nullspace_velocity_projection}
\end{equation}

In addition, the singular value decomposition (SVD) of \(J_c\) is
used.
The SVD is a standard matrix factorization that can be
applied to any real matrix.
It is useful here because it describes how a joint-space velocity is amplified
or weakened when it is mapped into the constrained Cartesian task space
\cite{Chiaverini1994DampedLeastSquares,Dietrich2015NullspaceSurvey}.
For the constrained-task Jacobian it is written as

\begin{equation}
J_c
=
U\Sigma V^{\top}.
\label{eq:active_jacobian_svd}
\end{equation}

This equation is not a new robot-specific control law.
It comes from the general SVD of a matrix
\(A\in\mathbb{R}^{m\times n}\), which states that

\begin{equation}
A
=
U\Sigma V^{\top},
\label{eq:general_svd}
\end{equation}

where \(U\) and \(V\) are orthonormal matrices and \(\Sigma\) is a diagonal
matrix containing non-negative singular values.
In this work, the general matrix \(A\) is \(J_c\).
The columns of \(V\) describe orthonormal directions in joint-velocity space,
and the columns of \(U\) describe the corresponding orthonormal directions in
the constrained task space.
The singular values in \(\Sigma\) give the scaling between these two sets of
directions.
If a joint velocity is applied exactly in the direction of one column of
\(V\), the resulting constrained Cartesian velocity points in the corresponding
column direction of \(U\), scaled by the corresponding singular value
\(\sigma_i\).

For
\(J_c\in\mathbb{R}^{m\times7}\), the matrices have the
dimensions

\begin{equation}
U\in\mathbb{R}^{m\times m},
\qquad
\Sigma\in\mathbb{R}^{m\times7},
\qquad
V\in\mathbb{R}^{7\times7}.
\label{eq:active_svd_dimensions}
\end{equation}

The diagonal entries of the nonzero part of \(\Sigma\) are the singular values:

\begin{equation}
\Sigma
=
\begin{bmatrix}
\sigma_1 & 0 & \cdots & 0\\
0 & \sigma_2 & \cdots & 0\\
\vdots & \vdots & \ddots & \vdots\\
0 & 0 & \cdots & \sigma_m
\end{bmatrix},
\qquad
\sigma_i\geq0.
\label{eq:singular_value_matrix}
\end{equation}

This displayed matrix shows the diagonal part of \(\Sigma\).
Since the constrained-task Jacobian has seven columns, \(\Sigma\) has seven columns as
well; if \(m<7\), the remaining columns are zero.

The singular values can be computed from the eigenvalues of
\(J_cJ_c^{\top}\).
The reason for multiplying the constrained-task Jacobian by its transpose is that
\(J_c\) is generally not square:

\begin{equation}
J_c
\in
\mathbb{R}^{m\times7}.
\end{equation}

Eigenvalues are defined for square matrices.
Therefore, the rectangular Jacobian is converted into the square symmetric
matrix

\begin{equation}
J_cJ_c^{\top}
\in
\mathbb{R}^{m\times m}.
\end{equation}

This matrix acts in the constrained Cartesian task space.
Its eigenvectors \(u_i\) describe orthonormal directions in the constrained task
space, and its eigenvalues describe how strongly the robot can produce motion
in those directions.
The same nonzero singular values could also be obtained from
\(J_c^{\top}J_c\), which is a
\(7\times7\) joint-space matrix.
In this work, \(J_cJ_c^{\top}\) is used
because it has the smaller dimension \(m\times m\) and directly describes the
regulated Cartesian directions.
If

\begin{equation}
J_cJ_c^{\top}u_i
=
\lambda_i u_i,
\label{eq:active_jacobian_eigenvalue_problem}
\end{equation}

then the corresponding singular value is

\begin{equation}
\sigma_i
=
\sqrt{\lambda_i}.
\label{eq:singular_value_from_eigenvalue}
\end{equation}

This relation comes directly from the SVD.
Starting from \(J_c=U\Sigma V^{\top}\), one obtains

\begin{equation}
J_cJ_c^{\top}
=
U\Sigma V^{\top}V\Sigma^{\top}U^{\top}
=
U\Sigma\Sigma^{\top}U^{\top}.
\label{eq:svd_eigenvalue_relation}
\end{equation}

Because \(V^{\top}V=I\), the middle term disappears.
The diagonal entries of \(\Sigma\Sigma^{\top}\) are
\(\sigma_i^2\).
Therefore the eigenvalues of
\(J_cJ_c^{\top}\) are
\(\lambda_i=\sigma_i^2\), and the singular values are obtained as
\(\sigma_i=\sqrt{\lambda_i}\).

The same eigenvalue calculation also gives the left singular vectors.
For every nonzero eigenvalue \(\lambda_i\), the vector \(u_i\) is the
corresponding normalized eigenvector of
\(J_cJ_c^{\top}\):

\begin{equation}
J_cJ_c^{\top}u_i
=
\lambda_i u_i,
\qquad
\|u_i\|
=
1.
\label{eq:left_singular_vector_from_eigenproblem}
\end{equation}

After \(u_i\) and \(\sigma_i\) are known, the corresponding right singular
vector \(v_i\) can be calculated from the defining relation

\begin{equation}
J_cv_i
=
\sigma_i u_i.
\label{eq:singular_vector_defining_relation}
\end{equation}

A vector that satisfies this relation can be obtained by projecting \(u_i\)
back into joint space:

\begin{equation}
v_i
=
\frac{1}{\sigma_i}
J_c^{\top}u_i,
\qquad
\sigma_i>0.
\label{eq:right_singular_vector_from_left}
\end{equation}

Indeed, substituting this expression gives

\begin{equation}
J_cv_i
=
\frac{1}{\sigma_i}
J_cJ_c^{\top}u_i
=
\frac{\lambda_i}{\sigma_i}u_i
=
\sigma_i u_i,
\label{eq:right_singular_vector_verification}
\end{equation}

because \(\lambda_i=\sigma_i^2\).
Thus, the mathematical construction of the SVD can be summarized as follows:
first solve the eigenvalue problem of
\(J_cJ_c^{\top}\) to obtain \(u_i\) and
\(\lambda_i\); then compute \(\sigma_i=\sqrt{\lambda_i}\); finally compute
\(v_i=J_c^{\top}u_i/\sigma_i\) for every nonzero singular
value.
The matrices \(U\) and \(V\) are then formed by collecting these vectors as
columns:

\begin{equation}
U
=
\begin{bmatrix}
u_1 & u_2 & \cdots & u_m
\end{bmatrix},
\qquad
V
=
\begin{bmatrix}
v_1 & v_2 & \cdots & v_7
\end{bmatrix}.
\label{eq:svd_u_v_column_construction}
\end{equation}

If \(J_c\) has rank \(r<7\), only the first \(r\) right
singular vectors are obtained from
\eqref{eq:right_singular_vector_from_left}.
The remaining vectors \(v_{r+1},\ldots,v_7\) are chosen as orthonormal vectors
that complete the basis of the seven-dimensional joint space and satisfy

\begin{equation}
J_cv_i
=
0,
\qquad
i=r+1,\ldots,7.
\label{eq:right_singular_vectors_nullspace}
\end{equation}

These remaining right singular vectors span the null space of the
constrained-task Jacobian.
In numerical software such as Eigen, this construction is carried out
automatically by the SVD algorithm, but the mathematical meaning is exactly the
one described above.

Thus, the singular values measure how strongly joint-space motion can produce
motion in the regulated Cartesian directions.
This can be seen from the differential kinematic relation

\begin{equation}
\dot{x}_{c}
=
J_c\dot{q}.
\label{eq:active_differential_kinematics_svd}
\end{equation}

Here, \(\dot{q}\) is a joint-space velocity direction and
\(\dot{x}_{c}\) is the resulting velocity in the constrained Cartesian task
space.
Using the SVD,

\begin{equation}
\dot{x}_{c}
=
U\Sigma V^{\top}\dot{q}.
\label{eq:active_svd_velocity_mapping}
\end{equation}

The columns of \(V\) are directions in joint space, while the columns of
\(U\) are the corresponding directions in the constrained Cartesian task space.
The singular values in \(\Sigma\) are the scaling factors between these two
spaces.
For example, if the joint velocity is chosen in the \(i\)-th right singular
direction \(v_i\), then

\begin{equation}
\dot{q}
=
v_i,
\qquad
J_cv_i
=
\sigma_i u_i.
\label{eq:singular_value_direction_mapping}
\end{equation}

This means that the Jacobian maps the joint-space direction \(v_i\) into the
constrained Cartesian direction \(u_i\), and the singular value \(\sigma_i\)
describes how much this direction is amplified or weakened.
Large singular values indicate directions in which a small joint motion
produces a relatively large motion in the constrained Cartesian task space.
Small singular values indicate weak directions, where joint motion produces
only a small constrained Cartesian motion and the robot is close to losing the
ability to generate motion in one regulated direction.
The smallest singular value is therefore defined as

\begin{equation}
\sigma_{\min}
=
\min_i \sigma_i,
\label{eq:min_singular_value}
\end{equation}

and is used as a measure of the kinematic conditioning of the robot with
respect to the constrained task.
A small value of \(\sigma_{\min}\) indicates proximity to a singular or poorly
conditioned configuration, whereas a larger value indicates better
conditioning \cite{Chiaverini1994DampedLeastSquares}.
The controller therefore tries to choose null-space motions that increase
\(\sigma_{\min}\), while the constrained Cartesian task remains preserved by the
projector \(N\).

For the optimization, a small motion in a null-space direction \(n\) is
considered.
This direction is obtained from the SVD of the constrained-task Jacobian. With

\begin{equation}
J_c
=
U\Sigma V^{\top},
\end{equation}

the matrix \(V\) contains joint-space directions,

\begin{equation}
V
=
\begin{bmatrix}
v_1 & v_2 & \cdots & v_7
\end{bmatrix}.
\end{equation}

A null-space direction is a joint-space direction that produces no, or only a
very small, motion in the constrained Cartesian task space:

\begin{equation}
J_cn
\approx
0.
\end{equation}

The reason for using the last column of \(V\) is the ordering used in the SVD.
The right singular vectors in \(V\) are ordered in the same sequence as the
singular values:

\begin{equation}
J_cv_i
=
\sigma_i u_i.
\label{eq:svd_direction_mapping}
\end{equation}

This means that a joint-space motion in direction \(v_i\) produces a
constrained Cartesian motion in direction \(u_i\), scaled by \(\sigma_i\).
Here, \(v_i\) is not the current joint configuration \(q\).
It is a direction for a joint displacement.
Equivalently, it can be interpreted as a normalized \(\Delta q\) direction.
The actual small joint displacement is obtained by multiplying this direction
with a step size:

\begin{equation}
\Delta q
=
\alpha v_i.
\label{eq:svd_direction_as_joint_displacement}
\end{equation}

The corresponding candidate joint configuration is then

\begin{equation}
q_{\mathrm{new}}
=
q
+
\Delta q
=
q
+
\alpha v_i.
\label{eq:svd_direction_candidate_configuration}
\end{equation}

Thus, \(q\) describes where the robot currently is in joint space, while
\(v_i\) describes in which joint-space direction the robot is locally moved.
The singular values are ordered from large to small.
Therefore, \(v_1\) is the joint-space direction with the strongest effect on
the constrained Cartesian task, while the last columns of \(V\) are the directions
with the weakest effect.
For a seven-joint robot, the last column is \(v_7\).
If the constrained task has fewer independent directions than seven, some of the
last singular values are zero, or close to zero in the damped numerical case.
The corresponding vectors are then null-space directions.
Thus, \(v_7\) is not weak because it belongs to joint 7; it is weak because it
is the last direction in the SVD ordering.

For the secondary optimization, the controller does not test every right
singular vector.
It uses one representative weak direction, because directions associated with
large singular values would strongly disturb the constrained Cartesian task.
Since the SVD orders the singular vectors according to decreasing singular
values, the weakest available direction is the last column of \(V\).
For the seven-joint Panda, the tested direction is therefore

\begin{equation}
n
=
v_7.
\label{eq:nullspace_search_direction}
\end{equation}

The vector \(v_7\) is not selected manually.
It is calculated automatically as the seventh column of the matrix \(V\)
obtained from the SVD of \(J_c\).
The index \(7\) in \(v_7\) does not mean the scalar joint coordinate \(q_7\).
It means the seventh SVD direction vector.
Because the robot has seven joints, this vector has one component for each
joint:

\begin{equation}
v_7
=
\begin{bmatrix}
v_{7,1} &
v_{7,2} &
\cdots &
v_{7,7}
\end{bmatrix}^{\top}
\in
\mathbb{R}^{7}.
\label{eq:last_right_singular_vector_components}
\end{equation}

Each entry \(v_{7,j}\) describes how much joint \(j\) participates in this
joint-space direction.
Larger absolute entries mean that the corresponding joints contribute more to
this direction, while entries close to zero mean that the corresponding joints
contribute only weakly.
The sign of each entry determines the positive or negative joint direction.
Therefore, \(v_7\) does not describe a Cartesian direction.
It describes a coordinated direction of motion for all seven joints.
It is suitable as a search direction for the secondary optimization because it
changes the constrained Cartesian task as little as possible.
The local test then only has to compare the two signs of this direction,
\(+v_7\) and \(-v_7\).
Two candidate configurations are formed:

\begin{equation}
q_{+} = q + \alpha n,
\label{eq:nullspace_candidate_plus}
\end{equation}

\begin{equation}
q_{-} = q - \alpha n.
\label{eq:nullspace_candidate_minus}
\end{equation}

The parameter \(\alpha\) is a small step size in joint space.
For both candidates, the constrained-task Jacobian is recomputed:

\begin{equation}
J_{c,+}
=
J_c(q_{+}),
\label{eq:active_jacobian_candidate_plus}
\end{equation}

\begin{equation}
J_{c,-}
=
J_c(q_{-}).
\label{eq:active_jacobian_candidate_minus}
\end{equation}

Here, \(J_c(q_{+})\) and \(J_c(q_{-})\)
mean that the Jacobian is evaluated again at the two candidate joint
configurations.
First, the forward kinematics and the full geometric Jacobian are recomputed
for \(q_{+}\) and \(q_{-}\).
Then the same row selection used for the real configuration \(q\) is
applied again.
Thus, only the rows corresponding to the regulated Cartesian axes are kept:

\begin{equation}
J_{c,+}
=
S_cJ(q_{+}),
\qquad
J_{c,-}
=
S_cJ(q_{-}).
\label{eq:active_jacobian_candidate_row_selection}
\end{equation}

The matrix \(S_c\) represents the row-selection operation
defined by the constrained translational and rotational axes.
It is directly constructed from the binary axis-selection vectors
\(\mathrm{fixp}\) and \(\mathrm{fixR}\).
These vectors are combined into one constrained-axis vector

\begin{equation}
a
=
\begin{bmatrix}
\mathrm{fixp}_x &
\mathrm{fixp}_y &
\mathrm{fixp}_z &
\mathrm{fixR}_x &
\mathrm{fixR}_y &
\mathrm{fixR}_z
\end{bmatrix}^{\top}
\in
\{0,1\}^{6}.
\label{eq:active_axis_vector_from_fixp_fixr}
\end{equation}

The entries of \(a\) correspond to the row order

\begin{equation}
\begin{bmatrix}
x &
y &
z &
R_x &
R_y &
R_z
\end{bmatrix}.
\label{eq:active_axis_row_order}
\end{equation}

An entry \(a_i=1\) means that the corresponding Cartesian axis is regulated and
therefore its Jacobian row is kept.
An entry \(a_i=0\) means that the corresponding Cartesian axis is free and its
Jacobian row is removed.
Let

\begin{equation}
\mathcal{I}_{c}
=
\left\{
i
\in
\{1,\ldots,6\}
\mid
a_i=1
\right\}
=
\left\{
i_1,\ldots,i_m
\right\}
\label{eq:active_axis_index_set}
\end{equation}

be the set of constrained row indices, where

\begin{equation}
m
=
\sum_{i=1}^{6}a_i
\label{eq:number_of_active_axes_from_fix_vector}
\end{equation}

is the number of regulated Cartesian axes.
Using the standard unit row vectors \(e_i^{\top}\in\mathbb{R}^{1\times6}\),
the selection matrix is

\begin{equation}
S_c
=
\begin{bmatrix}
e_{i_1}^{\top}\\
e_{i_2}^{\top}\\
\vdots\\
e_{i_m}^{\top}
\end{bmatrix}
\in
\mathbb{R}^{m\times6}.
\label{eq:active_selection_matrix_definition}
\end{equation}

It is not an additional optimization variable and it does not choose the
null-space direction.
Its only role is to extract from the full geometric Jacobian the rows that
belong to the currently regulated Cartesian axes.
If \(m\) Cartesian axes are constrained, then

\begin{equation}
S_c
\in
\mathbb{R}^{m\times6},
\qquad
J(q)
\in
\mathbb{R}^{6\times7},
\qquad
J_c(q)
=
S_cJ(q)
\in
\mathbb{R}^{m\times7}.
\label{eq:active_selection_matrix_dimension}
\end{equation}

Thus, \(S_c\) keeps the optimization consistent with the same
task that is used by the controller.
For example, if only \(m\) of the six Cartesian directions are regulated, the
smallest singular value is evaluated only for these \(m\) constrained directions,
not for the full six-dimensional Cartesian task.
The controller therefore checks how the constrained-task Jacobian would change
if the robot moved slightly in the positive or negative null-space direction.
The vector \(v_7\) is used only to define the two candidate configurations
\(q_{+}\) and \(q_{-}\).
After these candidate configurations are formed, the controller computes the
complete constrained-task Jacobian for each candidate again.
Therefore, the comparison is not made only on the seventh component of a
vector.
Instead, it is made on the smallest singular value of the complete candidate
constrained-task Jacobian.

The corresponding smallest singular values are then compared:

\begin{equation}
\sigma_{\min}^{+}
=
\sigma_{\min}
\left(
J_{c,+}
\right),
\label{eq:min_singular_candidate_plus}
\end{equation}

\begin{equation}
\sigma_{\min}^{-}
=
\sigma_{\min}
\left(
J_{c,-}
\right).
\label{eq:min_singular_candidate_minus}
\end{equation}

The variable \(s\) is only a sign selector.
It is introduced because the controller does not yet know whether moving a
small step along \(+n\) or along \(-n\) gives the better robot
configuration.
After the two candidates from \cref{eq:nullspace_candidate_plus} and
\cref{eq:nullspace_candidate_minus} have been evaluated, the sign \(s\)
stores which of the two directions should be used.
If the positive candidate \(q_{+}\) gives the larger smallest singular value,
then \(s=+1\).
Otherwise, \(s=-1\), so the controller uses the negative direction:

\begin{equation}
s
=
\begin{cases}
+1, & \text{if } \sigma_{\min}^{+} > \sigma_{\min}^{-},\\
-1, & \text{otherwise}.
\end{cases}
\label{eq:nullspace_direction_selection}
\end{equation}

Thus, \(s\) does not describe a new direction by itself.
It only chooses the sign of the already selected null-space direction \(n\).
The resulting joint-space change used by the optimization is therefore

\begin{equation}
\Delta q_{\sigma}
=
s
\alpha
n.
\label{eq:sigma_joint_step_from_sign}
\end{equation}

This comparison is a finite-difference approximation of the direction in which
\(\sigma_{\min}\) increases. The optimization is not an additional Cartesian
task; it is a secondary joint-space objective that is projected through \(N\)
so that the regulated Cartesian axes are disturbed as little as possible.
Thus, \(\Delta q_{\sigma}\) is a small joint-space step toward the candidate
with better constrained-task conditioning.
Multiplication by \(K_{\sigma}\) scales this step into the strength of the
secondary command.
In the general joint-space form, \(K_{\sigma}\) is a weighting matrix with one
row and one column for each robot joint:

\begin{equation}
K_{\sigma}
\in
\mathbb{R}^{7\times7}.
\label{eq:ksigma_dimension}
\end{equation}

This matrix can weight the contribution of the singular-value optimization
differently for each joint.
If the same weight is used for all joints, it reduces to the scalar special
case

\begin{equation}
K_{\sigma}
=
k_{\sigma}I_7,
\qquad
k_{\sigma}\in\mathbb{R}.
\label{eq:ksigma_scalar_special_case}
\end{equation}

Finally, the null-space projector \(N\) keeps only the part of this command
that lies in the null space of the constrained task and therefore does not
significantly disturb the regulated Cartesian directions.

The resulting optimization term in the null space is

\begin{equation}
\tau_{\sigma}
=
N
\left(
K_{\sigma}s\alpha n
\right).
\label{eq:singular_value_optimization_torque}
\end{equation}

Here, \(K_{\sigma}\in\mathbb{R}^{7\times7}\) is the joint-space weighting
matrix for the singular-value optimization, \(s\) is the selected sign,
\(\alpha\) is the joint-space step size, and \(n\) is the tested null-space
direction.
Thus, \cref{eq:singular_value_optimization_torque} comes from the finite
difference selection in \cref{eq:nullspace_direction_selection} and
\cref{eq:nullspace_candidate_plus,eq:nullspace_candidate_minus}.
The term is projected with the null-space projector \(N\) so that the
constrained Cartesian task is influenced as little as possible.
Consequently, the singular-value optimization only uses the remaining
redundant degrees of freedom after the constrained task has been defined.

A damping term is also used in the null space:

\begin{equation}
\tau_{\mathrm{damp}}
=
-
D_{\mathrm{null}}
N\dot{q}.
\label{eq:nullspace_damping_torque}
\end{equation}

The complete null-space term is therefore

\begin{equation}
\tau_{\mathrm{null}}
=
\tau_{\mathrm{damp}}
+
\tau_{\sigma}.
\label{eq:nullspace_torque}
\end{equation}

This term has the dimension

\begin{equation}
\tau_{\mathrm{null}} \in \mathbb{R}^{7},
\label{eq:nullspace_torque_dimension}
\end{equation}

because it is added directly to the seven joint torques.
The complete torque command of the controller can therefore be written as

\begin{equation}
\tau_{\mathrm{cmd}}
=
J^{\top}F_{\mathrm{cmd}}
+
\tau_{\mathrm{null}}
+
\tau_c.
\label{eq:nullspace_complete_torque_command}
\end{equation}

The term \(J^{\top}F_{\mathrm{cmd}}\) describes the Cartesian impedance
torque, \(\tau_{\mathrm{null}}\) describes the null-space optimization, and
\(\tau_c\) is the Coriolis compensation term added in the user callback.
The gravity compensation is handled by the Franka Control Interface for the
external torque command, as explained around
Equations~\eqref{eq:commanded_torque_libfranka} and
\eqref{eq:commanded_torque_libfranka_null}.
The null-space optimization in this work is used only for damping redundant
motion and for improving the smallest singular value.
Improving the smallest singular value means that the redundant joints are
biased toward a better-conditioned configuration of the constrained-task Jacobian.
This helps keep the regulated Cartesian directions controllable and avoids
configurations in which one constrained direction becomes weak or close to singular.
