This chapter introduces all mathematical concepts required to derive and
understand the Cartesian impedance controller used in this thesis.
The presentation follows a deliberate step-by-step order:
it begins with the geometry of coordinate frames, builds up the algebraic
tools for transformations, defines the controlled variable as the
end-effector position and orientation, derives the error signal, and then
develops the kinematic and dynamic machinery that connects joint torques
to Cartesian forces.

\section{Coordinate Systems and Transformations}
\label{sec:coord_systems}
%% ─────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────

\subsection{Reference Frames}

To describe the motion of a robotic manipulator, multiple coordinate
frames are used simultaneously \cite{Siciliano2009, Craig2005}.
A coordinate frame is a right-handed orthogonal system of
three mutually perpendicular unit vectors anchored at a chosen origin.
For the joint frames, this thesis follows the
Denavit--Hartenberg (\abbr{DH}) convention, a standard robotics
method for assigning coordinate frames to consecutive links in a kinematic
chain \cite{Denavit1955, Craig2005}.
Three frames are central to this work:

\begin{itemize}
  \item Base frame $\{0\}$: fixed to the robot mounting
        surface. Its origin is the robot base reference point used by
        the kinematic model. For the upright Panda configuration used in
        this thesis, the $z_0$-axis points normal to the mounting surface
        and is aligned with the first joint axis, while the $x_0$- and
        $y_0$-axes lie in the mounting plane and complete a right-handed
        coordinate system according to the manufacturer convention.
        All positions, orientations, and forces are ultimately expressed in
        this frame unless stated otherwise.
  \item Joint frames $\{1\},\{2\},\ldots,\{n\}$: rigidly
        attached to the successive links of the kinematic chain. With the
        standard Denavit--Hartenberg indexing used below, joint \(i\) rotates
        about \(z_{i-1}\), and \(p_{i-1}\) is a point on that axis. The
        \(x_i\)-axis is chosen along the common normal from \(z_{i-1}\) to
        \(z_i\), and \(y_i\) completes the right-handed frame. These frames
        encode the fixed geometry between consecutive joint axes.
  \item End-effector frame $\{\mathrm{EE}\}$: attached to the tool
        centre point. Its origin is the controlled point at the tool tip
        or flange, depending on the selected tool definition. The axes
        describe the tool orientation: $z_{\mathrm{EE}}$ is taken as the tool approach
        direction, while $x_{\mathrm{EE}}$ and $y_{\mathrm{EE}}$ span the tool plane and complete
        a right-handed frame. The position and orientation of $\{\mathrm{EE}\}$
        relative to $\{0\}$ constitute the controlled position and orientation of the robot.
        In the implementation, this frame is the libfranka end-effector/tool
        frame selected through the robot model interface.
\end{itemize}

The coordinate-frame assignment and the corresponding Denavit--Hartenberg convention for assigning consecutive coordinate systems
are illustrated in \cref{fig:coordinate_frame_robot}.
\begin{figure}[H]
    \centering
    \includegraphics[width=0.95\textwidth]{figures/coordinate_frames.png}
    \caption{Coordinate-frame assignment on the left and Denavit--Hartenberg convention on the right.}
    \label{fig:coordinate_frame_robot}
\end{figure}
This frame assignment forms the basis for describing the robot kinematics using homogeneous transformation matrices.

\subsection{Rigid Transformations}

A position is a vector \(p\in\mathbb{R}^3\). An orientation is represented by a
proper rotation \(R\in SO(3)\), for which
\[
R^\top R=I,\qquad \det R=1,\qquad R^{-1}=R^\top .
\]
For \(R={}^bR_a\), let \(e_i^b\) and \(e_j^a\) denote axes of the reference and
rotated frames. Its direction-cosine entries are
\begin{equation}
R_{ij}=(e_i^b)^\top e_j^a .
\label{eq:direction_cosine_entry}
\end{equation}
Thus column \(j\), rather than row \(j\), contains the \(j\)-th axis of frame
\(\{a\}\) expressed in frame \(\{b\}\) \cite{Craig2005}. The elementary
right-handed rotations used later for the surface tilt are
\begin{equation}
\begin{aligned}
R_x(\alpha)&=
\begin{bmatrix}1&0&0\\0&\cos\alpha&-\sin\alpha\\0&\sin\alpha&\cos\alpha\end{bmatrix},&
R_y(\beta)&=
\begin{bmatrix}\cos\beta&0&\sin\beta\\0&1&0\\-\sin\beta&0&\cos\beta\end{bmatrix},\\
R_z(\gamma)&=
\begin{bmatrix}\cos\gamma&-\sin\gamma&0\\\sin\gamma&\cos\gamma&0\\0&0&1\end{bmatrix}.
\end{aligned}
\label{eq:elementary_rotations}
\end{equation}

Rotation and translation are collected in the homogeneous transformation
\cite{Craig2005,Siciliano2009}
\begin{equation}
{}^bT_a=
\begin{bmatrix}
{}^bR_a & {}^bp_a\\
0^\top & 1
\end{bmatrix}.
\label{eq:homogeneous_transformation}
\end{equation}
It maps a point by
\({}^bp={}^bR_a\,{}^ap+{}^bp_a\), composes as
\({}^cT_a={}^cT_b{}^bT_a\), and has inverse
\begin{equation}
\begin{bmatrix}R&p\\0^\top&1\end{bmatrix}^{-1}
=
\begin{bmatrix}R^\top&-R^\top p\\0^\top&1\end{bmatrix}.
\label{eq:homogeneous_inverse}
\end{equation}

\subsection{End-Effector Pose}
\label{sec:position_orientation}

The controlled pose consists of the end-effector position
\begin{equation}
p_{\mathrm{EE}}
=
\begin{bmatrix}
p_{x,\mathrm{EE}} & p_{y,\mathrm{EE}} & p_{z,\mathrm{EE}}
\end{bmatrix}^{\top}
\in\mathbb{R}^3
\label{eq:end_effector_position}
\end{equation}
and orientation \(R_{\mathrm{EE}}\in SO(3)\), both expressed in the base frame.
They form
\begin{equation}
T_{\mathrm{EE}}
=
\begin{bmatrix}
R_{\mathrm{EE}} & p_{\mathrm{EE}}\\
0^\top & 1
\end{bmatrix}.
\label{eq:end_effector_transformation}
\end{equation}
The implementation reads this transformation directly from
\texttt{state.O\_T\_EE}; no Euler-angle conversion is used.

\subsection{Position and Orientation Error}
\label{subsec:position_orientation_error}

Let the desired pose be
\[
T_d=
\begin{bmatrix}R_d&p_d\\0^\top&1\end{bmatrix}.
\]
For reference, the desired-to-current relative transformation is
\begin{equation}
T_{\mathrm{err}}
=
T_d^{-1}T_{\mathrm{EE}}
=
\begin{bmatrix}
R_d^\top R_{\mathrm{EE}} &
R_d^\top(p_{\mathrm{EE}}-p_d)\\
0^\top&1
\end{bmatrix}.
\label{eq:expanded_transformation_error}
\end{equation}
The controller does not subtract transformation matrices. Its translational
spring uses the restoring error directly in the base frame,
\begin{equation}
e_p=p_d-p_{\mathrm{EE}}.
\label{eq:position_control_error}
\end{equation}

The rotational spring requires the rotation that carries the current
orientation to the desired orientation,
\begin{equation}
\Delta R=R_{\mathrm{EE}}^\top R_d .
\label{eq:relative_rotation_error}
\end{equation}
Let \((\phi,u)\) be its principal axis--angle pair, so that
\(\Delta R=\exp(\phi[u]_\times)\), \(\phi\in[0,\pi]\), and \(\lVert u\rVert=1\)
when \(\phi\ne0\). The local and base-frame rotation errors are
\begin{equation}
e_{R,\mathrm{EE}}=\phi u,
\qquad
e_R=R_{\mathrm{EE}}e_{R,\mathrm{EE}} .
\label{eq:rotation_error_base_frame}
\end{equation}
This sign convention is implementation-critical: a small positive
current-to-desired rotation produces a positive spring moment about the same
base-frame axis. Eigen performs the principal axis--angle extraction, including
the numerically delicate neighborhood of \(\phi=\pi\). The multiplication by
\(R_{\mathrm{EE}}\) puts \(e_R\), the angular velocity, and the commanded
rotational gains in the same base frame.

The six-dimensional controller error is therefore
\begin{equation}
e=
\begin{bmatrix}e_p\\e_R\end{bmatrix}\in\mathbb{R}^6 .
\label{eq:cartesian_error_vector}
\end{equation}

\section{Kinematics of a 7-DOF Manipulator}

For a serial manipulator with
\(q=[q_1,\ldots,q_n]^\top\), forward kinematics is the ordered product
\begin{equation}
{}^0T_n(q)
=
{}^0T_1(q_1)\,
{}^1T_2(q_2)\cdots
{}^{n-1}T_n(q_n).
\label{eq:forward_kinematics_chain}
\end{equation}
In the standard Denavit--Hartenberg convention used here, revolute joint \(i\)
rotates by \(q_i\) about \(z_{i-1}\). Its neighboring-frame transformation is
\cite{Denavit1955,Craig2005,Siciliano2009}
\begin{equation}
{}^{i-1}T_i
=
R_z(q_i)T_z(d_i)T_x(a_i)R_x(\alpha_i)
=
\begin{bmatrix}
c_i & -s_i c_{\alpha i} & s_i s_{\alpha i} & a_i c_i\\
s_i & c_i c_{\alpha i} & -c_i s_{\alpha i} & a_i s_i\\
0 & s_{\alpha i} & c_{\alpha i} & d_i\\
0&0&0&1
\end{bmatrix},
\label{eq:dh_transformation_matrix}
\end{equation}
where \(c_i=\cos q_i\), \(s_i=\sin q_i\),
\(c_{\alpha i}=\cos\alpha_i\), and \(s_{\alpha i}=\sin\alpha_i\).
The parameters \(q_i\) and \(d_i\) act about and along \(z_{i-1}\);
\(a_i\) acts along \(x_i\), and \(\alpha_i\) is the rotation from
\(z_{i-1}\) to \(z_i\) about \(x_i\). This indexing is used consistently in the
Jacobian below. The product in \cref{eq:forward_kinematics_chain} yields
\({}^0T_n=
\left[\begin{smallmatrix}
R_{\mathrm{EE}}&p_{\mathrm{EE}}\\0^\top&1
\end{smallmatrix}\right]\).
In the implemented controller, libfranka supplies this pose and the model
Jacobian; the DH form is retained to define the kinematic convention and
support the numerical example in the appendix.

\subsection{Differential Kinematics and Geometric Jacobian}

The geometric Jacobian maps joint velocity to the base-frame end-effector
twist,
\begin{equation}
\begin{bmatrix}\dot p_{\mathrm{EE}}\\\omega_{\mathrm{EE}}\end{bmatrix}
=
J(q)\dot q,
\qquad
J(q)=\begin{bmatrix}J_1&\cdots&J_n\end{bmatrix}
\in\mathbb{R}^{6\times n}.
\label{eq:jacobian_velocity_relation}
\end{equation}
For revolute joint \(i\), let \(z_{i-1}\) be its unit axis and \(p_{i-1}\) a
point on that axis, both expressed in the base frame. Its column is
\begin{equation}
J_i=
\begin{bmatrix}
z_{i-1}\times\left(p_{\mathrm{EE}}-p_{i-1}\right)\\
z_{i-1}
\end{bmatrix}.
\label{eq:jacobian_column}
\end{equation}
The upper block is the linear velocity generated by the lever from the joint
axis to the TCP; the lower block is the angular velocity. All three quantities
depend on the current configuration, so \(J(q)\) is configuration dependent.

\section{Cartesian Impedance Control}
%% ─────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────

The previous section introduced the Jacobian matrix, which relates joint
velocities to Cartesian end-effector velocities.
In the controller, this relationship is also used to map a Cartesian wrench to
joint torques \cite{Khatib1987}.
Before this mapping is introduced, the desired Cartesian interaction behaviour
is defined using impedance control.

Impedance control imposes a desired dynamic behaviour between the end-effector
motion and the forces acting on it \cite{Hogan1985,Ott2008,Mayr2024CartesianImpedance}.
Instead of commanding only a desired position, the robot is made to behave like
a virtual spring-damper system in Cartesian space.
In this way, deviations from the desired pose generate restoring forces and
moments, while the damping terms reduce oscillations.
The numerical choice of the stiffness and damping gains is therefore part of
the controller design, because the same impedance law can produce very
different contact behaviour depending on the selected values
\cite{Pollayil2023ImpedancePlanning}.

The Cartesian error vector was defined as

\begin{equation}
\mathbf{e}
=
\begin{bmatrix}
\mathbf{e}_p\\
\mathbf{e}_R
\end{bmatrix}
\in \mathbb{R}^6,
\label{eq:cartesian_error_impedance}
\end{equation}

where \(\mathbf{e}_p\) is the translational error and \(\mathbf{e}_R\) is the rotational error.
These two error components are used to compute the Cartesian impedance forces
and moments.

\subsection{Impedance Wrench}

The impedance command is evaluated in the robot base frame. Therefore the
matrices that appear in the commanded wrench are the base-frame stiffness and
damping matrices
\(K_{p,0}\), \(D_{p,0}\), \(K_{R,0}\), and \(D_{R,0}\). They are not assumed to
be diagonal in the base frame. If the task frame changes, for example because
the surface normal changes, these base-frame matrices change as well. As a
result, the commanded Cartesian force, moment, and joint torque also change
even if the local diagonal gain values are kept the same.

The positional impedance force is defined as the translational part of a
Cartesian spring-damper law \cite{Hogan1985,Mayr2024CartesianImpedance},

\begin{equation}
\mathbf{f}
=
K_{p,0} \mathbf{e}_p
+
D_{p,0}\left(\dot{p}_d-\dot{\mathbf{p}}_{\mathrm{EE}}\right)
\in \mathbb{R}^3.
\label{eq:positional_impedance_force}
\end{equation}

Here, \(K_{p,0} \in \mathbb{R}^{3 \times 3}\) is the positional stiffness
matrix expressed in the base frame, \(D_{p,0} \in \mathbb{R}^{3 \times 3}\) is
the positional damping matrix expressed in the base frame, and
\(\dot{\mathbf{p}}_{\mathrm{EE}}\in\mathbb{R}^3\) and
\(\dot{p}_d\in\mathbb{R}^3\) are the measured and desired Cartesian linear
velocities expressed in the same frame.
The stiffness term \(K_{p,0} \mathbf{e}_p\) generates a restoring force
proportional to the position error, while the damping term
\(D_{p,0}(\dot{p}_d-\dot{\mathbf{p}}_{\mathrm{EE}})\) damps the translational
velocity error. For a fixed setpoint, \(\dot{p}_d=\mathbf{0}\), so this term
reduces to \(-D_{p,0}\dot{\mathbf{p}}_{\mathrm{EE}}\).

The rotational impedance moment is defined as

\begin{equation}
\mathbf{m}
=
K_{R,0} \mathbf{e}_R
-
D_{R,0} \omega_{\mathrm{EE}}
\in \mathbb{R}^3.
\label{eq:rotational_impedance_moment}
\end{equation}

Here, \(K_{R,0} \in \mathbb{R}^{3 \times 3}\) is the rotational stiffness
matrix expressed in the base frame, \(D_{R,0} \in \mathbb{R}^{3 \times 3}\) is
the rotational damping matrix expressed in the base frame, and
\(\omega_{\mathrm{EE}}\in\mathbb{R}^3\) is the angular velocity of the
end-effector expressed in the base frame. The stiffness term
\(K_{R,0}\mathbf{e}_R\) generates a restoring moment proportional to the
orientation error, while the damping term \(D_{R,0}\omega_{\mathrm{EE}}\)
opposes the angular velocity of the end-effector.

The positional force and rotational moment are combined into the Cartesian
wrench. In this thesis, a wrench means a six-dimensional vector containing a
Cartesian force and a Cartesian moment:

\begin{equation}
\mathbf{F}
=
\begin{bmatrix}
\mathbf{f}\\
\mathbf{m}
\end{bmatrix}
=
\begin{bmatrix}
K_{p,0} \mathbf{e}_p + D_{p,0}\left(\dot{p}_d-\dot{\mathbf{p}}_{\mathrm{EE}}\right)\\
K_{R,0} \mathbf{e}_R - D_{R,0} \omega_{\mathrm{EE}}
\end{bmatrix}
\in \mathbb{R}^6.
\label{eq:cartesian_force_moment_vector}
\end{equation}

The vector \(\mathbf{F}\) is therefore the Cartesian wrench generated by the
impedance controller in response to position, orientation, linear velocity,
and angular velocity errors.
This wrench is later mapped to joint torques using the transpose of the
Jacobian.


\subsection{Joint-Torque Control Law}
\label{sec:control_law}
%% ─────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────

After defining the Cartesian impedance wrench, the final step is to convert it
into joint torques that can be commanded to the robot actuators.
This mapping is performed using the transpose of the Jacobian
\cite{Khatib1987}.

The Cartesian wrench is given by

\begin{equation}
\mathbf{F} =
\begin{bmatrix}
\mathbf{f}\\
\mathbf{m}
\end{bmatrix}
\in \mathbb{R}^6,
\label{eq:cartesian_force_moment_control_law}
\end{equation}

where \(\mathbf{f} \in \mathbb{R}^3\) is the Cartesian force and
\(\mathbf{m} \in \mathbb{R}^3\) is the Cartesian moment.
Using the principle of virtual work, this wrench is mapped to joint torques
\cite{Khatib1987} by

\begin{equation}
\boldsymbol{\tau}
=
J^\top(q)\mathbf{F}.
\label{eq:joint_torque_mapping}
\end{equation}

Here, \(\boldsymbol{\tau} \in \mathbb{R}^n\) is the joint torque vector,

\begin{equation}
\boldsymbol{\tau} =
\begin{bmatrix}
\tau_1 & \tau_2 & \cdots & \tau_n
\end{bmatrix}^\top,
\label{eq:joint_torque_vector}
\end{equation}

where each \(\tau_i\) is measured in \(\mathrm{Nm}\).
The matrix \(J^\top(q) \in \mathbb{R}^{n \times 6}\) is the transpose of the
Jacobian and maps the Cartesian wrench into joint torques.

Substituting the Cartesian impedance forces and moments gives the complete
Cartesian impedance control law:

\begin{equation}
\boldsymbol{\tau}
=
J^\top(q)
\begin{bmatrix}
K_{p,0} \mathbf{e}_p + D_{p,0}\left(\dot{p}_d-\dot{\mathbf{p}}_{\mathrm{EE}}\right)\\
K_{R,0} \mathbf{e}_R - D_{R,0} \omega_{\mathrm{EE}}
\end{bmatrix}.
\label{eq:cartesian_impedance_control_law}
\end{equation}

This equation is the computational core of the controller.
The matrices \(K_{p,0}\), \(D_{p,0}\), \(K_{R,0}\), and \(D_{R,0}\) are the
already transformed base-frame matrices. Therefore, for a surface task, changing
the surface frame changes these matrices before the wrench is computed, and the
joint torque command changes through the same equation.
At each control cycle, the current end-effector transformation is read from
the robot state and compared with the desired transformation.
From this comparison, the positional error \(\mathbf{e}_p\) and rotational error
\(\mathbf{e}_R\) are computed.
The corresponding Cartesian forces and moments are then evaluated and mapped
to joint torques using the Jacobian transpose.

\subsection{Coriolis and Gravity Compensation in libfranka}

In a general robot-dynamics formulation, the impedance torques are combined with
two model-based compensation terms: a gravity term and a Coriolis/centrifugal
term.
The commanded joint torque can therefore be written as

\begin{equation}
\tau_{\mathrm{cmd}}
=
J^\top(q)\mathbf{F}
+
\tau_g(q)
+
\tau_c(q,\dot{q}).
\label{eq:commanded_torque}
\end{equation}

Here, \(\tau_g(q) \in \mathbb{R}^n\) is the gravity compensation torque, which
depends on the joint configuration \(q\), and \(\tau_c(q,\dot{q}) \in
\mathbb{R}^n\) compensates for Coriolis and centrifugal effects, which depend on
both the joint configuration \(q\) and the joint velocity \(\dot{q}\).
This equation represents the general model-compensated impedance-control
structure; the two terms are, however, handled differently in the libfranka
implementation used in this work.

\paragraph{Coriolis term: computed and added explicitly.}
The Coriolis and centrifugal vector \(\tau_c(q,\dot{q})\) is not compensated by
the robot internally, so it is read from the libfranka model interface and added
explicitly to the commanded torque in the control callback. Including it cancels
the velocity-dependent coupling of the manipulator dynamics, so that the closed
loop behaves as the intended Cartesian impedance rather than being distorted by
the arm's own inertial coupling during motion.

\paragraph{Gravity term: already handled inside libfranka.}
The gravity torque \(\tau_g(q)\) is not added as a user-level term, because it is
already compensated internally by libfranka. In the external joint-torque
control mode of the Franka Control Interface, the robot runs its own rigid-body
model and continuously compensates gravity (and motor friction) on board; the
torque commanded through libfranka is added on top of this internal gravity
compensation, so the command is interpreted as the torque excluding gravity
\cite{FrankaFCI2023}.

Consequently, the implementation returns
\begin{equation}
\tau_{\mathrm{cmd,impl}}
=
J^\top(q)\mathbf{F}
+
\tau_c(q,\dot{q}),
\label{eq:commanded_torque_libfranka}
\end{equation}
to which the secondary null-space term is added once it has been introduced,
giving the complete command of
\cref{eq:nullspace_complete_torque_command}. Thus, of the two compensation terms
only the Coriolis term \(\tau_c(q,\dot{q})\) appears explicitly in the user
callback. Gravity is not neglected physically; the gravity torque \(\tau_g(q)\)
is already taken into account one level below the user equation, so adding it
again would compensate the same effect twice. This keeps the implementation
consistent with the official libfranka Cartesian impedance
example~\cite{libfrankaCartesianExample}.

\section{Surface Task Frame}
\label{subsec:surface_task_frame}

The contact experiment uses a task frame tied to the surface geometry. This
frame is defined before the stiffness and damping matrices are introduced, so
that the local gain entries can later be interpreted as normal and tangential
values instead of as abstract base-frame directions.

Let \(n_s\in\mathbb{R}^3\) with \(\|n_s\|=1\) be the unit surface normal,
expressed in the robot base frame. To complete the frame, choose an auxiliary
direction \(a_s\in\mathbb{R}^3\) that is not parallel to \(n_s\). This auxiliary
direction is only used to define a repeatable tangent direction on the surface.
The first tangent vector is obtained by projecting \(a_s\) onto the plane
orthogonal to \(n_s\), and the second tangent vector is then obtained by a cross
product. With \(\tilde{t}_1\) denoting the unnormalised projected tangent, the
construction is a Gram--Schmidt orthonormalization step applied to the surface
normal and the auxiliary direction \cite{Siciliano2009,Craig2005}:

\begin{equation}
\begin{aligned}
\tilde{t}_1
&=
a_s - n_s\left(n_s^\top a_s\right),\\
t_1
&=
\frac{\tilde{t}_1}{\|\tilde{t}_1\|},\\
t_2
&=
n_s\times t_1 .
\end{aligned}
\label{eq:surface_frame_tangents}
\end{equation}

Here, \(t_1\) and \(t_2\) are unit tangent vectors lying in the surface plane.
The construction is valid as long as \(\|\tilde{t}_1\|\neq0\), which means that
the auxiliary direction \(a_s\) must not be parallel to the normal \(n_s\). To
match the implemented controller and its parameter order, the surface task
frame collects the two tangents first and the normal last,
\begin{equation}
R_{\mathrm{surface}}
=
\begin{bmatrix}
t_1 & t_2 & n_s
\end{bmatrix}.
\label{eq:surface_task_frame_matrix}
\end{equation}

The column order is important: every surface-frame three-vector in the
implementation uses
\([\,\text{tangent 1},\text{tangent 2},\text{normal}\,]^\top\).
In the gain representation below, the contact task uses this frame by setting
\(R_{\mathrm{task}}=R_{\mathrm{surface}}\). This makes stiffness values such as
\(K_{t_1}\), \(K_{t_2}\), and \(K_n\) local surface-frame quantities in that
same order.

\section{Stiffness and Damping Representation}
\label{subsec:KD_matrices}

The positional stiffness matrix \(K_p\) and damping matrix \(D_p\) do not have
to be diagonal in the robot base frame \(\{0\}\).
Here, the subscript \(p\) refers to the positional part of the impedance
controller.
A diagonal matrix is obtained only when the coordinate axes of the chosen frame
are aligned with the principal stiffness directions.
For this reason, the positional stiffness and damping matrices are first
defined in a chosen task frame, where the principal directions are easier to
specify, and are then transformed into the base frame \(\{0\}\).
Let \(R_{\mathrm{task}}\in SO(3)\) be the orientation of this chosen task frame
with respect to the base frame. Its columns are the task-frame axes expressed
in the base frame. If the local task-frame matrices are
\(K_{p,\mathrm{task}}\), \(D_{p,\mathrm{task}}\),
\(K_{R,\mathrm{task}}\), and \(D_{R,\mathrm{task}}\), then the matrices used in
the command of \cref{eq:cartesian_force_moment_vector} are

\begin{equation}
\begin{aligned}
K_{p,0}
&=
R_{\mathrm{task}}K_{p,\mathrm{task}}R_{\mathrm{task}}^\top,
&
D_{p,0}
&=
R_{\mathrm{task}}D_{p,\mathrm{task}}R_{\mathrm{task}}^\top,\\
K_{R,0}
&=
R_{\mathrm{task}}K_{R,\mathrm{task}}R_{\mathrm{task}}^\top,
&
D_{R,0}
&=
R_{\mathrm{task}}D_{R,\mathrm{task}}R_{\mathrm{task}}^\top .
\end{aligned}
\label{eq:general_task_frame_gain_transform}
\end{equation}

The same notation can be written for the full six-dimensional stiffness and
damping matrices. For an uncoupled translational--rotational impedance in the
chosen task frame,

\begin{equation}
K_{\mathrm{task}}
=
\begin{bmatrix}
K_{p,\mathrm{task}} & 0\\
0 & K_{R,\mathrm{task}}
\end{bmatrix},
\qquad
D_{\mathrm{task}}
=
\begin{bmatrix}
D_{p,\mathrm{task}} & 0\\
0 & D_{R,\mathrm{task}}
\end{bmatrix}.
\label{eq:full_task_frame_gain_blocks}
\end{equation}

With
\(T_R=\operatorname{diag}(R_{\mathrm{task}},R_{\mathrm{task}})\), the
corresponding full base-frame matrices are

\begin{equation}
K_0=T_R K_{\mathrm{task}}T_R^\top,
\qquad
D_0=T_R D_{\mathrm{task}}T_R^\top .
\label{eq:full_task_frame_gain_transform}
\end{equation}

This is the important point for surface-dependent control: the controller does
not command the diagonal local matrices directly. It commands the base-frame
matrices after this transformation. Therefore, changing the surface orientation
changes \(R_{\mathrm{task}}\), which changes \(K_{p,0}\), \(D_{p,0}\),
\(K_{R,0}\), and \(D_{R,0}\), and consequently changes the Cartesian wrench and
the final joint torque command.

For the contact task, the task frame is the surface task frame introduced in
Section~\ref{subsec:surface_task_frame}. Setting
\(R_{\mathrm{task}}=R_{\mathrm{surface}}\) gives, for example,

\begin{equation}
K_{p,0}
=
R_{\mathrm{surface}}\,
\operatorname{diag}\!\left(K_{t_1},K_{t_2},K_n\right)\,
R_{\mathrm{surface}}^\top,
\label{eq:spatial_gain_matrix}
\end{equation}

and analogously for \(D_{p,0}\), \(K_{R,0}\), and \(D_{R,0}\). In particular,
the rotational diagonal follows the same column order,
\(\operatorname{diag}(K_{r,t_1},K_{r,t_2},K_{r,n})\), as do the translational
and rotational damping triples. In the implementation this operation is the
function \texttt{makeSpatialGainMatrix}.
Thus, choosing the directions through \(n_s\), \(t_1\), and \(t_2\) instead of
fixed base-frame axes lets the same impedance law act on any planar surface
orientation. If the surface normal changes, \(R_{\mathrm{surface}}\) changes,
so the base-frame matrices in the command change even though the local diagonal
entries \(K_{t_1}\), \(K_{t_2}\), and \(K_n\) may remain numerically identical.
This is the generic surface-frame construction and is used for both gain blocks
during approach. In the current set-up and grind implementation, only the
rotational block uses this construction; translational stiffness and damping
remain diagonal in base \([x,y,z]\), as detailed in
Chapter~\ref{ch:implementation}. The controller does not rotate active gains
with the live end-effector orientation \(R_{\mathrm{EE}}\).

\section{Center of Compliance and Virtual Pole}
\label{sec:coupled_pole_stiffness}

The task-frame construction of \cref{eq:full_task_frame_gain_transform} rotates
the principal stiffness directions but keeps the centre of compliance at the
end-effector origin, here identified with the tool centre point (TCP). A second
and independent design choice is \emph{where} the virtual spring is centred.
Shifting the centre of compliance from the TCP to another point, called here
the \emph{virtual pole} \(p_c\), turns a block-diagonal impedance at that pole
into a coupled impedance at the TCP
\cite{Murray1994,Fasse1997SpatialImpedance,Stramigioli2000IPC,LynchPark2017}.
This is the construction used by the controller during set-up: the
pole-coupled impedance biases alignment while contact constrains the motion.

The implemented pole-to-TCP lever, expressed in the base frame, is
\begin{equation}
r_c = p_{\mathrm{TCP}}-p_c \in \R{3},
\qquad p_{\mathrm{TCP}}=p_{\mathrm{EE}} .
\label{eq:pole_to_tcp_lever}
\end{equation}

Let \([r_c]_\times\) denote the skew-symmetric matrix satisfying
\([r_c]_\times a=r_c\times a\). Under the small-angle approximation, the
position error at the virtual pole is
\begin{equation}
\mathbf e_{p,c}
=
\mathbf e_p+[r_c]_\times\mathbf e_R .
\label{eq:pole_small_motion_error}
\end{equation}
The positive sign follows from the chosen pole-to-TCP lever: the vector from
the TCP to the pole is \(-r_c\), and
\(\mathbf e_R\times(-r_c)=[r_c]_\times\mathbf e_R\). Stacking the translational
and rotational errors therefore gives

\begin{equation}
\mathbf e_c
=
\begin{bmatrix}\mathbf e_{p,c}\\[2pt]\mathbf e_R\end{bmatrix}
=
\mathrm{Ad}(r_c)
\begin{bmatrix}\mathbf e_p\\[2pt]\mathbf e_R\end{bmatrix},
\qquad
\mathrm{Ad}(r_c)
=
\begin{bmatrix}
\mI_3 & [r_c]_\times\\[2pt]
0 & \mI_3
\end{bmatrix}.
\label{eq:offset_adjoint}
\end{equation}

The matrix \(\mathrm{Ad}(r_c)\) is the offset (point-shift) adjoint. It is the
translational counterpart of the block-diagonal rotation
\(T_R=\operatorname{diag}(R_{\mathrm{task}},R_{\mathrm{task}})\) of
\cref{eq:full_task_frame_gain_transform}: \(T_R\) rotates the principal axes,
whereas \(\mathrm{Ad}(r_c)\) moves the reference point. Let the decoupled gains
defined at the pole be
\begin{equation}
K_{\mathrm{pole}}
=
\begin{bmatrix}K_p&0\\0&K_R\end{bmatrix},
\qquad
D_{\mathrm{pole}}
=
\begin{bmatrix}D_p&0\\0&D_R\end{bmatrix}.
\label{eq:pole_block_gains}
\end{equation}
The pole-frame wrench is
\(w_{\mathrm{pole}}=K_{\mathrm{pole}}\mathbf e_c\). Transforming this
power-conjugate wrench to the TCP gives
\(w_{\mathrm{TCP}}=\mathrm{Ad}(r_c)^\top w_{\mathrm{pole}}\), and therefore
the stiffness expressed at the TCP is

\begin{equation}
K_{\mathrm{TCP}}
=
\mathrm{Ad}(r_c)^\top K_{\mathrm{pole}}\,\mathrm{Ad}(r_c)
=
\begin{bmatrix}
K_p & K_p[r_c]_\times\\[4pt]
[r_c]_\times^\top K_p & K_R + [r_c]_\times^\top K_p\,[r_c]_\times
\end{bmatrix},
\label{eq:coupled_tcp_stiffness}
\end{equation}

The same point shift must be applied to damping:
\begin{equation}
D_{\mathrm{TCP}}
=
\mathrm{Ad}(r_c)^\top D_{\mathrm{pole}}\,\mathrm{Ad}(r_c)
=
\begin{bmatrix}
D_p & D_p[r_c]_\times\\[4pt]
[r_c]_\times^\top D_p &
D_R+[r_c]_\times^\top D_p[r_c]_\times
\end{bmatrix}.
\label{eq:coupled_tcp_damping}
\end{equation}
The off-diagonal blocks \(K_p[r_c]_\times\) and \([r_c]_\times^\top K_p\) couple a
translational error into a restoring moment and a rotational error into a
restoring force. They are exactly the structured translation--rotation coupling
that the decoupled command of \cref{eq:cartesian_force_moment_vector} does not
contain. Physically, a pure infinitesimal rotation about the virtual pole has
zero translational error at that pole but produces the corresponding
lever-induced translation at the TCP. The coupled TCP wrench is the wrench
representation of that same virtual spring.

The sign of the translation-to-moment block can be checked without a rotational
error. For \(e_R=0\), let \(f=K_p e_p\). The elastic moment generated at the TCP
is then
\begin{equation}
m_c=[r_c]_\times^\top f=-\,r_c\times f .
\label{eq:pole_translation_moment_sign_check}
\end{equation}
Changing the direction of \(r_c\) therefore reverses this coupling moment. This
relation is also the direct sign test for a proposed pole and press direction.

Two properties follow directly from \cref{eq:coupled_tcp_stiffness}. First, the
coupling vanishes when \(r_c=0\). Conversely, if \(K_p\) is nonsingular, zero
coupling implies \(r_c=0\). Second, because \(\mathrm{Ad}(r_c)\) is invertible,
the congruence transformation preserves definiteness:
\begin{equation}
K_{\mathrm{pole}}\succeq0
\ \Longrightarrow\
K_{\mathrm{TCP}}\succeq0,
\qquad
K_{\mathrm{pole}}\succ0
\ \Longrightarrow\
K_{\mathrm{TCP}}\succ0,
\label{eq:pole_gain_definiteness}
\end{equation}
and the same statements hold for \(D_{\mathrm{pole}}\) and
\(D_{\mathrm{TCP}}\). Individual negative off-diagonal entries are therefore
coupling terms, not negative stiffness directions.

This construction is a local, small-motion model with \(r_c\) held fixed while
the wrench is evaluated. In the implemented frozen-pole mode, the selected
lever is kept constant, so the mapped gain matrices remain constant during the
motion segment. If the pole or lever is updated continuously, the gain matrices
become configuration dependent. Accordingly, \(p_c\) is a
\emph{local linearized centre of compliance}, not a guaranteed measured pivot.
It need not coincide with the physical contact point \(p_C\); finite rotational
stiffness, the frozen lever, the environment constraint and friction, and the
active sigma term introduced below all influence the observed motion.

\section{Complete Joint-Torque Command}
\label{sec:nullspace_svd}

The Franka Emika Panda has seven joints, while the Cartesian motion of the
end-effector is described by six degrees of freedom: three translational and
three rotational components.
The robot is therefore kinematically redundant.
This redundancy can be used to pursue a secondary objective that is
kinematically invisible to the primary Cartesian task to first order.

The relationship between the joint velocities and the Cartesian end-effector
velocity is described by the geometric Jacobian
\begin{equation}
\dot{x}
=
J(q)\dot{q},
\qquad
J(q)\in\mathbb{R}^{6\times7},
\label{eq:nullspace_jacobian_relation}
\end{equation}
where
\begin{equation}
\dot{x}
=
\begin{bmatrix}
\dot{x} & \dot{y} & \dot{z} &
\omega_x & \omega_y & \omega_z
\end{bmatrix}^{\top}
\in \mathbb{R}^{6},
\qquad
\dot{q}
=
\begin{bmatrix}
\dot{q}_1 & \cdots & \dot{q}_7
\end{bmatrix}^{\top}
\in \mathbb{R}^{7}.
\label{eq:nullspace_velocities}
\end{equation}

Since the joint space has seven dimensions while the Cartesian task has six,
a one-dimensional null space exists whenever the Jacobian has full rank.
A joint velocity in this null space satisfies
\begin{equation}
J(q)\dot{q}_0 = 0.
\label{eq:jacobian_nullspace_condition}
\end{equation}
The velocity \(\dot{q}_0\) therefore produces no first-order Cartesian
end-effector velocity and can be used for a secondary joint-space task at the
kinematic level.

In this work, the full six-dimensional geometric Jacobian is used for the
null-space construction because the controller regulates the complete
end-effector pose. The implementation forms a truncated Moore--Penrose
pseudoinverse from the SVD; it does not use a damped normal-equation inverse.
The full SVD is
\cite{Chiaverini1994DampedLeastSquares,Dietrich2015NullspaceSurvey}
\begin{equation}
J
=
U_J\Sigma_J V_J^{\top},
\qquad
U_J\in\mathbb{R}^{6\times6},\quad
\Sigma_J\in\mathbb{R}^{6\times7},\quad
V_J\in\mathbb{R}^{7\times7},
\label{eq:jacobian_svd}
\end{equation}
with the six reported singular values ordered as
\begin{equation}
\sigma_1\geq\sigma_2\geq\cdots\geq\sigma_6\geq0,
\qquad
Jv_i=\sigma_i u_i,\quad i=1,\ldots,6 .
\label{eq:svd_direction_mapping}
\end{equation}
The seventh column of the full right-singular-vector matrix is unpaired with a
reported singular value and satisfies
\begin{equation}
Jv_7=0 .
\label{eq:nullspace_search_direction}
\end{equation}
Thus \(v_6\), not \(v_7\), is associated with the smallest reported singular
value
\begin{equation}
\sigma_{\min}=\sigma_6 .
\label{eq:min_singular_value}
\end{equation}
For a full-row-rank \(6\times7\) Jacobian, \(v_7\) spans the single structural
null direction. The vectors \(v_6\) and \(v_7\) therefore have distinct roles:
\(v_6\) identifies the weakest task-producing direction, whereas \(v_7\) is
the redundant joint-motion direction.

The equality
\(\sigma_i=\sqrt{\lambda_i(JJ^\top)}\) explains why a decreasing
\(\sigma_6\) indicates approach to a row-rank singularity. However, the
geometric Jacobian stacks linear and angular velocity rows with different
physical units. Consequently, its numerical singular values, their cutoff,
and comparisons of their magnitudes depend on the chosen coordinate scaling
or characteristic length. Here \(\sigma_{\min}\) is used as an
implementation-specific conditioning indicator, not as a unit-invariant
manipulability measure.

Let \(\rho\geq0\) be the configured relative SVD tolerance and define
\begin{equation}
\varepsilon_\rho=\rho\sigma_1,
\qquad
\mathcal R_\rho=\{\,i\in\{1,\ldots,6\}\mid
\sigma_i>\varepsilon_\rho\,\}.
\label{eq:svd_relative_cutoff}
\end{equation}
Only the retained singular directions are inverted. The exact formula used by
the controller is
\begin{equation}
J_\rho^+
=
\sum_{i\in\mathcal R_\rho}
\frac{1}{\sigma_i}\,v_i u_i^\top
\in\mathbb{R}^{7\times6}.
\label{eq:truncated_svd_pseudoinverse}
\end{equation}
The reciprocal is \(1/\sigma_i\), and directions at or below the cutoff are
omitted rather than regularized. The corresponding joint-velocity and torque
projectors are
\begin{equation}
N_q=I_7-J_\rho^+J,
\qquad
N_\tau=N_q^\top
=I_7-J^\top J_\rho^{+\top}
\in\mathbb{R}^{7\times7}.
\label{eq:nullspace_velocity_torque_projectors}
\end{equation}
For this truncated SVD, \(J_\rho^+J\) is an orthogonal projector onto the
retained right-singular subspace. Hence \(N_q=N_\tau\) analytically; the
implementation symmetrizes \(N_\tau\) only to remove floating-point roundoff.
If all six directions are retained and \(J\) has full row rank, then
\(N_q=N_\tau=v_7v_7^\top\) and the null relation is exact. If directions are
truncated, they are deliberately admitted to the numerical null space and
\begin{equation}
\lVert JN_q\rVert_2\leq\varepsilon_\rho ,
\label{eq:nullspace_projected_vector_task_effect}
\end{equation}
up to floating-point error.

This projector is Euclidean and kinematic, not dynamically consistent. In
particular, \(JN_q=0\) does not in general imply
\(JM_q^{-1}N_\tau\tau=0\). A projected torque can therefore couple into
Cartesian acceleration through the inertia matrix even when it is orthogonal
to the retained task subspace. Claims of exact dynamic task decoupling would
require an inertia-weighted projector
\cite{Khatib1987,Dietrich2015NullspaceSurvey}.

The optional smallest-singular-value conditioning bias probes the unit
structural null direction
\(\hat v_7=v_7/\lVert v_7\rVert\). It is a two-point sign-selection heuristic,
not a continuous solver for the maximum of \(\sigma_{\min}(q)\). Two
configurations are sampled,
\begin{equation}
q_+=q+\alpha_{\mathrm{probe}}\hat v_7,
\qquad
q_-=q-\alpha_{\mathrm{probe}}\hat v_7,
\label{eq:nullspace_candidates}
\end{equation}
and the Jacobian and \(\sigma_6\) are recomputed at each sample. With
\(\sigma_+=\sigma_{\min}(J(q_+))\),
\(\sigma_-=\sigma_{\min}(J(q_-))\), and
\(\Delta_\sigma=\sigma_+-\sigma_-\), the sign selector is
\begin{equation}
s
=
\begin{cases}
+1, & \Delta_\sigma>\delta_\sigma,\\
-1, & \Delta_\sigma<-\delta_\sigma,\\
0,  & |\Delta_\sigma|\leq\delta_\sigma .
\end{cases}
\label{eq:nullspace_direction_selection}
\end{equation}
The deadband \(\delta_\sigma\geq0\) suppresses the conditioning torque when the
two probes are indistinguishable. The arbitrary SVD sign of \(v_7\) does not
change the result: reversing \(v_7\) swaps \(q_+\) and \(q_-\), reverses \(s\),
and leaves \(s\hat v_7\) unchanged. The angle \(\alpha_{\mathrm{probe}}\)
specifies only where \(\sigma_{\min}\) is sampled. It affects the sensitivity
of the comparison against the raw deadband, but it is not multiplied into the
commanded torque. Moreover, \(\hat v_7\) is only the instantaneous tangent to
the constant-pose self-motion at \(q\). For finite
\(\alpha_{\mathrm{probe}}\), neither \(q_+\) nor \(q_-\) is guaranteed to lie on
that nonlinear self-motion manifold. The controller does not test either probe
against joint limits. It uses only which sample is larger, not a gradient
magnitude, so the resulting constant-magnitude torque has no guarantee of
monotonic improvement in \(\sigma_{\min}\).

The two available torque contributions are
\begin{equation}
\tau_d=-d_{\mathrm{null}}N_\tau\dot q,
\qquad
\tau_\sigma=k_\sigma N_\tau(s\hat v_7),
\label{eq:nullspace_torque_components}
\end{equation}
where \(d_{\mathrm{null}}\) is the projected damping coefficient and
\(k_\sigma\) is the commanded torque magnitude. The implemented law has no
restoring spring to \(q_{\mathrm{start}}\). Its four selectable modes
are therefore
\begin{equation}
\tau_{\mathrm{null}}
=
\begin{cases}
0, & \text{off},\\
\tau_d, & \text{damping only},\\
\tau_\sigma, & \text{sigma only},\\
\tau_d+\tau_\sigma, & \text{damping and sigma}.
\end{cases}
\label{eq:nullspace_torque}
\end{equation}
The result is a seven-dimensional joint torque,
\begin{equation}
\tau_{\mathrm{null}}\in\mathbb{R}^{7}.
\label{eq:nullspace_torque_dimension}
\end{equation}
For \(d_{\mathrm{null}}\geq0\), the first term opposes projected joint motion.
The sign-selected \(\tau_\sigma\), by contrast, is an active, switched,
constant-magnitude bias, and sigma-only mode contains no null-space damping.
If the Jacobian loses row rank, the true null space has
dimension greater than one; the implementation still probes only the single
full-SVD vector \(v_7\), so it does not search over every newly available null
direction.

The complete torque command of the controller is therefore
\begin{equation}
\tau_{\mathrm{cmd}}
=
J^{\top}(q)\,\mathbf{F}_{\mathrm{cmd}}
+
\tau_{\mathrm{null}}
+
\tau_c,
\label{eq:nullspace_complete_torque_command}
\end{equation}
where \(J^{\top}\mathbf{F}_{\mathrm{cmd}}\) is the Cartesian impedance torque,
\(\tau_{\mathrm{null}}\) is the null-space term, and \(\tau_c\) is the Coriolis
compensation added in the user callback. The gravity compensation is handled by
the Franka Control Interface for the external torque command, as explained
around Equation~\eqref{eq:commanded_torque_libfranka}.
